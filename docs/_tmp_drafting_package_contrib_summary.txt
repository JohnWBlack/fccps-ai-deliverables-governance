## Preface
- 0.1 Advisory Framing and Use of This Document
- This report is intended to provide structured advisory input to the Falls Church City Public Schools School Board as it considers policy direction for artificial intelligence in FCCPS. It identifies areas of committee alignment, key policy tradeoffs, and areas of meaningful disagreement that the Board should be able to see clearly rather than infer indirectly.
- This document is not a final policy and should not be read as a fully negotiated committee consensus text. In some sections, the committee reached broad alignment on values, guardrails, or overall posture. In other sections, members advanced different policy approaches that reflect legitimate differences in judgment about learning, safety, equity, privacy, trust, implementation burden, and long-term student preparation. Where those differences remained meaningful, this report preserves them.
- To support readability, many WS-POL sections begin with a chair preface that summarizes common ground, characterizes the range of positions, and identifies the central tradeoff or unresolved question. Those prefatory paragraphs are followed by member contributions, which are presented as distinct committee viewpoints rather than merged into a single artificial compromise.

## WS-POL-A – Guiding Principles and Motivation – Chair Preface
- Areas of Broad Agreement on Values and Policy Goals
- The Committee’s discussion of guiding principles and motivation reflects substantial agreement on core values, even where members differ on policy posture. Across meetings and draft materials, members repeatedly emphasized that any FCCPS AI policy should be evidence-informed, aligned with student learning and FCCPS educational goals, tailored to developmental stages, human-centered in implementation, transparent to students, families, and staff, attentive to equity, and adaptable as technology changes. The Committee also broadly agreed that policy must protect student learning, assessment integrity, fairness, trust, privacy, and student well-being under conditions of uncertainty, while avoiding both unchecked adoption and impractical overrestriction.
- Main Differences in Overall Policy Posture
- Within that shared foundation, members expressed differing views about default posture. One position places greater weight on restrictions, especially where AI may weaken learning, distort assessment, or create fairness risks that schools cannot reliably manage. Another position places greater weight on thoughtful, well-governed integration, arguing that students and educators need structured opportunities to build AI literacy and applied competence in a society increasingly shaped by AI. Members also differed on how much policy should rely on restriction, design choices, proctoring, disclosure, human review, or pilot-based governance to manage risk. These differences are meaningful and should be made visible to the Board rather than harmonized away.

## WS-POL-A – Guiding Principles and Motivation – John Black
- FCCPS should adopt an AI policy that is protective without being passive. Current evidence does not support unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. Recent research raises credible concerns that AI can reduce independent effort, encourage passivity, and weaken durable learning when used inappropriately or too early (Bastani et al., 2024; Burns et al., 2026; Kosmyna et al., 2025). Those concerns should be taken seriously.
- At the same time, FCCPS cannot responsibly assume that avoidance alone is an adequate educational strategy. AI tools are already widely available outside school, and the relevant divide may increasingly be between those who develop fluency and judgment in using AI and those who do not (VandeHei & Allen, 2026). If that divide continues to widen, then a policy centered only on restriction may unintentionally deepen inequity by leaving AI literacy and practical fluency to families with greater access, time, and outside support. For that reason, FCCPS should regulate AI from a design-first posture: begin with learning goals, developmental needs, human-accountability requirements, and equity concerns; design tasks, workflows, and safeguards accordingly; and then impose targeted restrictions where those goals cannot otherwise be protected.
- Virtues We Are Trying to Capture
- FCCPS policy should be evidence-informed, but built for uncertainty. The district should be guided by evidence where evidence exists and proceed cautiously where it does not. The district should avoid both technological enthusiasm untethered from outcomes and a standard of proof so absolute that it prevents bounded learning, pilot-based governance, and policy adaptation.

## WS-POL-B – Adoption vs. Restriction Posture – Chair Preface
- Shared Premises About Protection, Preparation, and Enforceability
- This section addresses one of the clearest policy tradeoffs identified by the Committee: how FCCPS should balance protecting student learning and assessment integrity against enabling thoughtful, developmentally appropriate integration of AI to support student preparation and AI literacy. The Committee broadly agrees that policy must protect learning, fairness, trust, and student well-being, and that FCCPS should avoid both unchecked adoption and impractical overrestriction. The Committee also agrees that any policy in this area must account for the practical difficulty of enforcing AI restrictions in unproctored settings, the importance of preserving trust between students and teachers, and the risk of unfair outcomes, including false accusations and uneven compliance.
- Competing Default Postures for AI Use in FCCPS
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on default posture. One position places greater weight on restricting AI use unless clear educational benefit can be shown and risks are well controlled, emphasizing that current evidence of benefit is uneven while harms to learning and independent thinking are already visible. Another position places greater weight on guided, well-governed integration, emphasizing that AI is already widely accessible outside school, that restriction-first policies may be difficult to enforce consistently, and that FCCPS should prepare students to use AI thoughtfully, transparently, and responsibly. A middle view emphasizes that the Board should understand this as a genuine policy tradeoff rather than a disagreement over whether learning protection matters.

## WS-POL-B – Adoption vs. Restriction Posture – John Black
- Decision 1 — Committee perspective (no full consensus)
- The Committee is correct that this section presents a genuine policy tradeoff and not merely a disagreement over emphasis. I agree that FCCPS policy must protect student learning and assessment integrity, and I also agree that current evidence does not justify careless or unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. Recent research and policy guidance support caution: Brookings concluded that, at this stage, the risks of generative AI in children’s education outweigh its benefits, and Virginia’s statewide guidance stresses both “do no harm” and “augment, not replace humans.” At the same time, neither source supports a purely prohibition-based posture. Brookings also argues for holistic AI literacy and well-designed tools that teach rather than tell, while Virginia’s guidance explicitly calls for innovation, experimentation, and equitable access alongside guardrails. In my view, those sources point toward a design-first posture, not a restriction-first default. FCCPS should begin with learning goals, developmental needs, and human-accountability requirements, then decide where AI use should be permitted, structured, or restricted. (Brookings)
- A restriction-first posture is understandable, and I take its concerns seriously. The strongest case for it is that harms independent thinking, assessment validity, and student self-trust are already visible, while benefits remain uneven and context dependent. I share those concerns. But I do not believe they justify making broader adoption a secondary priority until benefits are proven in advance across all use cases. The more realistic and educationally responsible approach is to distinguish between contexts. Some uses should indeed be restricted or prohibited because they undermine core learning objectives. Other uses should be bounded, teacher-guided, and closely monitored. Still others should be explored through pilots and carefully governed classroom practice where they support AI literacy, responsible use, and student preparation for a rapidly changing environment. (Brookings)
- My position, therefore, is that FCCPS should adopt a design-first posture. That means protecting assessment validity primarily through assessment design, human review, process expectations, and instructional clarity, while using restrictions where those measures are insufficient. It also means recognizing that AI literacy includes learning when not to use AI. A design-first posture is not permissive by default. It is more demanding than either uncritical adoption or blanket prohibition, because it requires FCCPS to be explicit about educational purpose, developmental appropriateness, and accountability.

## WS-POL-C – Scope, Triaging, and Policy Crosswalk – Chair Preface
- Shared View of Policy Scope and Need for Triaging
- This section addresses how broadly FCCPS policy should define the AI problem it is trying to govern, how AI-related uses and risks should be triaged, and how new AI guidance should relate to existing FCCPS policy and regulation. The Committee broadly agrees that AI policy should be interpreted alongside existing FCCPS policies and should extend or clarify them where AI changes scale, ambiguity, evidentiary conditions, or implementation burden. The Committee also broadly agrees that some form of triaging is necessary, whether through risk categories, use-case distinctions, or supporting guidance, so that policy can remain both principled and practical.
- Differences Over Breadth, Placement, and Governance Structure
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on how broad the policy scope should be. One position favors a narrower focus on generative AI, detectors, academic integrity, and the most immediate classroom-facing issues. Another favors a broader scope that includes AI-enabled systems used in FCCPS contexts more generally, along with privacy, procurement, wellbeing, equity, and governance concerns that may not be reducible to GenAI alone. Members also differ on whether non-academic risks such as manipulative interaction, harassment, and quasi-social dependence should be addressed directly in Board-facing policy language or handled more fully through supporting guidance, implementation frameworks, or existing policies.

## WS-POL-C – Scope, Triaging, and Policy Crosswalk – John Black
- I do not think FCCPS should write an AI policy that is so narrow it governs only the most obvious current controversies, nor so broad that it becomes an unadministrable theory of all technology risk. The right approach is a broad but disciplined scope. FCCPS should write this policy to cover AI uses that materially affect student learning, assessment validity, student rights, student well-being, privacy, equity, or district governance. That means the policy should certainly address generative AI and detectors, because those are the most visible and immediate issues. But it should not stop there. It should also be capable of reaching AI-enabled systems, tools, and workflows used in FCCPS contexts when they change the practical risk environment enough that ordinary existing policy language is no longer sufficient. That is the key threshold: not whether a tool is branded “AI,” but whether AI changes scale, ambiguity, evidentiary conditions, or implementation burden in ways the Board should govern explicitly.
- In my view, a GenAI-only frame is too narrow for the policy itself, even if GenAI remains the center of gravity in communications and examples. The district will confront not only chatbots and generators, but also AI-enabled screening, translation, personalization, workflow, monitoring, and decision-support systems. Some of those tools will not present as “ChatGPT for school,” yet may have greater consequences for students and staff than classroom prompting alone. FCCPS should therefore write the policy so that it applies more broadly to AI-enabled systems used in FCCPS contexts, while using guidance and examples to keep the document concrete and understandable. This lets the Board avoid writing a policy that is obsolete the moment the product category shifts, while still acknowledging that generative AI is the current focal point of community concern.
- I also think this section should say plainly that academic integrity is not the only AI policy problem. Integrity matters, but it is not sufficient as the organizing scope of the policy. FCCPS should explicitly recognize non-academic AI risks where they materially affect the school environment, especially manipulative interaction, quasi-social dependence, harassment, synthetic media abuse, inappropriate content exposure, and contexts in which students may be nudged away from human support or judgment. I am not suggesting that this report attempt to solve every such issue in detail. I am saying the Board should be told that these risks belong within the district’s AI policy perimeter and should not be treated as accidental side effects outside the policy’s concern. If the committee omits them from Section C, the policy shell will understate the real problem FCCPS is trying to govern.
- At the same time, I would not write this section as an invitation to policy sprawl. The policy should not attempt to prescribe a separate rule for every conceivable AI-enabled feature or classroom edge case. Instead, FCCPS should adopt a triage structure. In my judgment, the district needs a practical way to sort AI uses by risk, consequence, and context. The policy should establish that not all AI uses are alike and that different uses warrant different levels of scrutiny, restriction, disclosure, or approval. A low-risk assistive use that does not touch student records or displace core learning work should not be governed in the same way as an identity-linked system, a detector, a student-facing conversational tool, or an AI-supported process affecting discipline, placement, accommodations, or other consequential decisions.

## WS-POL-D – Definitions – Chair Preface
- Shared Need for Clear, Operational Definitions
- This section addresses the foundational terms that make the rest of the policy intelligible and enforceable. The committee broadly agrees that FCCPS needs plain-language, operationally useful definitions for key terms such as AI, generative AI, AI detector, approved tool, high-stakes use, AI assistance, disclosure or attribution, human decision-maker, and vendor or data-processor terms. The committee also broadly agrees that these definitions are not merely editorial; they shape how later sections on student use, staff use, privacy, academic integrity, and accountability will actually function in practice.
- Differences Over How Much Policy Should Be Embedded in Definitions
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on how much policy content should be embedded in the definitions themselves. One position favors more neutral, descriptive definitions that clarify technical and operational terms while leaving most normative judgments to later sections. Another favors more interpretive definitions that draw sharper distinctions—for example, between citation and disclosure, constructive and evaluative feedback, or broad fluency and the “when not to use AI” dimension of AI literacy—and that use the definitions section to shape later policy decisions more explicitly. The clearest unresolved definition issue is “AI assistance,” where the committee considered material-contribution, tiered-functional, and inclusive-plus-exempt-list approaches because that term affects disclosure, restriction rules, classroom examples, tool approval, and integrity review.

## WS-POL-D – Definitions – David Berol
- Generative-AI
- See the definition provided by the Brookings Institute Report on AI in the schools: “Generative AI, the focus of this report, refers to a class of technologies that generate novel outputs such as text, images, audio, code, or video in response to user prompts. These technologies include LLMs, diffusion models, and other neural network architectures designed for content generation. Generative AI is a form of machine learning that creates new content rather than simply analyzing or classifying existing data. These systems are typically trained on vast datasets composed of publicly available digital content—words, images, and sounds . . . . Contemporary models increasingly incorporate curated, domain specific, and proprietary data tailored to specific applications, including educational ones.”1
- AI Literacy
- AI literacy comes from technical instruction about AI (e.g., training an AI model in computer science class) and from pragmatic instruction on the social, safety, and ethical issues involved in using AI.2   The core of AI literacy is understanding where AI use is and is not appropriate. Reasonable restrictions on AI use and encouraging AI literacy are complementary objectives. To the extent AI literacy is a call for students to use AI extensively in school, merely on the grounds that they will eventually need to use AI at work, note the following: When students graduate to the workforce, efficiency demands may very well oblige them to regularly use AI tools as a skill-multiplier. But school is fundamentally a place where students need to develop their innate skills, precisely so that they will have skills capable of being multiplied. It is essential to prioritize the development of students’ innate skills and school is a protected space for learning that need not always conform to the efficiency logic of the workplace.

## WS-POL-E – Staff and Teacher Use of AI – Chair Preface
- Common Ground on Human Judgment and Bounded Educator Use
- This section addresses how FCCPS should govern staff and teacher use of AI in planning, instruction, feedback, evaluation, integrity-related review, and other consequential school functions. The Committee broadly agrees that AI may play a bounded assistive role for educators, especially in planning, operational support, translation, and preparation of instructional materials, but that such use must augment rather than replace professional judgment, human accountability, or the educator’s responsibility for student-facing decisions. The Committee also broadly agrees that evaluative judgments, grading, discipline-related determinations, and other high-stakes decisions must remain human-led, and that policy should remain clear and communicable without collapsing into overly detailed implementation rules.
- Main Disagreements on Feedback, Evaluation, and Detector Use
- Within that shared foundation, members differ most sharply on teacher use of AI in feedback and evaluation. One position holds that evaluative feedback should be the teacher’s authentic work product and that even AI-assisted drafting in this area risks undermining trust, authenticity, and the student-teacher relationship unless subjected to unusually strong disclosure, consent, and review requirements; some members would prohibit such use altogether except under very narrow conditions. Another position permits limited assistive use, especially where AI helps with planning, accessibility, translation, or drafting support, so long as the educator meaningfully reviews, personalizes, and fully owns the final student-facing output. Members also differ on AI detectors: there is broad concern about reliability and fairness, but while some members would allow at most highly constrained advisory use with human review and due-process protections, others recommend a provisional or complete bar because detector use is seen as too likely to produce false accusations, confirmation bias, and unfair treatment.

## WS-POL-F – Student Use – PreK-5 – F Chair Preface
- Broad Agreement on Strong Caution for Younger Students
- This section reflects one of the Committee’s strongest areas of shared caution. Members broadly agree that AI use for younger students must be developmentally appropriate, that foundational cognitive, social, and relational learning should remain primary, and that routine direct student use in PreK–5 should be limited and educator-mediated. The Committee also broadly agrees that FCCPS should emphasize AI literacy in this band while maintaining stronger boundaries around conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating AI uses that may blur the distinction between human and machine interaction or encourage younger students to treat AI as a buddy, coach, co-teacher, or authority. Meeting 5 reinforced this general posture by emphasizing more restricted exposure for younger students, developmentally appropriate framing, and a policy-level recommendation structure rather than operational detail in the report itself.
- Differences Over Whether Limited Applied Use Should Be Permitted
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on how much room FCCPS should leave for school-sponsored applied uses of AI in PreK–5. One position favors a strongly protective posture in which GenAI use in this band is oriented primarily toward AI literacy, demonstration, and very limited supervised exposure, with no independent student use and little or no routine applied use in traditional subject learning. Another position remains highly protective but preserves narrow room for carefully bounded, educator-directed uses when they are developmentally appropriate, tied to a clear instructional purpose, subject to close adult supervision and approved-tool governance, and do not displace foundational skill-building or invite anthropomorphic dependence. These positions do not mainly reflect disagreement about whether younger children need protection; they reflect different judgments about whether limited, highly governed applied use is ever educationally justified in the elementary years.

## WS-POL-F – Student Use – PreK-5 – David Berol
- Younger children differ markedly from adults in their capacity to interpret technology tools that appear to be engaging with them socially (e.g., chatting or talking to them)1 They are prone to “plac[ing] too much trust in an AI partner” and struggle to “distinguish between simulated abilities and genuine human abilities.”2 A recent review by pediatricians at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia concluded that children under 6 “have a limited ability to distinguish between AI and human interaction.” Children in the 6-12 age band are characteristically prone to the following GenAI risks: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”3
- Considering these risks, school-sponsored use of Generative AI in grades pre-K through 5 should be primarily oriented towards teaching AI Literacy. That is, GenAI should primarily be used for demonstration purposes in instruction about Gen AI. It should be used minimally in contexts where the primary purpose is to provide instruction on traditional subjects such as math and language arts (i.e., “Applied Uses”). In all cases, GenAI use by students in this grade band should closely supervised by adults.
- When the school is considering implementing Applied Uses of GenAI tools in this grade band, it should consider in particular:
- Whether there is clear and convincing evidence of significant educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained.

## WS-POL-G – Student Use – Grades 6-12 – Chair Preface
- Shared Expectations for Gradual Expansion of Student AI Use
- This section reflects broad agreement that students in Grades 6–12 should encounter more structured opportunities to use AI than younger students, but under clearer, more differentiated expectations. The Committee generally agrees that use should become broader as age, task complexity, and student judgment increase; that learning goals remain primary; that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted; and that policy in this band should distinguish among categories of use, interaction boundaries, and disclosure expectations. The Committee also broadly agrees that integrity and fairness cannot be protected by detector-driven policing alone and that expectations for older students must be explicit because they will encounter AI both inside and outside school.
- Differences Over Restriction, Scaffolding, and Guided Integration
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on default posture and on how much AI should be allowed in ordinary academic work. One position favors stronger restrictions, especially in writing-heavy courses and higher-stakes contexts, treating AI mainly as temporary scaffolding and emphasizing in-class work, oral defense, disclosure, and a protected non-AI path. Another position favors guided, risk-tiered integration, emphasizing that AI access is already widespread and uneven, that restriction-first approaches may be difficult to enforce consistently, and that some uses—such as tutoring, formative practice, structured feedback, selected STEM and language-learning tasks, and computer science—may be educationally valuable when designed well. A smaller but real position is more innovation-forward still, placing greater weight on teacher judgment and on adopting AI where it appears to improve learning, even when the evidence base is incomplete.

## WS-POL-G – WS-POL-G Student Use – Grades 6-12 – John Black
- Students in Grades 6–12 should be prepared not only to avoid harmful or inappropriate uses of AI, but to understand and use AI well. For this grade band, I support a more permissive and innovation-forward posture than the one I would apply in PreK–5, while still preserving strong guardrails around learning goals, transparency, fairness, and human judgment. I agree with the Committee’s progression that older students should receive increased autonomy with clear expectations for disclosure and evaluation and that use should become broader as age, task complexity, and student judgment increase. I also agree that AI use in this band must support learning rather than replace it. Where I differ from the more restrictive position is that I do not think FCCPS should treat student AI use in Grades 6–12 primarily as a temporary exception to traditional schooling. I think FCCPS should treat it as an increasingly normal part of the environment students are entering and should therefore give students structured opportunities to build fluency, judgment, and responsibility under school-governed conditions.
- My starting point is that restriction alone is not a realistic or sufficient response for this age group. The Committee has already recognized that AI tools are widely accessible outside school and are increasingly part of students’ everyday academic and social environments. In Grades 6–12, that reality matters more, not less. Students in this band are closer to college, work, and civic settings in which AI tools will be part of ordinary problem-solving, research, drafting, coding, planning, and communication. A school policy that focuses primarily on limiting use, without also creating guided opportunities to practice effective and ethical use, risks leaving skill development to outside access, informal experimentation, and unequal support at home. FCCPS should instead make school the place where students learn how to use these tools critically and responsibly.
- I therefore support a guided-use posture for Grades 6–12. In this band, FCCPS should permit progressively broader categories of student AI use, but only with explicit instructional framing. Students should learn how AI works, where it is unreliable, how to challenge or verify it, when it is educationally useful, and when it is inappropriate. AI literacy at this level should not be defined only as caution or abstention. It should include practical fluency: knowing how to ask useful questions, compare outputs, test assumptions, identify hallucinations, preserve authorship, and disclose meaningful assistance honestly. Older students should graduate from FCCPS not only knowing that AI can be risky, but also knowing how to use it with discernment.
- That does not mean all uses should be treated alike. The Committee is right to call for clearer consensus by age, subject, and learning goal. I support that framework. In some contexts, especially those involving foundational reasoning, close reading, original argument formation, or high-stakes assessment, stronger limitations remain justified. But in many other contexts, especially formative work, feedback-rich practice, selected STEM and language-learning tasks, research support, coding, brainstorming, revision planning, and teacher-approved task support, AI can be allowed more openly. The key question should not be whether AI was present at all. The key question should be whether the use served the learning objective or displaced it.

- This section addresses how FCCPS should translate broad student-use principles into workable classroom categories, controls, and interaction boundaries. The Committee also broadly agrees that categories should be applied in light of grade band, learning goal, subject, and assignment context, and that controls should be credible enough to support real implementation rather than rely mainly on after-the-fact suspicion or weak enforcement.
- Differences Over Standardization, Teacher Discretion, and Interaction Boundaries
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on how prescriptive the category framework should be and how much discretion should remain with teachers. One position favors clearer district-level categories, stronger prohibitions for uses that undermine independent reasoning or assessment validity, and control strategies built into assignment design and controlled follow-on assessments. Another position also supports a common taxonomy, but places greater weight on teacher judgment, contextual flexibility, and a larger space for conditional use where AI may support learning if bounded appropriately. Members also differ on how explicitly policy should regulate interaction styles such as task-support, task-substitution, conversational use, and role-simulating use, especially where developmental, psychological, or instructional concerns may arise.

## WS-POL-H – Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback – Adriana Palacios
- Decision 11 — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)
- The committee generally agrees that transparency, communication, and accountability are essential to building trust in the use of AI in FCCPS. There is broad alignment that students and families should understand how AI is used and how it affects learning and student experience.
- The committee also recognizes that AI tools are already widely accessible outside of school environments and are increasingly part of students’ everyday reality. As a result, communication should not focus solely on restrictions or risk mitigation, but also on helping families understand how AI can be used responsibly to support learning.
- There are important tradeoffs regarding how these commitments are implemented. These include balancing clarity and transparency with administrative burden, ensuring consistency across classrooms while preserving educator flexibility, and determining when consent or opt-out mechanisms are appropriate versus impractical.

## WS-POL-H – Student Use Categories & Controls – Anubav Vasudevan
- The Board should direct the schools to develop a scheme for categorizing generative AI use cases for the purpose of regulating what counts as permissible and impermissible student use. The adopted scheme should reflect a human-centered approach to AI. The use of AI technologies may be permitted and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but the people involved in ensuring the responsible use of such technologies must remain accountable to existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.
- There are many dimensions along which a particular AI student use case may be characterized for the purpose of responsible regulation. These include, for example:
- A specification of the generative AI technology or technologies that will be used.
- A description of the manner in which those AI technologies will be used (e.g., students may be supplied with a set of permissible prompts or prompt-forms to use when interacting with an AI chatbot; or their at-home use of an AI technology may require adult supervision).

## WS-POL-J – Students with Disabilities and Human Decision Safeguards – Chair Preface
- Shared Guardrails for Disability-Related and Consequential Uses
- This section addresses how FCCPS should protect students with disabilities and preserve human judgment in decisions that materially affect student rights, access, accommodations, placement, and support. The Committee broadly agrees that IEP/504 and related determinations must remain human-led, that AI may assist but not substitute for qualified personnel, and that any such use must be supervised, documented, and constrained. The Committee also broadly agrees that accessibility and accommodation requirements must be considered explicitly in student-facing AI use, and that uses affecting protected groups or legally consequential educational decisions warrant stronger review for bias, accessibility, privacy, and disparate impact. Meeting 5 reinforces that this section should state clear policy guardrails while leaving operational detail to later implementation and regulation.
- Differences Over the Scope of Permissible Assistive Use
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on how much assistive AI use should be permitted in disability-related or otherwise consequential contexts. One position favors a narrower posture, limiting AI use as much as possible where accommodations, eligibility, placement, discipline, or other sensitive determinations are involved, emphasizing that qualified professionals must create and customize IEP/504 plans and that generative AI should not receive student information or function as more than a tightly bounded starting point. Another position allows limited assistive use under strict safeguards, emphasizing that human leadership, documentation, accessibility review, and bias-aware oversight can preserve student protections while still permitting carefully bounded support functions such as brainstorming, document support, or creation of accessible materials subject to human review. Members may also differ on whether this section should remain tightly focused on students with disabilities or speak more broadly to protected-group safeguards and civil-rights implications across FCCPS AI use.

- This section addresses how FCCPS should communicate AI-related expectations and safeguards to students, families, staff, and the broader community, and how the district should structure accountability, notice, consent, and feedback processes around AI use. ## WS-POL-L – Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback – David Berol
- With respect to any summative assessment of a student or any decision affecting student educational rights (such as a disciplinary record or an IEP), students and parents should have the right to know whether the assessment or decision was made:
- (A) without any reliance on AI tools;
- (B) with purely non-substantive contributions from AI tools; or
- (C) with substantive contributions from an AI tool

## WS-POL-L – Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback – Thomas Colvin
- There should be a single, high-level official that is solely responsible for the success or failure of AI in FCCPS. That official must have the following three qualities. First, they must have an enterprise-level view of the school system, since AI-related concerns cover a breadth of domains (learning outcomes, academic integrity, mental health, privacy, acquisitions, tech support, etc.). Second, they must be empowered to approve or veto AI-related actions; oversight responsibility without authority is a recipe for organizational failure. Third, their position must have relatively low turnover to avoid the moral hazard of their successor being blamed for the faults of the predecessor.
- FCCPS should institute the Rapid-Alert & Re-Assessment Pathway, which is a method for gathering frequent feedback from the local community on AI adoption in K-12 education. It was created with support from the State Council of Higher Education for Virginia (SCHEV) and it:
- Invites reports from every stakeholder—teachers, administrators, and students—through a one-click online form and a dedicated helpline.
- Triage-flags submissions within 48 hours (e.g., data-privacy breach, discriminatory outputs, instructional harm) and posts a public “under review” notice for the tool in question.

## WS-POL-N – Workforce & Budget Implications – Chair Preface
- Common Ground on Capacity, Workload, and Implementation Reality
- This section addresses how FCCPS should account for the budgeting, staffing, workload, and implementation-capacity implications of AI policy and practice. The Committee broadly agrees that these issues are not secondary operational details, but practical constraints that shape what policy can responsibly require. The Committee also broadly agrees that AI may reduce some burdens in planning, drafting, or administrative support, while also creating new demands in training, review, compliance, monitoring, and communication. Any policy that assumes unlimited staff capacity or ignores the real burden of implementation risks becoming unsustainable in practice.
- Differences Over Efficiency Gains Versus Burden Shifting
- Within that shared foundation, members differ on the balance between efficiency gains and burden shifting. One position emphasizes that AI may help relieve teacher workload and support more cost-effective delivery of some educational practices, provided human judgment remains primary and use is well governed. Another is more skeptical, warning that apparent efficiencies may be offset by new obligations such as training, tool review, disclosure, assessment redesign, vendor oversight, and compliance monitoring, and that these added demands may fall unevenly on teachers and staff. Members also differ on how much this section should speak directly to staffing levels versus focusing more narrowly on workload protection, implementation burden, and district capacity.

## WS POL N – Workforce & Budget Implications –  David Berol
- AI adoption may have an adverse long-term impact on faculty working conditions and availability of human-based educational services in Falls Church City.  Increasing the scope of teacher responsibilities, concurrent with providing AI tools that purport to make teachers more efficient, risks amounting to “AI replacing teachers,” or at least being perceived as such.
- To mitigate these concerns, the Board should reassure the community that it would be inappropriate to cite AI-driven efficiency gains as a justification to:
- Reduce the teacher workforce.
- Delay the backfill of faculty vacancies.

## WS-POL-O – Reassessment and Updating of Policy Analysis – Chair Preface
- Shared Commitment to Ongoing Review and Policy Adaptation
- This section addresses how FCCPS should reassess, update, and govern AI policy over time as technology, educational uses, and associated risks continue to change. The Committee broadly agrees that AI policy should not be treated as static and that FCCPS needs a structured way to review new uses, measure effects, respond to harms, and revise course when needed. ## WS POL O – Reassessment and Updating of Policy Analysis – Adriana Palacios
- Decision — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)
- The committee generally agrees that any AI policy must be adaptive and regularly updated in response to evolving technology, emerging risks, and observed outcomes in practice. There is broad alignment that static policy will quickly become outdated.

As a result, policy reassessment should not only respond to risks but also ensure that FCCPS continues to prepare students to engage with AI tools thoughtfully and responsibly.

## Workstream Sections Preface
- The workstream sections that follow are included to show the broader analytic and practical foundation from which the policy drafting emerged. They are not merely appendices, and they are not all drafted as final Board-facing policy language. Rather, they capture the committee’s supporting research, baseline assumptions, scenario work, governance frameworks, implementation considerations, and issue-specific analysis that informed the WS-POL sections.
- These sections should therefore be read as cross-cutting inputs to the policy shell. In some cases, a workstream provides research background or definitional clarity. In others, it provides scenario testing, governance structure, practical examples, equity review, or implementation constraints that helped shape later policy choices. Some workstreams are more developed than others, and some retain a more working-document character. That variation reflects the committee’s drafting process and the differing maturity of the underlying issues, not a judgment that some topics matter less than others.
- The relationship between the two parts of this report is intentional. WS-POL presents the main policy architecture and the range of committee positions in a form designed to support Board review. The workstream sections provide the supporting logic beneath that architecture: what the committee learned, what concerns or constraints surfaced repeatedly, what examples helped test proposed principles, and what operational realities may affect later implementation. In that sense, the workstreams function as the committee’s analytic substrate, while WS-POL functions as the policy-facing synthesis.
- Readers should not expect perfect one-to-one alignment between every workstream statement and every policy contribution. Some workstreams were written earlier in the process, some preserve unresolved tensions, and some provide broader framing than the policy shell ultimately adopts. That is appropriate. Their value lies partly in showing how the committee’s thinking developed and where important tradeoffs remained live.
