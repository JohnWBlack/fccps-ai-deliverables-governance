Introduction

# Purpose of This Report

This report provides the Falls Church City Public Schools School Board with the committee’s integrated advice on how FCCPS should govern artificial intelligence in schools. AI now touches teaching, student work, privacy, communications, procurement, accessibility, equity, and district operations. The Board therefore needs a policy approach that is clear enough to guide practice, disciplined enough to protect students and preserve human accountability, and flexible enough to adapt as technology and evidence change.

# How to Read This Report

The first part of this report presents the committee’s policy-facing sections. These are organized around the major decisions the Board will need to consider and are written to highlight the clearest recommendations, the most important guardrails, and the principal tradeoffs that still require Board judgment.

The second part contains the workstream sections. Those sections provide the supporting foundation beneath the policy-facing recommendations: shared baseline assumptions, privacy and governance analysis, academic integrity logic, classroom scenarios, communications considerations, equity review, and implementation constraints. They are included to show the reasoning, evidence, and practical considerations that shaped the committee’s advice.

# Policy and Implementation

This report distinguishes between policy direction and implementation design. Board policy should state what FCCPS intends to protect, require, and uphold. Implementation determines how those commitments are carried out through approvals, workflows, procurement controls, staff training, communications, and oversight.

That distinction matters because AI is evolving quickly, school contexts differ, and some operational details will need to change as new evidence, tools, and risks emerge. Policy should therefore provide durable direction without trying to lock the district into overly specific administrative choices that may soon need revision.

# What This Report Is Designed to Help the Board Do

Taken together, this report is intended to help the Board evaluate how FCCPS should govern AI in a way that protects learning, fairness, safety, privacy, equity, and human accountability while remaining realistic about district capacity and workload.

The committee does not assume that every AI question calls for the same kind of response. Some issues warrant firm boundaries. Others call for structured discretion, phased implementation, or regular review as district experience grows. This report is designed to help the Board distinguish among those cases and adopt an approach that is principled, usable, and sustainable.

WS-POL-A — Guiding Principles, Motivation, and Challenges

Question Before the Committee
What core values, educational aims, and risk assumptions should guide FCCPS AI policy from the outset?

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

how and when students learn to use and understand AI as a foundational tool for future study and work;

how and when our schools integrate AI into the design and delivery of education to enhance teaching and support to educators;

personalized learning and the overall student experience; and

how to capture the benefits that AI may create and how to mitigate the risks and community concerns posed by AI.

This section sets out the principles that should guide the AI policy and the challenges that a good policy must address.

# Guiding principles for the policy

IB-aligned. The policy should align with the values of the IB program, which seeks to develop thinking, research, communication, social, and self-management skills.

Evidence-informed. The policy should require uses of AI be clearly connected to measurable outcomes and the ability of AI to reach those outcomes should be supported by evidence. The strength of the policy’s guidance should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate.

Risks-mitigated. The risks of incorporating AI into K-12 education are demonstrable and have life-long consequences. The policy should prioritize risk mitigation over the pursuit of speculative benefits.

Age-appropriate. The policy should account for differences in learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age.

Human-centered. Some responsibilities require reasoning, judgement, relationships, or accountability that can only be provided by a human. This includes responsibilities for instruction, learning, evaluation, and discipline. The policy should ensure that AI protects or enhances these inherently human roles.

Community-integrated. The policy should ensure that students, parents, and educators receive all relevant information promptly and transparently. It is better to over-share than to under-share. Community feedback should be prioritized for assessments of new AI-policy actions.

Equity-aware. The policy should treat equity as a full-lifecycle obligation spanning procurement, access, accommodations, instructional use, assessment, family communication, monitoring, and review.

Evolutionary. The policy created by the school system should be revised frequently, because AI capabilities, interfaces, risks, and their associated community experiences are evolving rapidly.

# Challenges this policy should address

Benefits are uncertain for K-12 education. Some schools and researchers have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI. However, benefits are often context-dependent and difficult to generalize, with many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users. Since the benefits of AI in K-12 education are not clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions to take are uncertain.

AI introduces risks. AI exacerbates existing risks related to academic integrity by allowing the creation of long-form text, data privacy by aggregating large amounts of deeply personal data, and inequitable education outcomes as some adopters may be able to gain unfair advantages over their peers. AI capabilities also introduce new risks, such as creating realistic media with content that is harmful to students or teachers, preventing people from seeking human mental health support, and novel cyber vulnerabilities such as students using prompt-injection attacks to influence their grades or private information getting leaked if the AI models use it for training. Whether adequate safeguards can be put in place remains an open question.

AI is ubiquitous and not reliably detectable. Students and educators will have access to AI, especially as it is increasingly integrated into productivity software, regardless of school policy. Those who adopt AI may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those who do not. It is not reliably possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI, making rules and policies difficult to enforce.

The future quality of AI tools is uncertain. The AI community generally believes that their tools are improving and will continue to improve. However, “platform decay” is a documented trend for software systems to get worse over time as companies reduce service costs and quality to maximize profit. Tools are likely to be updated rapidly and it is impossible to know whether specific tools the school adopts will improve or decay in the future.

# Overall implementation guidance

The policy should create processes that lead to an appropriate balance between preventing harm and preparing students for a rapidly changing world. These processes should prioritize being protective and mitigating harm but cannot be overly prohibitory or rigid. Rigid restrictions can make it harder to experiment with new approaches that may create benefits, leaving students and educators to develop AI literacy through means that are informal and potentially unsafe, rather than guided practice in a safe environment. The following recommendations will help the district find and maintain balance.

The policy should clearly articulate the major challenges posed by AI adoption in FCCPS and ensure that every policy action explicitly describes how it addresses one or more of the major challenges. Requiring explicit justifications for actions raises the quality of the decision-making process by incentivizing carefully reasoned actions and giving broader stakeholders concrete justifications to evaluate and respond to. This also leads to more balanced outcomes since actions that are overly permissive or restrictive are hard to justify. Further, this will ensure that the resulting actions are addressing major challenges, rather than just minor challenges or feel-good issues. The school district will create a much stronger set of actions and a far more compelling story to push for effective changes.

Similarly, the policy should require an experimentation process for FCCPS to assess the effectiveness of any new AI action. This includes defining the specific benefits it expects to achieve, determining how those benefits will be measured, establishing a pre-implementation baseline, and assessing outcomes afterward. With this discipline, the district will produce decisions that are defensible, visible in their effects, and correctable when they fall short. Importantly, this experimental process enables iteration to converge on solutions that appropriately balance risks and rewards.

Taken together, these principles and challenges define the policy problem FCCPS must solve. The district should not begin from the assumption that AI is inherently beneficial, nor from the assumption that existing safeguards are sufficient. It should begin by naming the educational, developmental, governance, and equity challenges clearly, then judging each proposed use of AI by whether it advances FCCPS’s educational aims without displacing the human responsibilities schools exist to protect. The sections that follow apply that discipline to the major policy questions before the Board.

WS-POL-B — Adoption vs. Restriction Posture

Question Before the Committee

What overall posture should FCCPS adopt toward AI use, given competing needs for protection, preparation, and enforceability?

FCCPS should take a bounded, developmentally sensitive approach to AI. The committee does not support a blanket ban, and it does not support AI adoption for its own sake. AI should be allowed where it supports learning or operations under clear guardrails. Restrictions should be used where age, risk, assessment validity, privacy, safety, or decisions that must stay with people require them.

This means FCCPS should start with educational purpose. AI use should be judged by whether it helps learning, protects student rights, preserves human judgment, and can be implemented responsibly by staff. In many cases, learning and integrity can be protected through assignment design, drafts, conferencing, process checks, or other ways of making student thinking visible. In other cases, stronger limits are appropriate, especially when validity cannot otherwise be protected, when tools are unvetted, or when students are too young for direct or unsupervised use.

The committee also supports stronger protection in higher-risk settings. Younger students need tighter safeguards around direct exposure and use. FCCPS should be especially careful with student-facing conversational AI and with uses that raise concerns about harmful content, manipulation, over-reliance, data extraction, or reduced human interaction. These concerns narrow the district’s posture in some settings even within a broader bounded-use approach.

The committee also supports an iterative governance model. New or higher-risk uses should be introduced carefully, with clear success measures, monitoring, review points, and off-ramps if harms rise or benefits do not materialize. FCCPS should emphasize disciplined monitoring and the ability to pause, revise, or discontinue uses rather than expansion for its own sake.

Broadly, the committee agrees that AI use must be governed, that learning goals and human judgment should remain central, that developmental appropriateness matters, and that the policy should be reviewed as evidence and district experience evolve. A remaining difference is whether the policy should present this as design/process first with restrictions as needed, or whether that framing understates the need for stronger front-end restraint in some domains.

WS-POL-C — Scope, Triaging, and Policy Crosswalk

Question Before the Committee
What AI uses and risks belong within FCCPS policy, and how should they be triaged and connected to existing rules?

The committee recommends that the scope of AI policy be broad enough to address meaningful impacts on the school environment, while remaining practical and usable in real settings. The policy should apply to AI uses that materially affect student learning, assessment validity, student rights, student well-being, privacy, equity, school operations, or district governance. At the same time, the policy should avoid being defined so narrowly that it only addresses current high-visibility tools, or so broadly that it becomes difficult to apply in practice.

The committee recognizes that AI-related risks extend beyond academic integrity alone. While integrity remains an important concern, AI may also introduce risks related to privacy, fairness, developmental appropriateness, inappropriate content exposure, manipulation, or overreliance on automated systems. The policy should reflect this broader set of considerations while maintaining a clear and understandable structure for educators, students, and families.

To support practical decision-making, the committee recommends a triaging approach that distinguishes among AI uses based on their level of risk, consequence, and context. Not all AI uses require the same level of oversight. A low-risk assistive use should not be governed in the same way as a system that affects grading, placement, discipline, or access to services. Any triage structure should remain simple enough to be applied consistently across classrooms and schools, while allowing for professional judgment based on grade level, subject, and student population.

The committee suggests that triaging consider several key questions: the educational or operational purpose of the AI use; the level and type of risk involved, including impacts on privacy, fairness, and student well-being; who is affected and in what context; and whether the use influences decisions or workflows with significant consequences. These considerations provide a flexible framework that can evolve as technology changes, without requiring constant revision of the core policy.

The committee also distinguishes between policy-level guidance and implementation-level detail. The Board-facing policy should establish clear expectations and a general triage framework, while more detailed procedures, examples, and tools may be developed separately to support implementation. This distinction helps ensure that the policy remains focused and durable, while still enabling practical application in schools.

Finally, the committee recommends that AI policy function as an overlay to existing FCPS policies and regulations, rather than replacing them. Many existing policies already govern underlying conduct, such as academic integrity, student behavior, privacy, accessibility, and staff responsibilities. AI policy should clarify where AI changes the scale, interpretation, or evidentiary conditions of those policies, and where additional guidance may be needed. Cross-references to existing policies should be used to avoid duplication and to support coherence across the policy framework.

In practical terms, this crosswalk approach should identify where existing policies already apply, where AI introduces meaningful changes that require clarification, and where new guidance, procedures, or standards may be necessary. This allows the district to respond to AI-related challenges without creating unnecessary complexity or duplicative policy structures.

Overall, the committee recommends a policy approach that is clear in scope, grounded in risk-based triaging, and aligned with existing policy frameworks. This approach is intended to remain flexible as technology evolves, while providing administrators and educators with a usable structure for consistent and responsible decision-making.

WS-POL-D — Definitions

Question Before the Committee
Which definitions are necessary to make the policy clear, usable, and enforceable?

The following definitions establish shared language for this policy document. Where possible, definitions align with the Virginia Department of Education AI Guidelines (2024), the NEA AI Glossary (2025), existing FCCPS Regulation IIBEA-R, and applicable federal law. These definitions are intended to be functional rather than exhaustive and should be revisited as technology and regulatory language evolve.

Artificial Intelligence (AI). Any software system that uses machine learning, large language models, predictive algorithms, or similar techniques to generate content, make recommendations, assess student work, analyze student data, or support instructional or administrative functions. This includes both generative and non-generative applications.

Generative AI. A subset of AI that creates new content—including text, images, audio, video, or code—based on patterns learned from training data. Examples include large language models (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) and image generators (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney).

AI Detector. A software tool that uses statistical methods to assess the likelihood that a particular piece of content was authored by a generative AI model. AI detectors are probabilistic, not deterministic, and have documented limitations including elevated false positive rates for English language learners and students with disabilities (see Section F for detector-use policy).

Approved Tool. An AI tool that has been reviewed and authorized for use in FCCPS through the district’s tiered risk approval process (see Section K). Approval may be conditional, time-limited, or restricted to specific use cases, grade bands, or user roles.

High-Stakes Use. Any use of AI where the output informs, contributes to, or could reasonably influence consequential decisions about a student, including but not limited to grading, placement, disciplinary action, IEP or Section 504 determinations, special education referrals, or eligibility decisions. Teacher-designated assignments may also be classified as high-stakes based on a rubric considering the stakes involved, the authenticity sensitivity of the task, and student developmental stage.

AI Assistance. Use of AI that meaningfully contributes to the substance, structure, or content of student work. This includes generative AI output, AI-enabled editing or paraphrasing tools, and AI tutoring or coaching interactions. It does not include predictive text, autocomplete, standard spell-check, or accessibility tools embedded in standard platforms.

Disclosure. A student’s acknowledgment that AI assistance was used in completing an assignment, including a description of how the tool was used and which portions of the work reflect AI contributions. Disclosure expectations are developmentally appropriate and defined by the classroom teacher in alignment with district guidance (see Section I).

Human Decision-Maker. A qualified FCCPS professional—such as a classroom teacher, school psychologist, speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, school social worker, counselor, or administrator—who retains final authority over consequential decisions affecting students. AI may support but not replace human judgment in these contexts (see Section J).

Vendor / Data Processor. Any third-party entity that provides AI tools or services to FCCPS and that receives, processes, stores, or has access to student data in connection with those services. Includes sub-processors to whom a vendor transfers student data.

Consumer-Grade AI Tool. An AI application designed for the public that has not been reviewed or approved through FCCPS’s governance process. Consumer-grade tools may not meet FERPA, COPPA, or FCCPS data governance standards and are not authorized for use with student data.

Algorithmic Bias. Systematic, unwanted unfairness in how an AI system detects patterns or makes decisions, often reflecting existing inequities related to race, disability, language, gender, or socioeconomic status. In an FCCPS context, algorithmic bias may manifest in AI detectors disproportionately flagging English language learners or neurodivergent students, or in predictive tools that reproduce historical patterns of over-identification or under-referral (see Section M).

Data Processing Agreement (DPA). A binding contract between FCCPS and a vendor specifying how student data may be collected, used, stored, shared, and deleted; the vendor’s obligations under FERPA, COPPA, and Virginia law; sub processor restrictions; breach notification requirements; and retention and deletion schedules. Required for all Tier 2 and Tier 3 AI tools (see Section K).

Tiered Risk Classification. A three-level framework for categorizing AI tools based on the sensitivity of data they process and the consequentiality of their use, ranging from Tier 1 (low risk, no student-identifiable data) to Tier 3 (high risk, personally identifiable information or consequential decisions). Classification determines governance, approval, consent, and monitoring requirements (see Section K).

Embedded AI Feature. An AI capability introduced within an already-approved platform (e.g., generative AI added to Google Workspace, Microsoft Teams, or Canvas) that processes student data in ways not covered by the original approval. Embedded AI features must be reviewed independently under the tiered framework; existing platform approval does not automatically extend to new AI functionality (see Section K, Escalation Rules).

Routine Educational Purpose. Under FERPA, an educational interest that a district has identified in its annual notification to families as a legitimate reason for accessing or sharing student education records without additional consent. FCCPS should determine which AI-related data uses fall within the routine-use exception and which require separate parental consent, particularly for Tier 3 tools (see Section K).

Bounded Pilot. A time-limited, scoped implementation of an AI tool or practice in a defined number of classrooms, grade bands, or buildings, with pre-established evaluation criteria, a defined endpoint, and a decision framework for expansion, continuation, or termination (see Section O).

AI Literacy. The knowledge and skills needed to understand how AI systems work, to use AI tools effectively and responsibly, and to critically evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, bias, and appropriateness. AI literacy is both a learning objective across grade bands and a professional development requirement for staff (see Sections F, G, and WS-IPC).

Cross-References: Section K (tiered risk model, DPA requirements, and vendor governance); Section J (human decision-making safeguards); Section I (disclosure and citation); Section F (AI detector posture); Section M (equity and algorithmic bias); Section O (bounded pilots and reassessment); WS-IPC (AI literacy and PD); VDOE AI Guidelines (2024); FCCPS Regulation IIBEA-R; NEA AI Glossary (2025).

WS-POL-E — Staff and Teacher Use of AI

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS govern educator use of AI so that it remains assistive and does not displace professional judgment?

# Purpose and Guiding Principles

The committee recommends that Falls Church City Public Schools govern staff and teacher use of artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI, in ways that preserve professional judgment, protect students, and maintain trust with families. The committee believes AI may play a bounded, assistive role for educators, but should augment rather than replace human responsibility for teaching, learning, and student-facing decisions.

The committee agrees that policy should be clear, principled, and understandable to educators, students, and families. At the same time, the committee acknowledges that members hold different views on certain applications of AI, particularly in areas touching student feedback, evaluation, and academic integrity tools. This policy reflects shared ground while noting areas where future clarification or Board direction may be required

# Appropriate Uses of AI by Staff and Teachers

The committee agrees that staff and teachers may use AI tools for supportive, preparatory, and operational purposes, provided that educators exercise meaningful human judgment and take responsibility for final outputs. Areas of broad agreement include permitting AI use for:

Lesson planning, brainstorming, and preparation of instructional materials, with human review and ownership.

Translation and creation of accessible materials, subject to human verification prior to distribution.

Administrative and operational support, such as scheduling or summarizing non-sensitive information.

Assistive technologies that do not generate substantive new content, such as spelling and grammar correction or transcription tools.

The committee believes these uses align with professional practice when they enhance effectiveness, equity, or access and do not displace educator responsibility.

The committee also agrees that staff and teachers should receive appropriate training before using AI tools with students and that equity, inclusion, and data-protection considerations must inform AI use across all contexts.

# AI Use in Student Feedback, Assessment, and Evaluation

The committee agrees that student-facing evaluative judgments must remain human-led. Grading, assessment, integrity-related determinations, and other high-stakes decisions are core responsibilities of educators and should not be delegated to automated systems.

Within that shared foundation, the committee acknowledges meaningful differences of perspective regarding the extent to which AI may assist with student feedback and evaluation:

Some members believe evaluative feedback should be the teacher’s authentic work product and that even AI-assisted drafting risks undermining trust, clarity, and the educator-student relationship unless subject to very strong restrictions or prohibited altogether.

Other members believe limited assistive use may be appropriate in narrow circumstances—such as drafting support, accessibility, or translation—so long as the educator meaningfully reviews, personalizes, and fully owns the final feedback provided to students.

Despite these differences, the committee agrees on the following guardrails:

AI should not replace educator judgment or decision-making in assessment or evaluation.

Student-facing feedback presented as the teacher’s work must reflect the teacher’s responsibility and accountability.

Policy should prioritize clarity and trust over efficiency gains in evaluative contexts.

The committee believes additional implementation guidance or future Board direction may be appropriate to clarify permissible boundaries in this area.

# Use of AI Detectors and Academic Integrity Tools

The committee shares broad concern about the reliability, fairness, and potential harms of AI detection tools when used to accuse students of misconduct.

Most committee members recommend a precautionary approach, provisionally prohibiting the use of AI detectors due to risks of false positives, bias against certain student groups, confirmation bias, and psychological or reputational harm to students. These members believe academic integrity is better supported through human-centered practices such as conversation, assessment design, and clear expectations.

Some members believe that if detector tools are ever considered, their use should be limited to highly constrained, non-dispositive advisory contexts with strong due-process protections and independent validation.

The committee agrees that, at present, AI detectors should not be relied upon as a primary basis for student integrity determinations and that any future consideration would require clear safeguards and Board review.

WS-POL-F+G — Student Use

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS structure developmentally appropriate student AI use so that AI supports learning without displacing authorship, reasoning, or integrity?

The committee recommends that FCCPS organize student AI-use policy by grade band, with more guided and limited use in earlier grades and broader, better-governed use in later grades.  Broadly speaking, the committee recommends more limits on AI use with younger children and broader use of AI with older children.

Notably, the committee had different views about the long-term risks and benefits of AI usage by students, which is currently an active area of research.  This was especially salient for older children in the context of workforce preparation.  This led to a strong interest in creating guided opportunities to practice effective and ethical use of AI so that graduates are prepared to be strong AI users.  However, there were equally strong concerns about students becoming dependent on AI and AI eroding human-centered, “durable skills”, such as learning, resilience, creativity, and critical thinking.  A key job for FCCPS will be balancing these tensions. Differential weighting of these concerns led committee members to advocate for broader vs. restrictive policies.

Across grade bands, the committee supports clear categories of permitted, conditional, and prohibited student AI use, with concrete examples published for students and teachers. Detailed controls and examples should be developed elsewhere in the package.

The committee consistently emphasized FCCPS must continue to prioritize foundational cognitive, social, and relational learning, and uses of AI must be safe, ethical, developmentally appropriate, and consistent with learning and educational goals. The committee considered several broad areas of student use of AI: 1) AI Literacy, 2) Personalized Learning, and 3) Direct Student Use: Educator-mediated and Independent use of GenAI.

# AI Literacy

There was strong agreement among the committee that AI Literacy is critical for all students (PreK-12) and FCCPS needs to integrate AI Literacy into the curriculum in developmentally-appropriate ways.  There are many AI Literacy frameworks that use the FCCPS school bands (e.g., UNESCO, AI4K12) and the committee encourages education practitioners to consider these frameworks.

The committee recommends FCCPS integrate developmentally appropriate AI Literacy across all grade bands and engage in vertical planning across grades and schools. UNESCO defines AI literacy as “the knowledge, skills, and attitudes that enable individuals to understand, engage with, and critically evaluate AI technologies in a responsible and ethical manner.” Core components include: 1) Understanding AI Fundamentals (data, algorithms, machine learning), including its applications and limitations, 2) Ethical Evaluation (bias, privacy, accountability, social justice), 3) Responsible Engagement (interacting safely and effectively, evaluating AI generated content), and 4) Human-Centered Mindset (understanding when and how to use AI to ensure that AI supports rather than replaces human decision-making).

Of note, AI Literacy was a student use case that the committee agreed was appropriate and important for students in the younger grade bands (PreK, K-2, and 3-5), whereas many other areas of student AI use had more caution for younger students. Indeed, the committee felt that the main student use of AI in younger grade bands should be related to AI Literacy and any direct usage required education-mediation.

# Personalized Learning

There is a lot of excitement about the potential for AI for personalizing learning.  Personalized learning may occur in the context of specific AI-driven software (e.g., DreamBox, Lexia, Zearn) as well as broad GenAI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude). For software packages, vendor/procurement guidelines from relevant policies should be followed.  Personalized learning through approved software packages were generally viewed as acceptable to the committee.  Support for the use of broad GenAI tools for personalized support varied by grade band.

Notably, there were strong concerns in the younger grade bands about students placing too much trust in an AI partner and having trouble distinguishing humans and AI.  For this reason, the committee favored maintaining stronger boundaries around conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating AI uses that may blur the distinction between human and machine interaction or encourage younger students to treat AI as a buddy, coach, co-teacher, or authority.  This concerns apply to both specific software packages and broader tools.

For students in older grade bands, there was recognition that GenAI holds promise as a tool for providing on-the-spot clarification and tutoring when students are working through homework.  The committee encourages teachers to support students in how to craft GenAI queries that are less likely to “spoil” the homework problems so that GenAI may be used in manner that promotes learning.

# Direct Student Use: Educator-Mediated and Independent Use of AI

The Committee generally agreed that: 1) learning goals remain primary, 2) AI use should become broader as age and student judgment increase, and 2) that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted. Broadly speaking, the committee recommends that any interactions with AI in younger children are educator-mediated and limited, whereas a mix of educator-mediated and independent use of AI is appropriate in older children.

# Younger Children

For younger grade bands, there was general agreement among the committee that educator-mediated AI use in the context of AI Literacy was acceptable. Other uses may be acceptable when there is evidence of an educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained, when there is close adult supervision, when learning and motivation remain, and there are minimal risks to the students in terms of safety, ethics and learning (e.g., mistaking AI tutors for humans.

In general, the committee felt that direct student interaction in younger grades with GenAI should be limited and educator-mediated. The early grades are not the place for routine independent chatbot use, free-form prompt experimentation, or unsupervised AI-supported task completion. Where students do interact directly with a system, that interaction should be brief, bounded, and supervised, with the teacher or another adult framing what the tool is doing and helping students interpret the output. It is particularly important, given that younger children may be prone to: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”

# Older Children

In addition to educator-mediated use of GenAI tools, the committee recognized that many students in older grade bands will encounter or use GenAI both in and out of school for learning support and schoolwork. For older grade bands, much of the committee discussion focused on independent use of GenAI and how permissible or restrictive policies should be. Regardless of whether committee members were restrictive or permissive, there was agreement that: 1) AI use in this band must support learning rather than replace it, and 2) students needed clear communication about when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted.  There was also a recognition that some learning scenarios may be especially well suited to assistance from GenAI and others may be impeded by assistance from GenAI.  The Committee recommends that the Board direct the schools to identify these learning scenarios in detail. Where AI use is optional, students should have a meaningful non-AI path or equivalent alternative.

Notably, there was a range of views on how permissible and restrictive student use policies should be in these grade bands.  By one view, there were deep concerns that GenAI can take over the essential thinking that writing is meant to teach.  Accordingly, it was recommended that teachers should set careful limits to ensure GenAI acts as a temporary support rather than a permanent replacement for fundamental thinking.  One specific recommendation was that educators vary their restrictions across assignments to prevent students from becoming dependent on technology for any single part of the writing process. There was a call to limit GenAI during early stages, like brainstorming and outlining, to endure students don’t skip the fundamental thinking to develop their own ideas.

In contrast, another view prioritizes structured opportunities for students to build AI fluency and judgment under teacher guidance in preparation for life after graduation. According to this view, it was recommended that FCCPS adopt a more permissive, innovation-forward, but clearly governed posture. AI should be integrated into student learning where it supports educational goals, builds literacy and judgment, and prepares students for the environments they are entering. Restrictions remain necessary in some contexts, especially where unaided performance is central. But the district should not define policy for older grade bands mainly by what students must not do. It should define it by what students should learn to do well, ethically, transparently, and with increasing independence.

WS-POL-H+I — Student Use Categories, Controls and Disclosure

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS categorize student AI use cases so that expectations are clear, consistent, and workable across a variety of classroom settings?

What controls and disclosure policies are needed to distinguish acceptable AI use cases from those that undermine learning objectives??

# Committee Brief

The Committee recommends that the Board direct the schools to develop a scheme for categorizing generative AI use cases for the purpose of regulating what counts as permissible and impermissible student use. The adopted scheme should reflect a human-centered approach to AI that aims to strike a reasonable balance between the potential risks and benefits that come with the utilization of AI technologies in the classroom. The use of AI technologies should be permitted and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but the responsible use of such technologies must be judged against existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

The aim of a categorization scheme is to reduce confusion and reinforce consistent expectations across a variety of classroom settings. Thus, every effort should be made to communicate policies regulating the student use of AI in ways that are understandable and accessible to families. Overly complex rules or disclosure requirements may create unnecessary obstacles to compliance, resulting in new inequities and limiting meaningful engagement. The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Board should define a simple set of broad categories into which AI use cases can be sorted, such as:

Prohibited without administrative exception

Prohibited without express teacher allowance

Permitted unless otherwise restricted

Such a broad-level categorization scheme will help the schools to devise uniform policies by providing a common framework in which to draw boundaries around potential AI use cases. It will also provide a common regulatory language in which to communicate these policies, in the clearest terms possible, to both students and families.

Apart from specifying such an overarching regulatory framework, however, the Committee does not recommend that the Board take it upon themselves to enact more specific rules determining what should count as a permissible use of generative AI. This is because the real-world scenarios that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Consequently, the concepts and categories by which generative AI use cases are sorted and the specific controls implemented to regulate their adoption by students should maximize the ability of teachers to exercise intelligent discretion on a case-by-case basis.

Nevertheless, owing to the rapid rate of technological advance and the associated challenge of maintaining AI literacy over time, teachers must be given concrete and specific guidance as to how to exercise responsible control over the use of AI by students. To strike a balance between the need for flexibility and concrete guidance, the Board should provide schools with a list of heuristics and guidelines, targeted to each grade band and developmental stage, that can assist educators in exercising responsible control over the student use of AI. Examples of such heuristics might include:

The use of generative AI technologies should be allowed in all and only those cases in which the use of AI can be given a clear justification in terms of existing standards of sound pedagogy.

Example: There is evidence indicating that immersion is an effective methodology for language instruction at the primary school level. If the use of AI technology can lower the economic and psychological barriers to implementing this methodology in the classroom, then its use by students should not only be allowed but should be encouraged by teachers.

Example: There is evidence to suggest that the ability to produce structured written language is significantly impacted by the language one has previously been exposed to in reading, and not just by repeated practice composing text. Accordingly, teachers may have good reason to restrict AI usage for the purpose of forcing students to read whole texts, and not just AI generated summaries, since this has been shown to promote a student’s intellectual growth.

Instead of regulating AI usage exclusively through restrictions on student use and disclosure policies, teachers and staff should be encouraged to explore alternative pedagogical methodologies that could circumvent the need for such controls.

Example: Instead of enacting policies restricting the use of AI to aid in the composition of written work, teachers might instead assign more frequent in-class writing exercises to assess students’ unaided writing skills in a controlled environment. Additionally, they may explore a “reverse lecturing” or “flipped classroom” paradigm, where students are delivered instructional content outside of the classroom and produce their work during class in a supervised setting where AI assistance is unavailable.

The Committee recommends that the Board advise the schools against implementing policies that focus on citation as the primary mechanism for responsible attribution of authorship to generative AI systems. Text that is generated by AI doesn’t qualify as a source, and so should not be directly cited. In fact, AI literacy programs should be devised, in part, to discourage teachers and students from interacting with these technologies as if they were accessing reliable reference materials.

Disclosure, rather than citation, is the more appropriate frame in which to address the responsible disclaiming of the sources of student work. The main aim of a disclosure policy is to help maintain a culture of honesty, trust, and responsibility in the academic community by requiring students to fully disclose any external aids, resources, or borrowed material that they utilize in their schoolwork. That being said, the Committee does not recommend that the schools enact a general policy requiring full disclosure of any student use of generative AI technology for school-related tasks or activities. Apart from being impractical, there are many serious flaws with such a policy, including its potential to infringe upon student privacy, the risk of over-standardizing classroom practices, and the incentivization for non-compliance due to negatively biased perceptions of AI-assisted work.

The issue of responsible disclosure of AI use is a nuanced policy question about which the Committee did not arrive at a shared consensus. In the view of the Committee, however, any such policy must:

Acknowledge the increasing ubiquity of AI technologies and their inevitable integration into content generation workflows;

Protect student privacy and respect rights to fair use;

Discourage dishonesty and non-compliance through equitable enforcement; and

Empower teachers to make assignment choices on pedagogical rather than policing grounds

# Summary Recommendations

In summary, the Committee recommends that the Board:

Direct the schools to develop a scheme for categorizing generative AI use cases for the purpose of regulating what counts as permissible and impermissible student use.

Define a simple set of broad categories into which AI use cases can be sorted for the purpose of providing a common regulatory language in which to enact clear and straightforward policies regulating AI use.

Refrain from enacting specific rules determining what should count as a permissible student use of AI, so as to maximize teacher flexibility and discretion.

Provide schools with a list of heuristics and guidelines, targeted to each grade band and developmental stage, to assist educators in exercising responsible control over the student use of AI.

Advise the schools against implementing policies that focus on citation as the primary mechanism for responsible attribution of authorship to generative AI systems.

Direct the schools to enact a disclosure policy for AI use that strikes a balance between the competing considerations of practicability, student privacy, incentivization of compliance, and teacher empowerment.

WS-POL-K — Protection of Student Privacy, Data Governance, and Vendor/Procurement Governance

Question Before the Committee
Protection of Student Privacy, Data Governance, and Vendor/Procurement Governance

What privacy, data-governance, and vendor-review requirements should FCCPS impose before AI tools are approved or adopted?

The committee recommends that FCCPS establish a structured governance framework for AI tools that protects student privacy, ensures responsible data practices, and creates clear accountability for vendors and staff. This section draws on WS-DPS workstream deliverables, Virginia HB1186/SB394 (enrolled March 2026), FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, Section 504, and VDOE AI Guidelines (2024).

# 1. Guiding Principles

The committee believes that student data protections for AI should meet or exceed existing FERPA and state-law requirements. The following principles should guide all AI-related data practices in FCCPS:

All AI tools processing student data must comply with FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, Section 504, and Virginia student data privacy statutes.

Data minimization: AI tools should collect only the data necessary for their approved educational function and retain it no longer than required.

No student data collected through AI tools may be used for non-educational purposes, including advertising, profiling, or sale to third parties.

FCCPS must maintain transparency with families about which AI tools are in use, what data they collect, and how that data is protected.

Vendor compliance is a condition of continued use, not a one-time checkpoint.

# 2. Tiered Risk Model for AI Tool Approval

The committee recommends a three-tier classification framework, consistent with VDOE tiered approval guidance, to match governance requirements to risk level:

Tier 1 — Low Risk: Tools that do not collect, store, or process any student-identifiable information. Require department-level approval and basic instructional-use documentation.

Tier 2 — Moderate Risk: Tools that process de-identified or aggregated student data, or that receive student work artifacts. Require IT review, a signed Data Processing Agreement (DPA), and annual recertification.

Tier 3 — High Risk: Tools that collect, store, or process personally identifiable student information, connect to FCCPS identity systems, or inform consequential decisions. Require full privacy impact review, executed DPA with sub-processor restrictions, parental notice, and governance-office approval.

# 3. Escalation Rules

The following conditions automatically escalate a tool to Tier 3 review regardless of vendor assurances:

Identity linkage: Any tool requiring student accounts or connecting to FCCPS identity systems (SSO, SIS, LMS).

Consequential use: Any tool used to inform placement, grading, intervention, discipline, or special education decisions.

Third-party data sharing: Any tool whose vendor shares student data with sub-processors or downstream analytics providers, unless all sub-processors are contractually bound to equivalent protections.

Embedded AI features: When an already-approved platform introduces new AI capabilities (e.g., generative AI, predictive analytics), those features must be reviewed independently. Existing platform approval does not extend to new AI functionality.

Consumer-grade tools: AI applications not reviewed through the FCCPS governance process are prohibited for use with student data. This is both a governance and equity issue—consumer-grade tools lack the privacy protections, accessibility standards, and bias review that FCCPS requires.

# 4. Vendor and Procurement Requirements

The committee recommends that FCCPS require the following as conditions of AI tool approval for Tier 2 and Tier 3 tools:

Executed Data Processing Agreement specifying data use limitations, retention and deletion schedules, breach notification procedures, and sub-processor restrictions.

Vendor commitment that student data will not be used for model training, advertising, or any purpose beyond the contracted educational function.

Annual vendor recertification confirming continued compliance, with provision for accelerated review if the vendor’s data practices, ownership, or product capabilities materially change.

Documented accessibility review for tools used with students with disabilities, ensuring alignment with IDEA and Section 504 (see Section J).

# 5. Non-Negotiable Constraints

The committee identifies the following as non-negotiable:

No AI tool may process student-identifiable data without an executed DPA on file with FCCPS.

No vendor may use student data for model training, product development, or any purpose outside the contracted educational function.

No AI-generated output may serve as the sole or primary basis for consequential decisions about a student (see Section J).

Tools used in special education contexts require additional review—including clearance by qualified professionals (school psychologists, SLPs, OTs, social workers, counselors), bias assessment, and family notification specific to AI use in the student’s program.

# 6. Decisions for Board Consideration

The committee identifies three tradeoffs for Board resolution: (1) whether FCCPS should adopt a proactive annual notice to families identifying all AI tools and their data practices, or affirm that existing FERPA compliance is sufficient; (2) what the consequence should be when staff use a consumer-grade or unapproved AI tool with student data; and (3) who within FCCPS is responsible for ongoing governance, vendor review, and compliance monitoring—and what capacity is needed to sustain this work.

WS-POL-L — Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback

Question Before the Committee
What notice, explanation, consent, and feedback processes should FCCPS provide to build trust and accountability around AI use?

The committee believes that responsible adoption of AI in schools requires strong accountability structures for governance and incident response, plain-language communication about how AI is being used, and effective mechanisms for students, families, and staff to provide feedback when concerns, harms, or new risks emerge.

# Accountability

The policy should create an accountability structure that gives a single high-level official the responsibility for the success or failure of AI in FCCPS. Compared to diffuse responsibility, where no one is clearly accountable, connecting a single person’s reputation to final outcomes creates incentives for better decisions, faster problem recognition, and stronger drives to correct course when needed. The accountable official should have an enterprise-level view of AI-related implementation issues (e.g., learning outcomes, academic integrity, mental health, privacy, procurement, tech support). Second, they should have authority to approve or veto AI-related actions; responsibility without authority is a recipe for failure. Third, they should have sufficient tenure that they will still be in the role when the effects of their decisions come to fruition.

The policy should establish a process for identifying the uses of AI that should be consent-based. Even if the use of AI is broadly accepted by the community, some parents will not want their children using AI tools or having AI tools used on their children in any significant capacity. FCCPS should recognize that gathering consent is a mechanism to protect the interests of minority stakeholders in such situations. # Disclosure

The policy should grant parents and students the right to know the role that AI played in critical decisions, such as those contributing to final grades, disciplinary actions, and IEPs. For example, such decisions could be labeled to indicate whether it was produced (A) without any AI involvement, (B) with only non-substantive AI contributions, or (C) with substantive AI contributions. # Notice and Feedback

The policy should adopt the Rapid-Alert & Re-Assessment Pathway, which is a mechanism for gathering and responding to concerns from teachers, administrators, parents, and students. It was created with support from State Council of Higher Education for Virginia (SCHEV) for use with AI-adoption. It proceeds as follows.

Invite reports from every stakeholder through a one-click online form and a dedicated helpline.

Triage submissions within 48 hours (e.g., data-privacy breach, discriminatory outputs, instructional harm) and posts a public “under review” notice for the tool or issue in question.

Convenes an expert review panel to decide whether to (a) maintain approval, (b) approve with conditions, or (c) suspend clearance pending vendor remediation.

Publishes an outcome summary so that stakeholders have a shared understanding of decision.

Logs all incidents in an open dashboard to spot systemic risks and guide future policy updates.

The committee also recognizes that access to AI tools already varies significantly outside of school environments. As a result, policies that rely primarily on restrictions within school may unintentionally widen gaps between students who have access to AI outside of school and those who do not.

Question Before the Committee
What staffing, workload, training, and budget realities must FCCPS account for in adopting and governing AI?

The Committee agrees that it is technically feasible for FCCPS staff to use Generative AI to substantially reduce the amount of staff time spent on a variety of work-related tasks (e.g., writing lesson plans, administrative materials, and e-mails to parents). The overhead costs of AI governance, such as:

Staff training

Implementation support

Vetting tools

Vendor oversight,

Privacy/security review

Incident triage

Evaluating and updating AI policy

The Committee suggests investing resources in centralized AI governance for FCCPS. We hope this will avoid inconsistency, avoid duplication of effort, and thereby decrease the costs associated with using AI tools.

We foresee the following trade-off: As greater effort is invested in aligning AI use with community expectations, anticipated efficiency gains may evaporate. As efficiency gains are prioritized, staff will feel pressure to cut corners in their implementation of AI governance.

Assuming there are net efficiency gains from AI use, the Board should not treat them as justification to reduce the teacher workforce or increase teacher responsibilities. AI-driven net efficiency gains should be reinvested in: (1) Improved student learning experience and 2) Improved working conditions for FCCPS staff.

WS-POL-O — Reassessment and Updating of Policy

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS reassess and update AI policy as technology, evidence, and school practice evolve?

The committee recommends that AI policy be adaptive and regularly updated in response to evolving technology, emerging risks, and observed outcomes in practice. AI policy should not be treated as static. Instead, the district should establish a clear and predictable approach for reviewing new uses, evaluating impacts, responding to incidents, and revising policy and guidance over time.

The committee recognizes that AI capabilities are developing rapidly and that students and staff are already interacting with these tools both within and outside of school environments. As a result, policy reassessment should support both risk mitigation and the district’s responsibility to prepare students to use AI tools thoughtfully and responsibly.

To support effective governance, the committee recommends a structured but flexible approach to reassessment. Review processes should be stable enough to support trust and consistent implementation, while remaining responsive to new evidence, technological change, and classroom realities. The district should define how AI uses are evaluated over time, what outcomes or incidents trigger review, and how feedback from students, families, and staff informs updates.

The committee recommends that new or higher-risk uses of AI begin as bounded pilots rather than immediate system-wide implementation. Pilot programs should include clearly defined goals, appropriate safeguards, and limits on scope and duration. Piloting should serve not only to identify risks, but also to better understand how AI can support learning, efficiency, and access.

The committee further recommends that the district establish clear criteria for evaluating the effectiveness and impact of AI use. Evaluation should consider anticipated benefits, baseline conditions prior to implementation, and outcomes over time. It should also include attention to unintended effects, including impacts on student learning, equity, and staff workload.

The committee emphasizes the importance of clear processes for responding to incidents involving AI tools. These may include issues related to academic integrity, privacy or data exposure, or harmful or inappropriate outputs affecting students. Response processes should include defined escalation pathways, clear responsibility for review and action, and timely communication where appropriate. In all cases, incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, and transparency.

The committee recommends that implementation occur in phases, recognizing that some elements of AI use can begin immediately while others require additional infrastructure, training, or evaluation. A phased approach supports thoughtful adoption and reduces disruption, allowing the district to sequence more complex implementations over time.

The committee also recommends establishing a regular review cadence for AI policy and related guidance. This may include periodic administrative review, School Board review at defined intervals, and accelerated review when triggered by significant incidents, technological developments, or new evidence. Review processes should be predictable, while allowing flexibility when needed.

Ongoing feedback should be an integral part of policy reassessment. Input from students, families, and staff should inform continuous improvement, alongside observed classroom outcomes, implementation challenges, and emerging risks or opportunities. Feedback mechanisms described in related sections (e.g., communication and feedback processes) should be integrated into reassessment efforts.

Finally, the committee recognizes that continuous reassessment must be balanced with the need for clarity and stability. Frequent or poorly communicated changes may create confusion, while delayed updates may limit the district’s ability to respond effectively. The policy should therefore establish a clear framework for reassessment that supports timely, practical updates without undermining consistency.

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

how students learn to use AI as a foundational tool for future study and work

## Principles for Developing Policy

As the FCCPS develops its policy, the policy must be developed with the following principles:

Evidence-informed. The policy should clearly articulate the human skills, competencies, and other outcomes it prioritizes. Recommendations should be clearly connected to these outcomes and supported by evidence. The strength of recommendations should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate.

Alignment with IB values. The AI policy should align with the values and learning goals for the IB program, which is designed to build the following five skills:

Thinking (critical, creative, and ethical)

Research (comparing, contrasting, validating and prioritizing information)

Communication (written and oral, effective listening, and formulating arguments)

Social (forming and maintaining positive relationships, and conflict resolution)

Self-management (organizational skills, such as managing time and tasks, and affective skills, such as managing state of mind and motivation)

Age-appropriate. Approaches to learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age. For example, the Primary Years Programme encourages PK-5 students “to become active, caring, lifelong learners.” As students age, the Middle Years Programme for grades 6-10 encourages students “to become creative, critical and reflective thinkers.” Finally, in high school, the Diploma Programme and Career Programme equip students in grades 11-12 with skills tailored to the pathway they will choose to pursue after graduation.

Centered on the needs of our students and educators. While we must look to research, policy professionals, and other communities for guidance in creating our policy, such guidance may not always be appropriate for or replicable in our community. We must ensure that feedback from our students, parents, and educators are prioritized when assessing whether a new AI-policy action is appropriate or effective.

Evolutionary. This is the school’s first AI policy and AI is evolving rapidly. The policy created by the school system should be revised more frequently than other policies that deal with slower-moving issues. This will require frequent re-assessment of the policy and its implementation.

## Challenges

We have identified the following challenges that make adoption of AI in FCCPS challenging. A successful policy must clearly address these challenges.

The benefits of using AI in K-12 education are highly uncertain. If the benefits of AI in K-12 were clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions would be clear. In reality, some schools have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI, while the broader literature contains many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users. Just because one school system managed to implement this new technology well, does not mean that another school system will necessarily achieve a similar result.

The use of AI introduces risks. AI introduces novel risks associated with academic integrity; for instance, by making it easier to cheat on writing assignments. Students may be less likely to reach out to a human for mental health issues. AI makes it easy to create hurtful or otherwise inappropriate images, videos, and sound recordings, including of other students and teachers. There are privacy concerns associated with student information being uploaded to servers the school does not control; student inputs may get used for training purposes, which present a pathway for leaking sensitive information to the broader community. Whether adequate guardrails can be incorporated into AI systems is currently unknown.

Students and educators will have access to AI regardless of school policy. This means that those who choose to adopt the technology may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those that do not adopt AI; this applies to both students and teachers alike. Likewise, educators may use the technology against the wishes of students and parents who do not want their work run through an AI system.

It is generally not possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI. This makes any rule or policy potentially difficult to enforce.

# Current State of AI and Education

## Working definitions

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a label given to a computing system that can perform a task commonly associated with human intelligence. The specific technologies given this label have varied widely over time as computing systems have evolved. What we consider AI today may not be considered AI in the near future.

Language models are computing systems that predict sequences of words in response to an input. Originally designed to use strict grammatical rules, the language models began to use purely statistical methods for predicting words in the 1980s. Once viewed as a form of artificial intelligence, these methods are now considered old forms of machine learning.

Large language models (LLMs) are the name for the current iteration of language models. They are based on a new computational technique, called the transformer model, that allows language models to be run on GPUs. This allows the models to calculate statistics over larger sets of training data than was previously possible. As with any language model, the outputs are sequences of words based on the statistics from their training data.

Generative AI (GenAI) is the label given to LLMs and the broader set of tools that have extended the functionality of LLMs to create statistical predictions of images, audio, video, code, or other forms of output.

AI Literacy:  “AI literacy is a set of foundational skills and competencies that enable individuals to understand how AI works, responsibly evaluate AI systems, and use AI effectively as a human-centered tool across various contexts, including learning, work, and civic applications, while adapting to its ongoing evolution.” (Digital Promise)

AI Education: The study of how to design, build, and research artificial intelligence systems. It involves developing the technical knowledge and skills necessary to create, improve, and advance AI technologies. AI education is typically associated with computer science and related technical fields, and includes areas such as programming, machine learning, data science, and algorithm development.

AI in Education: The integration of artificial intelligence technologies into educational environments to support student learning, instructional design, assessment practices, and institutional operations. This includes both instructional applications—such as personalized learning and generative AI tools—and administrative uses that improve efficiency and decision-making.

Personalized Academic Support. A student uses an AI to work through adaptively created algebra problems that reinforce concepts that the student has had trouble with. ## Federal, State, and Local Policies

Virginia provides a succinct set of Guidelines for AI Integration that were released as part of a 2024 Executive Order issued by then-Governor Youngkin. These guidelines boil down to: do no harm; don’t replace humans; use AI to empower success; keep updated, include professional development.

In 2025, the State Council of Higher Education for Virginia supported a Summit of experts and leaders to create a comprehensive Reference Guide for AI Integration in Education in the Commonwealth of Virginia. They found that K-12 schools are at a relatively low level of readiness for AI; at this stage, there are significant needs in infrastructure and teacher training. Appropriate actions are early-stage pilots in tutoring and foundational literacy. These pilots should be conservative and on an opt-in basis. They also noted that younger learners require heightened privacy protections and require age-appropriate safeguards.

They also recommend a method for gathering frequent feedback from the local community. Called a Rapid-Alert & Re-Assessment Pathway, this method:

Invites reports from every stakeholder—teachers, administrators, and students—through a one-click online form and a dedicated helpline.

Triage-flags submissions within 48 hours (e.g., data-privacy breach, discriminatory outputs, instructional harm) and posts a public “under review” notice for the tool in question.

Convenes an expert review panel (VITA, VDOE, SCHEV, and classroom practitioners) to decide whether to (a) maintain approval, (b) approve with conditions, or (c) suspend clearance pending vendor remediation.

Closes the loop with the reporter and publishes an outcome summary so that divisions and colleges have a clear, shared understanding of the tool’s status.

Logs all incidents in an open dashboard to spot systemic risks and guide future policy updates.

## Efficacy of AI in Learning Environments

### Kosmyna 2025. “Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task.”

Relevance: Using genAI during initial essay writing appears to be far less effective than using a brain-only or search-engine-enhanced approach for cultivating the IB values of critical thinking, creative thinking, and communication. Using genAI during the ideation stage of a project leads to an echo chamber effect where essay topics cluster around the top responses of the LLM, which themselves may be affected by the business priorities of the LLM company. “These findings support an educational model that delays AI integration until learners have engaged in sufficient self-driven cognitive effort.”

Summary: This Time article provides a plain-language summary. Adults (54) were assigned 4 20-minute essay writing tasks that are based on SAT writing prompts. While writing, their brain activity was measured by EEG. The adults were sorted into three groups; those that could write their essay 1) with the help of only chatgpt, 2) with the help of internet resources but no AI assistance, and 3) without any outside help, similar to the environment of taking the SAT. The research found the group that was allowed to use ChatGPT had the lowest brain engagement and “consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels.” In subsequent interviews, this group struggled to recall what they had written, whereas the other groups had retained substantial recall of their work. The essays created by the chatgpt users were more homogeneously written in terms of the concepts used to respond to the prompt and the language used to write the essay. The group that was forced to use only their brains–no outside help–showed the highest neural activity in areas that are associated with creativity, memory, and the ability to process meaning. This paper is not peer-reviewed.

Scenario Bank

Below are several grade-banded vignettes describing AI use-cases that illustrate how the values and principles endorsed by this committee, as documented in [V&P doc ref], can be applied in actual classroom settings. Each scenario is followed by a brief description of its potential benefits and risks, and a list of scenario specifications in which the described AI use-case should be allowed, restricted, or prohibited, in accordance with this committee’s values and principles.

These determinations reflect a human-centered approach to AI. The use of AI technologies should be allowed and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but the people involved in administering the use of such technologies must remain accountable to existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

The real-world scenarios involving the use of AI that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Accordingly, the examples described below should be taken as illustrative not prescriptive, and with the acknowledgement that educators must be afforded full allowance to exercise their own discretion on a case-by-case basis.

## 1) Grades K–2 : “Read-Aloud Buddy at Home”

Scenario: A 1st grader uses an AI chatbot and voice assistant at home to generate a story from a prompt , interacting with the AI chatbot to practice letter sounds, vocabulary, and spelling .

Benefits: Build confidence and engagement through the use of personally curated content.

Risks: Privacy exposure. Generation of inaccurate or age-inappropriate material.

Allowed:

The student employs an AI tool that is supplied by the school and engages with the AI chatbot using only approved teacher-scripted prompts and with adult supervision.

The student’s at-home use of the AI chatbot reinforces in-class instructional programming and aligns with sound pedagogical practices. If, for example, it is determined that excessive use of the chatbot discourages students from engagement with in-print materials, or inhibits creative forms of word-based experimentation  that are deemed important to achieve early literacy goals, its use should be limited.

Not Allowed:

The student uses the AI-chatbot to generate a story from prompts that reference the names of other students, their photos, or other identifying information.

The student uses a tool that records and collects the child’s voice or personal information without parental approval and FCCPS sanctioned controls.

The student uses a tool that is not supplied by the school or uses that tool without adult supervision.

## 2) Grades 3–5 : “AI Assistance with Images for a Science Poster”

Scenario: A 4th grader is assigned the task of creating a poster for a science class that depicts in visual form the differences between weathering, erosion, and deposition. The relevant information about the topic can be obtained from the various materials that have been officially sanctioned for class use . The purpose of the assignment is for students to exercise their creativity in order to visually represent science concepts presented in class.

Benefits: Supplement irrelevant skills , which, given the format of an assignment, may have a differential impact on the final product.

Risks: Obviate core skill-building activities. Produce polished work that can misrepresent student knowledge or abilities.

Allowed:

After researching the topic, the student drafts detailed descriptions of the images to be included on the poster and then uses an AI image-generation tool to produce these images and to assist in the layout and design of the poster . When submitting the poster, the student is required to clearly indicate that an image was generated using AI and to include the specific prompt used to generate that image.

Conditional:

The student uses AI to assist in organizing or summarizing officially sanctioned class materials . Especially in earlier grades, when assignments are not just meant to test content knowledge but also to help students acquire core skills deemed essential to later learning, more restrictive limitations on the use of AI may be called for. In this case, part of the purpose of the assignment could be for students to practice navigating larger documents, like a textbook or online resource, in search of specific information. Even if AI systems can reliably perform such preliminary organizational tasks, students should not be encouraged to offload this work to AI systems.

Not Allowed:

The student uses AI to generate a part or the whole of the poster directly from the assignment prompt (The student uses AI to generate a part or the whole of the poster directly from the assignment prompt (“Draw some pictures illustrating the difference between weathering, erosion, and deposition?”, “Create a poster that illustrates the difference between weathering, erosion, and deposition”) )

## 3) Grades 6–8 : “Study Coach for a History Exam”

Scenario: A 7th grader uploads course material to an AI tool and asks it to deliver a concise lecture reviewing the main points covered in the material. The student then asks this AI tool to generate practice questions about the material in the notes in a similar format to the questions that will be included on an upcoming exam. The student asks the AI for feedback assessing the quality of their submitted answers and providing advice as to how they can be improved.

Benefits: Potential to balance inequities created by high-cost instructional aids like extra-curricular classes or private tutors. Provides immediate feedback, tailored to student needs.

Tradeoffs: Overreliance on AI generated answers undermines deep engagement with course content. Possible misinformation and bias in AI generated summaries, questions, and feedback.

## 4) Grades 9-12 : “Writing Support versus Writing Replacement”

Scenario: An 11th grader is assigned a literary analysis essay that requires citing textual evidence and developing an original argument about a novel studied in class. The student uses an AI tool to brainstorm possible thesis statements and outline counterarguments. The student then drafts the essay on their own, occasionally revising it in response to targeted queries to the AI tool about organization, structure and style, while working to ensure that the essay is still written in the student’s own voice, and makes reference to the assignment and in-class discussions.

Benefits: Encourages a structured approach to writing that utilizes auxiliary tasks such as brainstorming, outlining, and multiple draft revision. Potential to improve the structure, clarity, and style of the final product through immediate, targeted feedback. Reduce writing anxiety and offer support to multilingual learners and students with writing challenges.

Tradeoffs: Overreliance on AI-generated language can obviate the core analytical and compositional skills needed to produce quality written work. Increases the difficulty of assessing academic integrity and may exacerbate inequities if only some students are adept at using AI writing tools effectively.

## 5) Grades 9-12 : “Synthetic Media, Deepfakes and Harm”

Scenario: In a digital citizenship unit, 12th graders examine how generative AI can be used create synthetic media and deepfakes that convincingly alter audio, images, or video of real people. The students use an AI tool to generate or refine short, clearly labeled synthetic clips  and then analyze how such media could mislead viewers. The students then discuss and develop guidelines for verifying authenticity and responding ethically when they encounter or are targeted by synthetic media.

Benefits: Builds critical media literacy by helping students recognize the capacity of AI to alter context, identity, and evidence in synthetic media, and by giving them strategies for verification and source-checking. Provides a relevant platform  for ethical reflection about consent, privacy, and harm, equipping students to resist participating in or amplifying deepfake-based bullying, harassment, or misinformation

Tradeoffs: Exposure to realistic synthetic content, even in controlled settings, may seem to normalize or trivialize the use of deepfakes, and may be distressing to some students depending on the examples selected. If safeguards are weak, students might attempt to create or share unauthorized deepfakes of other students or staff, leading to serious privacy violations, reputational harm, and disciplinary or legal consequences.

## 6) Teachers : “Lesson Planning and Differentiation”

Scenario: A 3rd  grade teacher inputs information to an AI lesson-planning assistant about student reading levels, known learning needs, and language backgrounds, and asks the tool to create multiple versions of core materials  while maintaining common learning objectives. [Allowed?: The teacher then reviews, edits, and adapts the AI-generated materials, discarding those that are pedagogically unsound or misaligned with local curriculum, and explicitly documents how AI was used in preparing the lesson].

Benefits: Reduces routine planning time thus improving the quality of work-life for teachers. Generates varied entry points, scaffolds, and enrichment options that support differentiated instruction at scale. Can surface new activity formats or question types that teachers might not have otherwise come up with, broadening their instructional repertoire.

Tradeoffs: Over time, increased dependence on AI-produced lesson plans may enervate teachers’ professional judgment, leading to in-class assignments that are misaligned with students’ actual needs, local standards, or up-to-date pedagogical practices. Inaccurate, biased, or developmentally inappropriate content may be introduced into lessons if teachers lack time or expertise to thoroughly review AI-generated materials before classroom use.

## 7) Teachers : “Grading, Feedback and Fairness”

Scenario: A 9th grade teacher uses an AI-assisted grading tool to generate draft rubric scores and feedback comments on short written responses. Students submit work through a learning platform that forwards anonymized responses to the AI system, which produces suggested scores and comments aligned with a teacher-defined rubric. [Allowed: The teacher then reviews a sample of AI-scored work, adjusts scores and comments where necessary, and retains final authority over grades. The teacher may choose to use AI feedback as a first pass that students see quickly, followed by more selective, human-written comments on key assignments.]

Benefits: Speeds up routine scoring and enables more timely, specific feedback, freeing teacher time for higher-value instruction, small-group meetings, and individual work with students. AI-supported scoring can increase consistency in rubric application and help identify patterns of misunderstanding or inequitable outcomes across student groups.

Tradeoffs: Algorithmic bias, rubric misinterpretation, or over-general feedback may unfairly advantage or disadvantage certain students, especially multilingual learners or those with atypical learning styles. Students and parents may perceive AI-mediated grading as impersonal or opaque, undermining teacher trust and professional responsibility.

## 8) Teachers/Admin : “AI for Parent Communication of Sensitive Information”

Scenario: A school counselor or administrator uses an AI writing assistant to draft emails to parents about sensitive issues, such as a student’s pattern of concerning behavior, or a student’s academic struggles. [Allowed: The educator inputs only high-level, non-identifying details into the AI tool  and requests help crafting a message that is clear, empathetic, and free of inflammatory language. The educator then revises the draft to include student-specific details outside of the AI environment, ensuring it reflects school policy, legal requirements, and professional judgment before sending.]

Benefits: Supports clear, professional, and empathetic communication in high-stakes situations, especially for educators who may feel uncertain about tone or language when delivering difficult news. Can help standardize parent communication, reducing the likelihood of omissions or the inadvertent disclosure of personal information. Lowers the cost of creating accessible parent communication systems that are accessible to families with diverse language backgrounds.

Tradeoffs: Inputting identifiable or highly sensitive student information into third-party AI tools can create privacy, confidentiality, and data-governance concerns, including potential non-compliance with FERPA or FCCPS policy. Overreliance on AI-generated language for delicate conversations may flatten nuance, overlook cultural context, or unintentionally convey a tone that damages relationships.

Engagement Methods 
Effective community engagement requires multiple methods tailored to different audiences because stakeholders interact with schools—and technology—in very different ways. Parents may prefer town halls or written feedback opportunities, educators often respond best to structured surveys and listening sessions, and students provide valuable insight through advisory groups where they feel comfortable sharing their perspectives. Using a variety of engagement approaches improves perception and trust, signaling that the district is not making decisions in isolation but is actively listening to the community it serves. It also strengthens buy-in, because stakeholders are more likely to support policies they had an opportunity to understand and shape. For complex issues involving technology, student safety, academic integrity, and classroom practices, the perspectives of many groups must be considered early. When engagement is broad and structured, it helps surface concerns, identify unintended risks, and clarify misconceptions before policies are finalized. In contrast, relying on a single feedback channel can leave important voices unheard and allow misunderstandings

or concerns to grow unchecked. A diverse engagement strategy therefore acts as both a trust-building mechanism and a risk-management tool, ensuring the district gathers balanced input and prevents issues from escalating or “snowballing” as new technologies are introduced into schools.

# Decision — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee broadly agrees that AI policy implementation must be phased, that professional development is essential before broad adoption, and that governance infrastructure should be in place before higher-risk tools are deployed. There is strong alignment that unphased adoption creates inequitable implementation and that staff training is a prerequisite, not an afterthought.

There are differing perspectives on how prescriptive the phasing requirements should be.

Some members favor explicit gate requirements between phases, where Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 infrastructure is confirmed operational.

Others prefer a more flexible approach that allows building-level discretion on readiness.

These perspectives reflect a legitimate tradeoff between consistency and responsiveness. There is also meaningful disagreement on whether special education-specific professional development should be mandated in policy or left to administrative discretion. This draft recommends the former, but the Board should be aware this is a contested position.

# Commitments to Consider

## Phased Implementation Model

The committee recommends a phased implementation approach with explicit requirements between phases. This model is designed to align with FCCPS’s existing Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS), extending a familiar and proven tiered framework into the AI governance domain. Staff and administrators will recognize the logic immediately, which significantly improves feasibility and reduces the learning curve.

Gate requirement: Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 governance and training infrastructure is confirmed operational. This is not a slowdown mechanism. It is a quality assurance requirement. Piloting higher-risk tools without governance infrastructure in place creates exactly the equity gaps the committee is trying to prevent.

Phasing principle:

Implementation should be phased by risk tier, not by school or grade level.

Piloting high-risk tools in school buildings with higher concentrations of students with IEPs and 504 plans, or buildings experiencing staff shortages, without full safeguards in place would create inequitable conditions for the students most in need of protection.

### Professional Development Framework

The committee recognizes that effective AI governance requires professional development as infrastructure, not as an add-on. CDT’s 2025 survey found that only 22% of teachers have received formal training on AI use and risk management — even as 57% of special education teachers report already using AI for IEP development. This gap between adoption and training must be closed before broader deployment.

The PD framework should include differentiated tracks based on role and responsibility:

PD should be designed to accommodate the full range of FCCPS staff — from experienced educators and support personnel (paraprofessionals, counselors, nurses, custodians, office staff), to librarians and language staff (ELL specialists, district substitutes), to early-career teachers who may use AI tools extensively but without governance awareness. Training should meet staff where they are, not assume uniform starting knowledge.

### Policy Review Cadence

The committee recommends establishing a structured review cycle that balances adaptability with governance efficiency:

Annual administrative review: The superintendent or designee reviews the AI tool inventory, risk tier assignments, incident log, and equity indicators each year. Updates at this level should not require full Board action.

Biennial Board review: The full Board reviews the AI policy every two years, incorporating updated research, technological developments, legal and regulatory changes, and community feedback.

Trigger-based accelerated review: Immediate review required upon:

significant federal or state regulatory change affecting student data privacy or special education, such as updated VDOE guidance following Virginia HB1186

documented harm or inequitable impact on students

emergence of new AI capability categories not covered by existing risk tiers

Tradeoff for Board consideration: Annual versus biennial Board review is a live tradeoff. More frequent review keeps the policy current but requires sustained Board time and staff capacity. Less frequent review provides stability but risks the policy lagging a technology landscape that is moving very fast. The trigger-based mechanism ensures the policy cannot become dangerously outdated between scheduled review cycles regardless of which cadence is selected.

### Pilot and Innovation Pathway

The committee recommends that new or higher-risk uses of AI begin as bounded pilots rather than immediate system-wide implementation. Pilots should have clearly defined goals, include guardrails appropriate to the level of risk, be limited in scope and duration, and include evaluation criteria established before launch. Piloting should also be used as an opportunity to understand how AI can support learning, not only to identify risks.

Pilot evaluation should include both quantitative outcomes (student performance data, usage patterns, incident reports) and qualitative feedback from educators, students, and families. Tools that cannot demonstrate benefit or that produce inequitable outcomes should not advance to broad deployment.

### Success Metrics

FCCPS should define a limited set of indicators to evaluate the effectiveness of AI policy implementation over time, consistent with outcome evaluation methods used in program evaluation research (Grinnell et al., 2019). Recommended metrics include:

Staff completion rates for AI PD by track (all staff, educators, administrators, SPED)

Reduction in potential compliance violations related to AI tool use

Number and tier classification of AI tools in the district inventory

Time from tool request to approval decision, by tier

Number and type of AI-related incidents reported, and resolution timelines

Equity indicators:

disaggregated incident and outcome data by student disability status, race, and language status (consistent with my capstone that uses the DEIPAR framework principles)

Family notification and consent completion rates for Tier 3 tools

Pilot outcomes relative to pre-established evaluation criteria

These indicators should be reviewed as part of the annual administrative review and reported to the Board during biennial policy review cycles.

#### Incident Response

FCCPS should establish a clear, documented process for identifying, reporting, and responding to incidents involving AI tools. Incidents may be caused by staff, students, vendors, or the tools themselves, and may include:

False or disputed academic integrity accusations generated by AI detection tools

Staff use of AI tools in ways that violate policy (e.g., entering student PII into unapproved tools, using AI to generate IEP content without disclosure)

Privacy or data exposure, including unauthorized sharing of student data with AI vendors

Harmful, biased, or inappropriate AI outputs affecting students

Student misuse of AI tools in ways that affect other students or compromise assessment integrity

Vendor breaches of DPA terms, including unauthorized data retention or model training

Response processes should include clear escalation pathways matched to the severity and risk tier of the incident, defined roles for who investigates and who communicates, documentation requirements for all incidents regardless of resolution, and timely notification to affected families where student data or well-being is involved. Incident data should feed into the annual administrative review cycle to identify patterns and inform policy updates. Incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, transparency, and due process for all parties.

# Decision Points and Tradeoffs for Board Consideration

Phase gate requirements: prescriptive vs. flexible. Should Phase 2 require confirmed operational readiness of Phase 1 infrastructure, or should building-level administrators have discretion to assess their own readiness? Prescriptive gates ensure consistency across the district; flexible gates allow faster movement for buildings that are ready

SPED-specific PD: mandated vs. discretionary. Should special education staff training on AI governance be required in policy or left to administrative discretion? IDEA’s qualified personnel requirements support the case for mandatory training, but mandating it in policy adds implementation burden.

Review cadence: annual vs. biennial Board review. Annual review maximizes responsiveness; a biennial review with strong trigger protocols may be more sustainable for a district of FCCPS’s size.

Research shows no standardized regulations currently address AI-specific risks in K-12 education (DOE, 2023; Roschelle et al., 2024). |
| --- | --- |
| Mitigation | Implement the tiered approval process (Deliverable 1). Maintain a district-wide AI tool inventory. Require DPA review for all Tier 2/3 tools. Communicate clear policy that unauthorized tool adoption is prohibited. |
| Owner | IT Department (tool inventory + DPA management) + Building  Administrators (enforcement) |

| RISK 2  Severity: HIGH | IEP/504 Data Exposure Through AI Tools  SPED students’ sensitive diagnostic information, behavioral data, and learning accommodations are increasingly processed through AI systems that may lack adequate safeguards (Roschelle et al., 2024). IDEA requires protections beyond standard FERPA, and AI tools were not designed with these requirements in mind (Haque & Li, 2024). |
| --- | --- |
| Mitigation | SPED add-on review layer for any tool used with IEP/504 students. Explicit parental/guardian consent for AI processing of disability-related data. Prohibit sharing IEP/504 data with AI vendors without IDEA compliance review. Regular audits of AI tools used in SPED contexts. |
| Owner | SPED Director + IT Department (joint oversight) + Legal Counsel  (IDEA compliance review) |

| RISK 3  Severity:  MEDIUM | Algorithmic Bias in Student Assessment and Intervention AI systems misclassify students with disabilities from marginalized backgrounds at rates up to 32% higher than peers (Nguyen et al., 2023). AI-driven behavior monitoring may flag cultural behavioral differences as problematic (Maslej et al., 2023). This can reinforce historical overrepresentation of students of color in certain disability categories. |
| --- | --- |
| Mitigation | Require algorithmic bias assessment for all Tier 3 tools. Establish routine bias audits with specific focus on outcomes for students with intersecting marginalized identities. Human-in-the-loop requirement for all consequential decisions. Review disaggregated outcome data annually. |
| Owner | Curriculum & Instruction (bias audits) + SPED Director (disability-specific disability specific review) + Equity Office (intersectionality lens) |

| RISK 4  Severity:  MEDIUM | Tool Proliferation Without Central Oversight  Without a centralized inventory and approval process, individual teachers and departments may independently adopt AI tools, creating an unmanageable landscape of vendor relationships, inconsistent data practices, and compliance gaps that IT and administration cannot effectively monitor. |
| --- | --- |
| Mitigation | Maintain centralized AI tool inventory. Require all AI tool adoptions to go through tiered approval. Conduct annual audit of tools in use across the district. Establish clear communication channels for staff to request new tools. |
| Owner | IT Department (inventory + technical review) + Curriculum &  Instruction (educational value assessment) |
