WS-POL-E — Staff and Teacher Use of AI

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS govern educator use of AI so that it remains assistive and does not displace professional judgment?

# Purpose and Guiding Principles

The committee recommends that Falls Church City Public Schools govern staff and teacher use of artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI, in ways that preserve professional judgment, protect students, and maintain trust with families. The committee believes AI may play a bounded, assistive role for educators, but should augment rather than replace human responsibility for teaching, learning, and student-facing decisions.

The committee agrees that policy should be clear, principled, and understandable to educators, students, and families. At the same time, the committee acknowledges that members hold different views on certain applications of AI, particularly in areas touching student feedback, evaluation, and academic integrity tools. This policy reflects shared ground while noting areas where future clarification or Board direction may be required

# Appropriate Uses of AI by Staff and Teachers

The committee agrees that staff and teachers may use AI tools for supportive, preparatory, and operational purposes, provided that educators exercise meaningful human judgment and take responsibility for final outputs. Areas of broad agreement include permitting AI use for:

Lesson planning, brainstorming, and preparation of instructional materials, with human review and ownership.

Translation and creation of accessible materials, subject to human verification prior to distribution.

Administrative and operational support, such as scheduling or summarizing non-sensitive information.

Assistive technologies that do not generate substantive new content, such as spelling and grammar correction or transcription tools.

The committee believes these uses align with professional practice when they enhance effectiveness, equity, or access and do not displace educator responsibility.

The committee also agrees that staff and teachers should receive appropriate training before using AI tools with students and that equity, inclusion, and data-protection considerations must inform AI use across all contexts.

# AI Use in Student Feedback, Assessment, and Evaluation

The committee agrees that student-facing evaluative judgments must remain human-led. Grading, assessment, integrity-related determinations, and other high-stakes decisions are core responsibilities of educators and should not be delegated to automated systems.

Within that shared foundation, the committee acknowledges meaningful differences of perspective regarding the extent to which AI may assist with student feedback and evaluation

Other members believe limited assistive use may be appropriate in narrow circumstances—such as drafting support, accessibility, or translation—so long as the educator meaningfully reviews, personalizes, and fully owns the final feedback provided to students.

Despite these differences, the committee agrees on the following guardrails:

AI should not replace educator judgment or decision-making in assessment or evaluation.

Student-facing feedback presented as the teacher’s work must reflect the teacher’s responsibility and accountability.

Policy should prioritize clarity and trust over efficiency gains in evaluative contexts.

The committee believes additional implementation guidance or future Board direction may be appropriate to clarify permissible boundaries in this area.

# Use of AI Detectors and Academic Integrity Tools

The committee shares broad concern about the reliability, fairness, and potential harms of AI detection tools when used to accuse students of misconduct.

Most committee members recommend a precautionary approach, provisionally prohibiting the use of AI detectors due to risks of false positives, bias against certain student groups, confirmation bias, and psychological or reputational harm to students. These members believe academic integrity is better supported through human-centered practices such as conversation, assessment design, and clear expectations.

Some members believe that if detector tools are ever considered, their use should be limited to highly constrained, non-dispositive advisory contexts with strong due-process protections and independent validation.

The committee agrees that, at present, AI detectors should not be relied upon as a primary basis for student integrity determinations and that any future consideration would require clear safeguards and Board review.
