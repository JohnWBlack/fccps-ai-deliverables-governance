WS-POL-B Adoption vs. Restriction Posture:

Three viewpoints guide our overall approach:

We concur with the 2026 assessment from the Brookings Institute that “at this point in its trajectory, the risks of utilizing AI in education overshadow its benefits.”

We believe that Generative AI (GenAI) tools are already used widely throughout the FCCPS community. Accordingly, our top priorities are:

Teaching AI literacy and

Mitigating the potential harm from GenAI tools currently available to the FCCPS community.

Bringing even more GenAI applications into the school system is a secondary priority. Before acting, the school should take the time necessary to be reasonably certain that there will be net benefits from adopting new GenAI tools. We see no reason for haste here, except when new GenAI applications are intended to displace more harmful GenAI tools that are already in widespread use.

WS-POL-D Definitions:

Generative-AI: See the definition provided by the Brookings Institute Report on AI in the schools: “Generative AI, the focus of this report, refers to a class of technologies that generate novel outputs such as text, images, audio, code, or video in response to user prompts. These technologies include LLMs, diffusion models, and other neural network architectures designed for content generation. Generative AI is a form of machine learning that creates new content rather than simply analyzing or classifying existing data. These systems are typically trained on vast datasets composed of publicly available digital content—words, images, and sounds . . . . Contemporary models increasingly incorporate curated, domain specific, and proprietary data tailored to specific applications, including educational ones.”

AI Literacy: AI literacy comes from technical instruction about AI (e.g., training an AI model in computer science class) and from pragmatic instruction on the social, safety, and ethical issues involved in using AI.   The core of AI literacy is understanding where AI use is and is not appropriate. Reasonable restrictions on AI use and encouraging AI literacy are complementary objectives. To the extent AI literacy is a call for students to use AI extensively in school, merely on the grounds that they will eventually need to use AI at work, note the following: When students graduate to the workforce, efficiency demands may very well oblige them to regularly use AI tools as a skill-multiplier. But school is fundamentally a place where students need to develop their innate skills, precisely so that they will have skills capable of being multiplied. It is essential to prioritize the development of students’ innate skills and school is a protected space for learning that need not always conform to the efficiency logic of the workplace.

Constructive feedback: Constructive feedback is information about a student's work-in‑progress that is intended to guide revision and improvement prior to assessment. It focuses on how the work can be developed or corrected, typically by identifying strengths, weaknesses, and concrete possibilities for change, rather than by assigning a grade, score, or other evaluative verdict on the quality of the work at its current stage of development.

Evaluative feedback: Evaluative feedback is an assessment of a student's work treated as a finished product that assigns a judgment of its quality as measured against specified criteria or standards. It typically takes the form of a grade, score, or evaluative judgment that summarizes how well the work meets those criteria or standard, accompanied by additional comments or remarks that serve to justify that evaluation.

Assessment: The activity of reviewing a student's work for the purpose of providing that student with evaluative feedback.

AI Detector: A software tool that uses statistical methods to predict the relative likelihood that a particular block of text was authored by a Generative AI model, typically considering factors such as whether word choice, syntax, and organizational structure are characteristic of Generative AI output. AI detectors are typically trained by exposure to a corpus of texts that were pre-labeled with known authorship status.

Citation fulfils an ethical obligation to not steal ideas from the author’s sources. The author acknowledges the labor of those from whom they inherit the ideas they are working with. In addition, citation often serves to lend authority to claims made in the present and it maintains a traceable genealogy of where human knowledge comes from.

Disclosure, by contrast, aims to fulfill an ethical obligation to the author’s teacher and peers. The point is to allow the teacher to understand what tasks the student off-loaded onto the Generative AI, whether GenAI use was consistent with the assignment guidelines, and thus to fairly assess student achievement. Without disclosure, students can only assume that all their peers are making liberal use of AI assistance. This engenders a demoralizing race-to-the-bottom in which all are ultimately deprived of the educational benefit of learning to read, write, and think for themselves.

In various circumstances, disclosure could consist of:

Informing the instructor that GenAI was somehow used on an assignment

Providing a log of all chat prompts to the GenAI

Providing the full chat log

Providing a narrative description of the general nature of GenAI reliance

WS-POL-E Staff & Teacher Use

Staff Use of AI to Provide Feedback to Students

Policy Recommendations:

For purposes of making policy relating to the permissible use of AI tools by teachers, it is important to distinguish constructive feedback from assessment and evaluative feedback.

Students typically acquire constructive feedback from a variety of sources. With appropriate guardrails (e.g., teacher approval, age-appropriateness) it is reasonable for a Gen AI tool to be one such source.

By contrast, assessment and evaluative feedback that purports to be from the teacher should not (in general) incorporate substantive GenAI content.

Substantive GenAI content might nonetheless be acceptable in teacher-authored assessments and evaluative feedback if:

The teacher adequately reviews the GenAI content before publishing under their own name;

The teacher obtains prior consent from the student’s parents;

The presence of substantive GenAI content is disclosed in the assessment/feedback; and

The teacher is able and willing to disclose, on request, evidence of the raw GenAI inputs and the extent to which they were modified before ratification by the teacher.

The Board should develop policies and regulations to distinguish substantive GenAI content from other applications of AI that do not raise the same issues. For example:

Image-recognition tools that convert student handwriting to text or that record multiple choice answers without the need for bubble sheets.

AI tools that merely fix spelling and grammar errors and make minor stylistic improvements to human-originated text.

Rationale:

Most adults would be demoralized if their performance reviews were drafted in the first instance by AI, followed by an unknown degree of human editing. Students and parents should be treated with the same respect that one would want in one’s own workplace.

AI tools are touted as relieving extraneous drudgery and freeing humans to focus on their core responsibilities. But assessments are not extraneous drudgery.  They are a core responsibility of the teaching role that does not need to be off-loaded.

Precisely because they are authoritative, summative assessments and evaluative feedback need to be the teacher’s authentic work product. Injecting GenAI content into the workflow risks compromising (or at least appearing to compromise) the document’s authenticity. A teacher’s bare assertion that they reviewed the GenAI does not fix the problem. Here’s why:

GenAI content is designed to be persuasive and superficially appealing, even when it doesn’t really make sense.

Grading is already a repetitive and time-consuming task. Adequately reviewing GenAI content makes the task even more repetitive and time consuming. Thus there is a strong temptation to defer to the GenAI content, simply to save time and effort.

Without more transparency, affected students and parents will tend to assume the worst: that the teacher is merely fronting for the AI model.

Any professional benefit accruing to teachers from incorporating Generative AI into their assessment workflows is likely to be short-term. Letting GenAI contribute to student assessment will foreseeably become a justification for teacher workload creep and reduced staffing levels. Unless walled off (see WS-POL-N) the net effect would merely be a one-way ratchet of less human involvement in student assessment.

Staff Use of AI Detectors

Policy Recommendation:

The School Board should provisionally bar all use of AI Detectors in the school system, with the potential for this prohibition to be lifted after the Board:

Implements a framework for the resolution of positive AI-detection results. This framework should:

Explain what sorts of evidence are appropriate to confirm or rebut AI detection results

Explain where the burden of proof lies in such circumstances

Adequately suppress confirmation bias (i.e., the teacher’s natural tendency to seek out evidence that will confirm what they have already been led to believe by the AI model)

Adequately mitigate the psychological and reputational harm to students who are falsely flagged by the AI detector.

Determines that the specific software to be used:

Is accompanied by raw training and validation data, sufficient for an independent audit of statistical performance and resistance to bias

Has been found, through independent audit:

To have a false positive rate below 0.1%

To be resistant to foreseeable efforts to disguise AI authorship

To be sufficiently free from bias affecting subgroups of the student population, such as non-native speakers of English and neurodivergent students.

Will be subject to annual re-audits so that ongoing performance can be monitored as Generative AI models evolve

Handles essay data in a manner that complies with student privacy rights and all other student rights pertaining to the use of student creative content.

Rationale:

The potential for AI detectors to have unacceptably high false positive rates, to be evadable by technology-savvy users, to be biased against non-native English speakers, and to provoke confirmation bias against accused students, has been raised by numerous observers in the last several years. While we are not in a position to definitively resolve these technical and ethical concerns, they are sufficiently weighty to justify a precautionary approach. Under this approach, AI detectors are specifically excluded until such time as satisfactory answers to the concerns raised have been reviewed and approved by the Board. This recommendation is furthermore aligned with the IB Academic Integrity Policy:

“The IB aims to avoid joining the “arms race” between AI tools that claims to be able to tell the difference between AI and human authors. It is better to ensure that students can speak about their work with their teachers rather than accusing them of misconduct based on increasing small differences in the predictability of their word choices.”

The proposed false positive rate (0.1%) is an order of magnitude below what is commonly promised by the current generation of AI detectors. This approach is intended to reasonably address the risk that students will be subjected to false AI detection results at any point in their school career. Supposing that the false positive rate is merely 1% per essay, that each essay is an independent risk, and that students write 30 substantial essays over the course of their schooling, that would mean that about a quarter of students would have to confront a false accusation from an AI detector at some point in their school careers. Demanding an additional order of magnitude at least gets this fraction to less than 3% of students. In any event, note that ascertaining the actual false positive rate of detectors, as applied in City schools, will be difficult because it depends in part on estimating the baseline prevalence of Generative AI use among students who know that their work is being screened.

Furthermore, in many cases teachers will opt to allow certain bounded use of Generative AI tools by students (e.g., to suggest edits to an essay initial drafted by the student). It is not clear to that AI detectors would currently be able to reliably diagnose whether any detected Generative AI use was in accordance with teachers’ allowed parameters. If not, then a detector would only be useful in the narrow subset of cases where absolutely no Generative AI is permitted in a students’ work. This will be difficult standard for students to achieve in a world where every-day writing tools incorporate Generative AI. For such scenarios, a proctored assessment environment with either pen-and-paper or a locked-down assessment word-processor is a more practical solution than an un-proctored assessment followed by an AI detector.

WS-POL-F Student Use – PreK-5

Younger children differ markedly from adults in their capacity to interpret technology tools that appear to be engaging with them socially (e.g., chatting or talking to them) They are prone to “plac[ing] too much trust in an AI partner” and struggle to “distinguish between simulated abilities and genuine human abilities.” A recent review by pediatricians at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia concluded that children under 6 “have a limited ability to distinguish between AI and human interaction.” Children in the 6-12 age band are characteristically prone to the following GenAI risks: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”

Considering these risks, school-sponsored use of Generative AI in grades pre-K through 5 should be primarily oriented towards teaching AI Literacy. That is, GenAI should primarily be used for demonstration purposes in instruction about Gen AI. It should be used minimally in contexts where the primary purpose is to provide instruction on traditional subjects such as math and language arts (i.e., “Applied Uses”). In all cases, GenAI use by students in this grade band should closely supervised by adults.

When the school is considering implementing Applied Uses of GenAI tools in this grade band, it should consider in particular:

Whether there is clear and convincing evidence of significant educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained.

Whether the intended educational objective can be achieved while still maintaining close adult supervision of all student use of the GenAI.

Whether the tool in question claims to be the student’s friend (i.e., to be an “AI buddy”) or to have authority over the student (i.e., to be an “AI co-teacher”). Such tools are inappropriate for younger students because they may be taken at face value.

Whether the tool would deprive children of motivation to reach educational milestones that were considered fundamental prior to the availability of GenAI.

WS-POL-G Student Use – Grades 6-12

Within this grade band, it is appropriate to expect that students will start using GenAI tools to obtain assistance in learning and completing their schoolwork.  Teachers need to impose pragmatic and nuanced restrictions on GenAI uses so that they function solely as scaffolding for the desired learning goals. Note a critical feature of scaffolding: it is intended to be eventually removed. By contrast, certain GenAI use cases seem likely to train students to have life-long tool dependency.

This concern is particularly pronounced with respect to at-home student writing in the humanities. There is evidence that writing done in “collaboration” with AI is analytically passive and less rewarding to students.

Furthermore, there is no analogy from the impact of calculators on mathematics instruction to the impact of Generative AI on writing instruction. De-centering the skill of performing rapid mental calculations is appropriate to conserve time and energy for learning more abstract mathematical concepts.  But Generative AI can now de-center the abstract work of figuring out what one wishes to say in an essay, finding evidence to support one’s views, and arguing persuasively from evidence to conclusion.  To what supposedly more important end are we relieving students from this labor?

The following corrective measures are recommended:

Teachers should recognize that they are de-centering whatever aspect of the writing process they allow to be AI-assisted. Depending on the learning goals of the assignment, teachers should establish precise restrictions on GenAI usage. These restrictions should vary from assignment to assignment to avoid training students into long-term habits of AI-dependency for any given step of the writing process.

Teachers should be particularly sparing in allowing GenAI at the initial stages of the writing process (i.e., brainstorming, suggesting organization, suggesting argumentation strategies). At this stage, students using AI are missing out on the most fundamental thinking processes that writing is intended to develop.

Develop GenAI disclosure policy and regulations that will motivate students to comply with the teacher’s restrictions on usage. Most students in this grade band are not so intent on cheating that they would stoop to developing falsified chat logs for purposes of deceiving their teachers about their use of GenAI.

Increase the amount of writing work that occurs in-class, where it can be directly proctored.

Introduce additional in-class components to writing assignments. See discussion under WS-POL-H (“Control Strategies”).

Other subjects and learning scenarios may be especially well suited to assistance from GenAI. There, student usage ought to be less restricted. The Board should direct the schools to identify these learning scenarios in detail. Here are two illustrative examples:

Formative assignments in STEM and Foreign Language: For most students in this grade band, it will be clear that summative assessment will eventually occur in a proctored environment without the use of GenAI (i.e., a paper and pencil exam). Thus, savvy students have an intrinsic motivation to avoid becoming dependent on tools that will be unavailable to them in the actual summative. Furthermore, it is common for students to discover that they are confused about math and science concepts (or foreign language grammar/vocabulary) as they are working through their homework. Gen AI holds promise as a tool for providing on-the-spot clarification and tutoring in these circumstances. Teachers should support students in how to craft GenAI queries that are less likely to “spoil” the homework problems and should rely on AI-free summatives as the principal integrity control measure.

Computer science assignments: To a certain extent, learning to write computer code could be analogized to writing essays in the humanities.  But in recent years the practice of writing computer programs has become pervasively and permanently AI-integrated. Therefore, there is less reason to be concerned about fostering AI-dependency here than in the case of an English or History essay.

WS-POL-H Student Use Categories & Controls

Use Categories

The Board should direct the schools to develop a taxonomy of Generative AI use cases. This taxonomy will help organize the scenarios of potential student use and provide a “common language” for discussing appropriate AI use. Having a common language will:

Help students conceptualize the various possible uses of Generative AI and the relevant boundaries between those scenarios

Serve as a starting point for discussions relevant to AI literacy

Facilitate clear communication among teachers and students about GenAI use expectations.

Once the use categories are defined, discretion to select the appropriate use categories for any specific assignment should rest with the teacher. An illustrative set of starting examples follows:

The student asks the AI…

to correct spelling, grammar, and minor stylistic problems in an essay

to translate between languages

to provide iterative feedback on computer programming code

to generate an inspirational sketch for a non-digital art project

to generate a digital artwork by iteratively submitting prompts

to locate reading materials relevant to a particular research topic

to summarize reading materials

to explain general concepts, without application to the assigned problem

to work an assigned problem in detail, after the student first tried it and got stuck on it

to work an assigned problem in detail without first trying to solve it.

to provide a single pass of substantive feedback on a first draft authored by the student without AI assistance.

to iteratively modify a first draft originally authored by the student without AI assistance. The student adopts, with minor adjustment, whatever edits are suggested by the AI.

to supply evidence and reasoning to justify the student’s thesis. The student then paraphrases the AI reasoning in their own words.

to generate work product from scratch, with the GenAI operating as the student’s agent and the student only providing iterative supervisory feedback until the work product meets assignment expectations.

Control Strategies:

Don’t make rules without a compliance strategy.

Don’t rely on low-probability, high-consequence enforcement consequences to incentivize students

Do scale the energy invested in compliance to the grade weight of the assignment.

Do re-structure pedagogy so that prohibited AI applications are inherently less useful/attractive.

Do re-structure pedagogy to take the pressure off teachers to detect whether students are engaging in prohibited AI use cases. Detection is less of a concern when there are downstream skill assessments in controlled environments that exclude AI assistance by design. For example: students who get an unfair AI advantage on math homework will eventually be assessed in paper-and-pencil tests where AI is inaccessible. Similar backstops should be introduced in classes focused on literacy skills. For example:

In-class assessments of how closely students read assigned texts.

Oral presentation/defense of written work product

In-class editing assignments to test students’ ability to respond to feedback or changes in the essay assignment.

More frequent in-class writing assignments to assess students’ unaided writing skills in a controlled environment.

WS-POL-I Citation & Disclosure

With respect to citation, note the following common-sense principles:

Text generated by AI doesn’t qualify as a source and so it shouldn’t be directly cited.

If Generative AI leads students to human sources, those human sources must be cited if the ideas therein are not common knowledge and the student reads and relies on the ideas in those sources for their work.

For citation purposes, reading an AI-generated summary of a document is not the same as actually reading the document. Students shouldn’t cite documents they haven’t actually read.

With respect to disclosure of Generative AI, the Board should recognize that the core of the issue is how much needs to be disclosed to the teacher in order enable fair assessment of the assignment at hand. This may vary depending on the learning objectives, the age of the students, and the subject being taught. In lieu of a comprehensive matrix of all possible scenarios, here are some heuristic principles:

In contexts where highly iterative and interactive reliance on Generative AI is expected and allowed (e.g., certain computer programming assignments) it is impractical to ask students to retain a chat log. For similar reasons, one would not expect students to keep track of minor grammar/style corrections that their word processor automatically suggests to them.

When the assignment is merely formative (i.e., as opposed to summative), teachers may be able to get by with a lesser standard of disclosure from their students regarding their GenAI use on the assignment. Nonetheless, it would be good for teachers to have a general awareness of what AI their students are using on the formatives, to help explain a sudden drop in performance from the formatives to the summative.

In contexts where the teacher has imposed a subtle boundary on permissible Generative AI use (e.g., “you can ask it to review your first draft but you can’t ask it to make the revisions for you”), there is a higher need for detailed student disclosure of what exactly they asked the AI to do. Knowing that one’s chat logs with the AI are accessible to the teacher will help to keep “honest students honest.” Without a chat log, students will be tempted to incrementally and semi-intentionally wander off into prohibited use cases.

WS-POL-L Public Accountability

With respect to any summative assessment of a student or any decision affecting student educational rights (such as a disciplinary record or an IEP), students and parents should have the right to know whether the assessment or decision was made:

(A) without any reliance on AI tools;

(B) with purely non-substantive contributions from AI tools; or

(C) with substantive contributions from an AI tool

The Board should develop policy and regulations spelling out what “substantive” means. With respect to documents bearing a (C) label, students and parents should have the right to inspect the record of substantive AI use contributing to the decision, so that they can discern for themselves how much of the writing and thinking behind that decision was really done by the people who signed off on the document. The Board should develop policy and regulations to clarify the necessary document retention and document request procedures.

The default assumption should be that parents do not want AI tools to be used in substantively planning or drafting summative assessments and critical decision documents affecting their children.  To the extent such AI applications are potentially allowable under FCCPS policy, they should still be contingent on obtaining opt-in from parents.

WS-POL-N Workforce & Budget Implications

AI adoption may have an adverse long-term impact on faculty working conditions and availability of human-based educational services in Falls Church City.  Increasing the scope of teacher responsibilities, concurrent with providing AI tools that purport to make teachers more efficient, risks amounting to “AI replacing teachers,” or at least being perceived as such.

To mitigate these concerns, the Board should reassure the community that it would be inappropriate to cite AI-driven efficiency gains as a justification to:

Reduce the teacher workforce.

Delay the backfill of faculty vacancies.

Increase the scope of teacher responsibilities.
