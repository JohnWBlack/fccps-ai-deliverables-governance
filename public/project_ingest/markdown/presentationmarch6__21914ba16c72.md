March 6 Notes

Opposition to Green Item R6: AI may assist drafting feedback; educators review/personalize/own final feedback; AI is never sole source of evaluative feedback or grades.

Acceptable Revision: Mechanical grading tasks (e.g., multiple choice grading, detecting objective errors in student work, handwriting recognition) may be automated with AI tools.  AI tools may not be used for any other student assessment purposes, except that when preparing written feedback and assessment of students, teachers may use AI solely for the purpose of fixing spelling errors, grammatical errors, and minor stylistic defects in their own writing.

Rationale:

I would imagine most adults would be frustrated with the idea of having their performance reviews primarily drafted by AI and then reviewed to an unknown extent by their human boss. We should treat student and parents with same respect that we would expect in our own workplaces.

AI tools are meant to eliminate drudgery and free humans to focus on their core responsibilities. Why should we treat writing feedback as mere drudgery? Isn’t it a core responsibility of teachers?

A flexible allowance for AI to ghost-write teacher feedback will undermine students’ willingness to invest effort in their own writing and to comply with restrictions on their own AI dependance.

Allowing teachers to use AI for substantive feedback without crisp boundaries will foreseeably be used to justify teacher workload creep, creating a one-way ratchet of less and less human involvement in student assessment.

Presentation on Deliverables

Draft Policy Outline

This is an outline of the policy document intended to provide basic scoping of what we’re going to cover.

Constraints:

Policy focus (for this year) is GenAI (including GenAI detection tools).

Policy focus is on:

Promoting AI literacy

Establishing reasonable restrictions on school-related AI use by staff and students

Promoting AI literacy is not 1:1 equivalent to promoting AI use in school, though it may overlap

Risks:

Risk 1: Race-to-the-bottom in a laissez-faire world. Unfettered availability of GenAI leads to cognitive dependence.

Mitigation: Restrict GenAI use where there is a community benefit from collectively declining to use it.

Risk 2: Well-meaning but largely unenforceable rules put complying students at a competitive disadvantage.

Mitigation: Don’t implement student GenAI restrictions that lack a coherent compliance strategy.

Risk 3: Well-meaning rules have an unacceptable enforcement cost:

Mitigation:  Transparently balance the benefit of having restrictions on student GenAI use against the harshness/intrusiveness of what will be necessary to enforce.  Sometimes the cure is worse than the disease.

Request: Fill in any major gaps in the outline and then adopt it as a starting point for further amendment.
