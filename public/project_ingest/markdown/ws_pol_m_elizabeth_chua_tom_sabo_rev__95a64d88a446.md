WS POL M – Equity and Accessibility in AI Use Across FCCPS Community – Elizabeth Chua Tom Sabo

As AI tools increasingly influence instruction, assessment, and administration, FCCPS must ensure that their use advances equity, access, and student well-being. Equity considerations should be embedded from the start of all AI-related decisions, not added after tools or practices are already in place. The following policy recommendations outline priority considerations for those with authority over district-level equity and technology policy.

# Key Policy Recommendations

Make Equity a Foundational Requirement
Affirm that equity, accessibility, and disability accommodations are core criteria for all AI-related decisions—from procurement through classroom and administrative use. Students are a protected and vulnerable population requiring heightened safeguards.

One of our primary considerations is that there's a lot of concern about data mining around things like detecting at risk students and determining disciplinary actions that can disproportionately harm marginalized students. See WS-DPS for the tiered risk framework governing tools that process identifiable student data, including escalation rules for tools used in consequential decisions and the prohibition on consumer-grade AI tools for student data.

Recommendation: Adopt a clear policy statement affirming that equity, accessibility, and disability accommodations are core criteria for all AI-related decisions—from procurement through classroom practice and administrative use.

Our current working policy statement surrounding equity in community:

FCCPS will evaluate AI tools and AI usage for equity and accessibility from the procurement stage to the classroom, including administrative uses. FCCPS will monitor and evaluate AI usage in terms of equity and access, and collect data on reported bias incidences. Monitoring and evaluation should be incorporated into relevant existing school structures. The committee must include, but is not limited to, School Technology Professionals, Special Education and English Language Learner coordinators.

Topics related to equity will be included as part of AI Literacy for students and staff. This will include understanding how models are trained and how to identify algorithmic bias.

Adopt Guiding Principles With Structured Review
Pair district-level AI Equity & Access Guiding Principles with a proportionate equity review process for AI policies, tools, and workflows. Guiding principles articulate intent; structured review ensures consistent application without overburdening staff.

We initially set up a equity and accessibility checklist (See WS-EQA-1) however, felt that while a checklist was important, we did not have the proper resources from the district to determine how useful or unwieldy it could be. Please refer to WS-EQA-1 as a starting point should a checklist be desirable.

Our working guiding principle: How can we ensure that our approach to AI sufficiently continues to uphold the standards and values on learning that align with the IB program, and that AI is used in the service of those goals, including equity?

Prohibit High-Stakes Decisions Based Solely on AI Outputs
Disallow disciplinary, academic integrity, or profiling decisions based only on AI detection tools or automated risk indicators. Require human judgment, contextual evidence, and equitable dispute processes.

Recognize AI Literacy as an Equity Issue
Commit to equitable AI literacy for students and staff that addresses AI limitations, bias, and responsible use—without assuming advanced technical expertise or creating unfunded expectations.

# Recommendation: Recognize AI literacy as an equity issue

Commit to district-supported learning that helps students and staff understand AI limitations, bias, and responsible use—without assuming advanced technical expertise

This can be a part of general curriculum at an appropriate age level

How are models trained? How does bias enter AI models? This goes along with the IB mindset. We need to teach our kids when to trust themselves and in doing so, how to better trust AI results where appropriate or where there’s a logical human/computer intersection.

AI as an ally could be an early education message. Let’s put our kids in the driver’s seat here.

Implement Feasible Monitoring and Feedback Loops
Track a limited number of high-value equity indicators (e.g., access barriers, accommodation conflicts, integrity disputes, bias reports) using existing structures where possible and use findings to revisit policy when risks emerge.

Recommendation: Require feasible, limited, and high-value equity monitoring—focused on identifying disparities early rather than exhaustive measurement.

Rationale: Capacity constraints are real. A small number of well-chosen indicators (e.g., access barriers, integrity disputes, accommodation conflicts, bias reports) can surface issues before harm becomes systemic.

"See WS-DPS for a detailed risk and mitigation table identifying specific monitoring responsibilities and suggested owners, including equity-related risks such as algorithmic bias in assessment and unreviewed AI features in already-approved platforms."

## Policy Implication:

Tie monitoring to existing structures where possible.

Commit to revisiting policy when equity indicators signal risk.

# Closing Note

Equitable AI policy is not about maximizing AI use, but about deciding when, how, and whether AI should be used at all. By centering equity early, FCCPS can reduce unintended harm, support educators, and uphold its values while responsibly navigating rapid technological change.
