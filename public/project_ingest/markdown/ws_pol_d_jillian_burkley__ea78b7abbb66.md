# WS-POL-D: Definitions

The following definitions establish shared language for this policy document. Where possible, definitions align with the Virginia Department of Education AI Guidelines (2024), the NEA AI Glossary (2025), existing FCCPS Regulation IIBEA-R, and applicable federal law. These definitions are intended to be functional rather than exhaustive and should be revisited as technology and regulatory language evolve.

Artificial Intelligence (AI). Any software system that uses machine learning, large language models, predictive algorithms, or similar techniques to generate content, make recommendations, assess student work, analyze student data, or support instructional or administrative functions. This includes both generative and non-generative applications.

Generative AI. A subset of AI that creates new content—including text, images, audio, video, or code—based on patterns learned from training data. Examples include large language models (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) and image generators (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney).

AI Detector. A software tool that uses statistical methods to assess the likelihood that a particular piece of content was authored by a generative AI model. AI detectors are probabilistic, not deterministic, and have documented limitations including elevated false positive rates for English language learners and students with disabilities (see Section F for detector-use policy).

Approved Tool. An AI tool that has been reviewed and authorized for use in FCCPS through the district’s tiered risk approval process (see Section K). Approval may be conditional, time-limited, or restricted to specific use cases, grade bands, or user roles.

High-Stakes Use. Any use of AI where the output informs, contributes to, or could reasonably influence consequential decisions about a student, including but not limited to grading, placement, disciplinary action, IEP or Section 504 determinations, special education referrals, or eligibility decisions. Teacher-designated assignments may also be classified as high-stakes based on a rubric considering the stakes involved, the authenticity sensitivity of the task, and student developmental stage.

AI Assistance. Use of AI that meaningfully contributes to the substance, structure, or content of student work. This includes generative AI output, AI-enabled editing or paraphrasing tools, and AI tutoring or coaching interactions. It does not include predictive text, autocomplete, standard spell-check, or accessibility tools embedded in standard platforms.

Disclosure. A student’s acknowledgment that AI assistance was used in completing an assignment, including a description of how the tool was used and which portions of the work reflect AI contributions. Disclosure expectations are developmentally appropriate and defined by the classroom teacher in alignment with district guidance (see Section I).

Human Decision-Maker. A qualified FCCPS professional—such as a classroom teacher, school psychologist, speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, school social worker, counselor, or administrator—who retains final authority over consequential decisions affecting students. AI may support but not replace human judgment in these contexts (see Section J).

Vendor / Data Processor. Any third-party entity that provides AI tools or services to FCCPS and that receives, processes, stores, or has access to student data in connection with those services. Includes sub-processors to whom a vendor transfers student data.

Consumer-Grade AI Tool. An AI application designed for the public that has not been reviewed or approved through FCCPS’s governance process. Consumer-grade tools may not meet FERPA, COPPA, or FCCPS data governance standards and are not authorized for use with student data.

Algorithmic Bias. Systematic, unwanted unfairness in how an AI system detects patterns or makes decisions, often reflecting existing inequities related to race, disability, language, gender, or socioeconomic status. In an FCCPS context, algorithmic bias may manifest in AI detectors disproportionately flagging English language learners or neurodivergent students, or in predictive tools that reproduce historical patterns of over-identification or under-referral (see Section M).

Data Processing Agreement (DPA). A binding contract between FCCPS and a vendor specifying how student data may be collected, used, stored, shared, and deleted; the vendor’s obligations under FERPA, COPPA, and Virginia law; sub processor restrictions; breach notification requirements; and retention and deletion schedules. Required for all Tier 2 and Tier 3 AI tools (see Section K).

Tiered Risk Classification. A three-level framework for categorizing AI tools based on the sensitivity of data they process and the consequentiality of their use, ranging from Tier 1 (low risk, no student-identifiable data) to Tier 3 (high risk, personally identifiable information or consequential decisions). Classification determines governance, approval, consent, and monitoring requirements (see Section K).

Embedded AI Feature. An AI capability introduced within an already-approved platform (e.g., generative AI added to Google Workspace, Microsoft Teams, or Canvas) that processes student data in ways not covered by the original approval. Embedded AI features must be reviewed independently under the tiered framework; existing platform approval does not automatically extend to new AI functionality (see Section K, Escalation Rules).

Routine Educational Purpose. Under FERPA, an educational interest that a district has identified in its annual notification to families as a legitimate reason for accessing or sharing student education records without additional consent. FCCPS should determine which AI-related data uses fall within the routine-use exception and which require separate parental consent, particularly for Tier 3 tools (see Section K).

Bounded Pilot. A time-limited, scoped implementation of an AI tool or practice in a defined number of classrooms, grade bands, or buildings, with pre-established evaluation criteria, a defined endpoint, and a decision framework for expansion, continuation, or termination (see Section O).

AI Literacy. The knowledge and skills needed to understand how AI systems work, to use AI tools effectively and responsibly, and to critically evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, bias, and appropriateness. AI literacy is both a learning objective across grade bands and a professional development requirement for staff (see Sections F, G, and WS-IPC).
