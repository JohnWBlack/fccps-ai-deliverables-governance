# WS POL E David Berol

WS-POL-E Staff & Teacher Use

Staff Use of AI to Provide Feedback to Students

Policy Recommendations:

For purposes of making policy relating to the permissible use of AI tools by teachers, it is important to distinguish constructive feedback from assessment and evaluative feedback.

Students typically acquire constructive feedback from a variety of sources. With appropriate guardrails (e.g., teacher approval, age-appropriateness) it is reasonable for a Gen AI tool to be one such source.

By contrast, assessment and evaluative feedback that purports to be from the teacher should not (in general) incorporate substantive GenAI content.

Substantive GenAI content might nonetheless be acceptable in teacher-authored assessments and evaluative feedback if:

The teacher adequately reviews the GenAI content before publishing under their own name;

The teacher obtains prior consent from the student’s parents;

The presence of substantive GenAI content is disclosed in the assessment/feedback; and

The teacher is able and willing to disclose, on request, evidence of the raw GenAI inputs and the extent to which they were modified before ratification by the teacher.

The Board should develop policies and regulations to distinguish substantive GenAI content from other applications of AI that do not raise the same issues. For example:

Image-recognition tools that convert student handwriting to text or that record multiple choice answers without the need for bubble sheets.

AI tools that merely fix spelling and grammar errors and make minor stylistic improvements to human-originated text.

Rationale:

Most adults would be demoralized if their performance reviews were drafted in the first instance by AI, followed by an unknown degree of human editing. Students and parents should be treated with the same respect that one would want in one’s own workplace.

AI tools are touted as relieving extraneous drudgery and freeing humans to focus on their core responsibilities. But assessments are not extraneous drudgery.  They are a core responsibility of the teaching role that does not need to be off-loaded.

Precisely because they are authoritative, summative assessments and evaluative feedback need to be the teacher’s authentic work product. Injecting GenAI content into the workflow risks compromising (or at least appearing to compromise) the document’s authenticity. A teacher’s bare assertion that they reviewed the GenAI does not fix the problem. Here’s why:

GenAI content is designed to be persuasive and superficially appealing, even when it doesn’t really make sense.

Grading is already a repetitive and time-consuming task. Adequately reviewing GenAI content makes the task even more repetitive and time consuming. Thus there is a strong temptation to defer to the GenAI content, simply to save time and effort.

Without more transparency, affected students and parents will tend to assume the worst: that the teacher is merely fronting for the AI model.

Any professional benefit accruing to teachers from incorporating Generative AI into their assessment workflows is likely to be short-term. Letting GenAI contribute to student assessment will foreseeably become a justification for teacher workload creep and reduced staffing levels. Unless walled off (see WS-POL-N) the net effect would merely be a one-way ratchet of less human involvement in student assessment.

Staff Use of AI Detectors

Policy Recommendation:

The School Board should provisionally bar all use of AI Detectors in the school system, with the potential for this prohibition to be lifted after the Board:

Implements a framework for the resolution of positive AI-detection results. This framework should:

Explain what sorts of evidence are appropriate to confirm or rebut AI detection results

Explain where the burden of proof lies in such circumstances

Adequately suppress confirmation bias (i.e., the teacher’s natural tendency to seek out evidence that will confirm what they have already been led to believe by the AI model)

Adequately mitigate the psychological and reputational harm to students who are falsely flagged by the AI detector.

Determines that the specific software to be used:

Is accompanied by raw training and validation data, sufficient for an independent audit of statistical performance and resistance to bias

Has been found, through independent audit:

To have a false positive rate below 0.1%

To be resistant to foreseeable efforts to disguise AI authorship

To be sufficiently free from bias affecting subgroups of the student population, such as non-native speakers of English and neurodivergent students.

Will be subject to annual re-audits so that ongoing performance can be monitored as Generative AI models evolve

Handles essay data in a manner that complies with student privacy rights and all other student rights pertaining to the use of student creative content.

Rationale:

The potential for AI detectors to have unacceptably high false positive rates, to be evadable by technology-savvy users, to be biased against non-native English speakers, and to provoke confirmation bias against accused students, has been raised by numerous observers in the last several years. While we are not in a position to definitively resolve these technical and ethical concerns, they are sufficiently weighty to justify a precautionary approach. Under this approach, AI detectors are specifically excluded until such time as satisfactory answers to the concerns raised have been reviewed and approved by the Board. This recommendation is furthermore aligned with the IB Academic Integrity Policy:

“The IB aims to avoid joining the “arms race” between AI tools that claims to be able to tell the difference between AI and human authors. It is better to ensure that students can speak about their work with their teachers rather than accusing them of misconduct based on increasing small differences in the predictability of their word choices.”

The proposed false positive rate (0.1%) is an order of magnitude below what is commonly promised by the current generation of AI detectors. This approach is intended to reasonably address the risk that students will be subjected to false AI detection results at any point in their school career. Supposing that the false positive rate is merely 1% per essay, that each essay is an independent risk, and that students write 30 substantial essays over the course of their schooling, that would mean that about a quarter of students would have to confront a false accusation from an AI detector at some point in their school careers. Demanding an additional order of magnitude at least gets this fraction to less than 3% of students. In any event, note that ascertaining the actual false positive rate of detectors, as applied in City schools, will be difficult because it depends in part on estimating the baseline prevalence of Generative AI use among students who know that their work is being screened.

Furthermore, in many cases teachers will opt to allow certain bounded use of Generative AI tools by students (e.g., to suggest edits to an essay initial drafted by the student). It is not clear to that AI detectors would currently be able to reliably diagnose whether any detected Generative AI use was in accordance with teachers’ allowed parameters. If not, then a detector would only be useful in the narrow subset of cases where absolutely no Generative AI is permitted in a students’ work. This will be difficult standard for students to achieve in a world where every-day writing tools incorporate Generative AI. For such scenarios, a proctored assessment environment with either pen-and-paper or a locked-down assessment word-processor is a more practical solution than an un-proctored assessment followed by an AI detector.
