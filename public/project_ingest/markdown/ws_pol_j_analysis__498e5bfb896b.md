WS-POL-J – Students with Disabilities and Human Decision Safeguards Analysis

The strongest overall conclusion is that WS-POL-J is one of the committee’s strongest human-safeguard sections. The record shows broad agreement that IEP/504 and other required determinations must remain human-led, that AI may be assistive at most, and that uses affecting students with disabilities require stronger review for accessibility, bias, documentation, and accountability. The main disagreements are narrower: how much assistive AI use to permit, whether informed consent or advance disclosure should be required for some uses, and how explicit the policy should be about accommodations, accessibility review, and ongoing disparate-impact monitoring.

# Core conclusion

The public record supports four high-confidence conclusions.

First, the committee’s center of gravity is clearly that eligibility, placement, discipline-linked determinations, and other legally consequential decisions cannot be delegated to AI. That principle appears in the V3 outline as Decision 7, where discipline, IEP/504, and other required determinations remain human-led; AI may assist but not substitute; and any use must be documented, supervised, and constrained.

Second, the committee appears to agree that AI uses affecting students with disabilities should be reviewed not only for privacy and bias, but also for accessibility and accommodation conflict. The Equity & Access deliverable explicitly asks whether a use conflicts with accommodations or assistive technology, adds barriers for sensory/attention/motor needs, or creates inequity for multilingual learners. (D-EQA-1)

Third, the record supports a strong expectation that tools affecting protected groups or consequential educational outcomes require stronger governance review, not ordinary classroom discretion alone. The D-DPS workstream does this through tiered review and special handling for consequential uses and sensitive records.

Fourth, the record shows less disagreement about the human-led principle itself than about the boundary between assistive use and impermissible substitution.

# How the committee got here

This section grows out of the committee’s broader move from general values to guardrails for consequential decisions. By the time the V3 outline was posted, the committee had already separated:

staff and teacher use,

student use,

privacy and procurement governance,

and, in WS-POL-J, students with disabilities and human decision safeguards.
That placement matters. It suggests the committee understood disability-related AI use as more than a subtopic of equity or privacy. It is a distinct policy area requiring explicit safeguards.

The Meeting 4 readiness snapshot reinforces that reading. It shows D-EQA-1 and D-DPS-1 as active deliverables informing the recommendation outline, while D-CCI-1 and D-IPC-1 were still not started in the public snapshot. For WS-POL-J, that means the strongest documentary base comes from equity/access, privacy/security/governance, the outline, and the survey, rather than from communications or implementation planning. (Meeting 4 readiness)

# Where agreement appears strongest

## 1. Human-led decisions are the baseline

The clearest shared ground is the human-led rule. The V3 outline says:

IEP/504 and related determinations remain human-led,

AI may assist but not substitute for qualified personnel,

tools affecting protected groups require pre-adoption bias and accessibility review,

ongoing monitoring for disparate impact,

and accessibility and accommodation requirements must be considered in any student-facing AI use.

The survey supports that strongly. The prompt “IEP/504 decisions stay human” was widely approved or only lightly revised. Even many respondents with different overall postures accepted the core principle that eligibility, placement, or required determinations should not be delegated to AI. (Survey export)

## 2. Accessibility and accommodation conflict must be reviewed explicitly

The strongest workstream evidence here comes from D-EQA-1. Its rapid triage asks whether a proposed use:

conflicts with accommodations or assistive tech,

adds barriers for sensory/attention/motor needs,

penalizes translation/paraphrase supports,

or creates inequity for students developing English.
Its deep-dive prompts then ask which accommodations apply, how a tool might undermine approved supports, and what guidance or professional development is required for consistent implementation. (D-EQA-1)

That is a strong sign that the committee did not view “students with disabilities” mainly through a legal-compliance lens alone. It also viewed the issue as one of practical instructional fit and assistive-technology compatibility.

## 3. Consequential uses require stronger governance

The D-DPS draft aligns closely with WS-POL-J even though it is a privacy/governance workstream. It treats consequential uses, identity-linked uses, and sensitive records as escalated cases requiring stronger review, and it crosswalks those decisions to FERPA, IDEA, Section 504/ADA, COPPA, and vendor/data-protection requirements. That means the committee was not relying only on educator judgment for these contexts. It was building a governance layer around them.

## 4. Bias and disparate-impact monitoring are part of the safeguard model

The V3 outline explicitly calls for tools affecting protected groups to receive pre-adoption bias and accessibility review and ongoing monitoring for disparate impact. D-EQA-1 operationalizes that through its risk ratings, mitigation actions, and equity metrics, including subgroup disaggregation and tracking conflicts with IEP/504 supports. (D-EQA-1)

# Main fault lines and the range of opinions

## 1. Assistive use allowed under supervision vs. much narrower use

This is the biggest substantive split.

The outline position is moderate: AI may assist but not substitute; uses must be supervised, documented, and constrained. That leaves room for some assistive functions.

Several survey responses support that middle position. One respondent approved language that AI may support documentation or analysis, but not required determinations. Another proposed wording such as: FCCPS will not delegate student assessments or decisions as to eligibility, placement, or progress to AI; any AI use must be assistive, supervised, and documented. That is a classic guardrailed-assistance posture. (Survey export)

A stricter position is also visible. One respondent proposed that FCCPS may not use any generative AI to assist with the composition of documents bearing on discipline or disability accommodations unless use is previously disclosed and informed consent is provided by all affected parties. Another objected that earlier human-written language had been softened into “AI is fine to use, just document it,” and tried to restore a more restrictive anti-AI intent around IEPs and related determinations. That is not a total rejection of all AI, but it is much closer to a consent-first / use-minimization posture. (Survey export)

2. Documentation and disclosure vs. informed consent

The committee seems broadly comfortable with documentation. The outline itself requires that AI use in these contexts be documented, supervised, and constrained.

But there is meaningful disagreement over whether documentation is enough. Some respondents appear satisfied with documentation plus human leadership. Others argue that where disability accommodations, medical-style notes, discipline, or eligibility are involved, the district should go further and require informed consent from affected parties before using AI assistance at all.

This is a genuine philosophical difference:

one side sees documentation and governance as sufficient safeguards,

the other sees these contexts as sensitive enough to require something closer to affirmative permission.

## 3. Policy-level rule vs. implementation-level mechanism

The public record suggests fairly strong agreement on the principle, but less clarity on the operational mechanics.

The V3 outline states the policy-level rule clearly. D-EQA-1 then asks whether mitigations should live in policy, guidance, PD, or tool governance. D-DPS similarly crosswalks decisions into procurement, approval, legal review, and retention rules. This suggests the committee may agree on the “human-led” standard while still differing on how much of the implementation detail should appear directly in policy text versus in procedures and checklists. (D-EQA-1)

## 4. Disability-specific safeguards vs. broader protected-groups framing

The outline frames this section partly through students with disabilities and partly through tools affecting protected groups. That is important. It suggests some members may prefer a narrower disability/IEP/504 focus, while others favor a broader civil-rights lens that includes disability, language, and other protected-group risks.

D-EQA-1 strongly supports the broader lens by pairing disability/accommodation review with ELL, access, bias, and enforcement-fairness questions. That may make the section more powerful, but it can also broaden it beyond what some members think WS-POL-J should cover.

# Best characterization of the range of opinions

The accessible record suggests three main positions.

A guardrailed human-led position appears to be the committee center: IEP/504 and similar determinations remain human-led; AI may assist in limited ways; all such use must be supervised, documented, and constrained; accessibility and bias review are required.

A stricter consent-first position accepts the human-led principle but is much less comfortable with any AI use touching disability accommodations, discipline, or eligibility, and may require prior disclosure and informed consent.

A governance-heavy implementation position emphasizes that the principle is not enough by itself; approval tiers, legal crosswalks, procurement controls, documentation, accessibility testing, and equity monitoring are necessary to make the safeguard real. This is the posture most visible in D-DPS and D-EQA. (D-EQA-1)

# What this means for your drafting

For WS-POL-J, the strongest committee-grounded common ground is:

required determinations affecting students with disabilities should remain human-led,

AI may be assistive, not substitutive,

any such use should be supervised, documented, and constrained,

accessibility/accommodation conflicts should be reviewed explicitly,

bias and disparate-impact review should occur before adoption and over time,

and governance for these tools should be tighter than for ordinary classroom experimentation.

Where you will need to choose your own stance is on:

whether to preserve a relatively broad assistive-use pathway,

whether to endorse stronger disclosure or informed-consent language,

and how prominently to frame the section as disability-specific versus part of a broader protected-groups / civil-rights safeguard model.

The next clean step is a WS-POL-J chair preface, followed by your member contribution for the section.
