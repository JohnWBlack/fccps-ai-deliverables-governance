Preface

# 0.1 Advisory Framing and Use of This Document

This report is intended to provide structured advisory input to the Falls Church City Public Schools School Board as it considers policy direction for artificial intelligence in FCCPS. It identifies areas of committee alignment, key policy tradeoffs, and areas of meaningful disagreement that the Board should be able to see clearly rather than infer indirectly.

This document is not a final policy and should not be read as a fully negotiated committee consensus text. In some sections, the committee reached broad alignment on values, guardrails, or overall posture. In other sections, members advanced different policy approaches that reflect legitimate differences in judgment about learning, safety, equity, privacy, trust, implementation burden, and long-term student preparation. Where those differences remained meaningful, this report preserves them.

To support readability, many WS-POL sections begin with a chair preface that summarizes common ground, characterizes the range of positions, and identifies the central tradeoff or unresolved question. Those prefatory paragraphs are followed by member contributions, which are presented as distinct committee viewpoints rather than merged into a single artificial compromise.

# 0.2 Purpose and Applicability

The purpose of this report is to support the development of FCCPS guidance and policy for the responsible use of artificial intelligence in educational and operational contexts. The report addresses student use, staff and teacher use, privacy and data governance, academic integrity, public accountability, equity, implementation, and the ongoing reassessment of policy as technology changes.

The report is intended to apply, where relevant, to students, staff, contractors, vendors, and FCCPS systems or practices that materially affect learning, assessment, student rights, student well-being, privacy, equity, or district governance. While much of the committee’s work focused on generative AI because of its immediacy and visibility, the report is also meant to inform FCCPS responses to AI-enabled systems more broadly where those systems create similar risks or governance needs.

# 0.3 Relationship to Existing FCCPS Policy and Regulation

This report should be interpreted alongside existing FCCPS policies, regulations, and legal obligations. It is not intended to replace those frameworks wholesale. In many areas, existing rules already govern the underlying conduct or responsibility. What changes in an AI context is often the scale, ambiguity, evidentiary condition, implementation burden, or difficulty of preserving human accountability.

Accordingly, one purpose of this report is to identify where existing policy is already sufficient, where clarification may be needed, and where additional AI-specific guidance, regulation, approval criteria, or operational safeguards may be appropriate. The committee expects that some recommendations in this report would ultimately be implemented not only through Board policy, but also through administrative guidance, procurement controls, staff training, communications, and review procedures.

# 0.4 Policy vs. Implementation Boundary

The committee emphasizes the importance of distinguishing between policy-level guidance and implementation-level decisions. Policy should articulate what should be true: the values FCCPS intends to uphold, the boundaries it intends to set, the protections it intends to guarantee, and the principles that should guide later decisions. Implementation concerns how those commitments are carried out in practice through tools, workflows, approvals, communications, training, and oversight.

That distinction matters because AI is evolving quickly, school contexts differ, and some operational details will need to change as evidence, tools, and risks change. Overly specific operational requirements in Board-facing policy may reduce flexibility, create avoidable burden, or prematurely lock FCCPS into administrative choices that are better handled through guidance and regulation. At the same time, implementation cannot be treated as an afterthought; policy commitments should be realistic enough that the district can support, monitor, and govern them in practice.

# 0.5 Documenting Dissent

Where significant differences in perspective exist, those differences should be preserved explicitly rather than harmonized away. The committee does not regard dissent as a defect in the process. On many questions, disagreement reflects real policy tradeoffs under uncertainty rather than a failure to care about student learning, fairness, safety, or accountability.

Accordingly, this report includes multiple contributions in several sections. In some cases, those contributions reflect different default postures, different judgments about evidence and risk, or different views about how much detail belongs in policy as opposed to guidance. Preserving those differences gives the Board a more accurate understanding of the committee’s work and helps distinguish shared principles from unresolved policy choices.

# 0.6 How to Read This Document

This report is organized in two main parts.

The first part, WS-POL, provides the main policy shell. These sections are organized around the major questions the Board will need to consider, including guiding principles, adoption posture, scope, definitions, staff use, student use, disclosure, privacy and governance, equity, workload, and policy updating. In many sections, the chair preface provides a concise synthesis of broad agreement and the range of positions, followed by member contributions that present fuller arguments or proposed language.

The second part contains the workstream sections. These materials provide the cross-cutting foundation for the policy shell: research and shared baseline assumptions, privacy and security analysis, academic integrity logic, classroom scenarios, communications considerations, equity review, and implementation issues. These workstream materials are not all written in the same style or at the same level of completion, but together they provide much of the committee’s evidentiary basis, supporting logic, and design context.

Readers may find it useful to read the document in two passes: first, the WS-POL section to understand the policy architecture and major tradeoffs; second, the workstream sections to review the supporting analysis, examples, and implementation implications that informed the policy drafting.

WS-POL-A — Guiding Principles and Motivation

WS-POL-A – Guiding Principles and Motivation – Chair Preface

Areas of Broad Agreement on Values and Policy Goals

This section addresses the core values, educational aims, and policy motivations that should guide FCCPS AI policy as a whole. The Committee broadly agrees that FCCPS should adopt an AI policy that is evidence-informed, developmentally appropriate, human-centered, transparent, equity-aware, and capable of evolving over time. The Committee also broadly agrees that policy should remain aligned with FCCPS educational goals and IB values, that student learning and human judgment must remain primary, and that the district must respond to both the risks and the practical reality of AI’s growing presence in school and outside school contexts.

Main Differences in Overall Policy Posture

Within that shared foundation, members differ in how they frame the district’s starting point. One position places greater emphasis on uncertainty, visible risks to learning and integrity, and the need for a more restrictive or highly cautious posture unless educational benefit is clearly demonstrated. Another shares those concerns but places greater weight on structured, design-first integration, arguing that FCCPS should not leave AI fluency, judgment, and responsible use to outside access and informal experimentation. Members also differ on how strongly this section should foreground strategic preparation, workforce relevance, and inequities in outside access, as opposed to emphasizing protection of foundational human capacities from the outset.

What the Board Should Understand About the Remaining Disagreement

The Committee agrees that FCCPS should neither normalize AI uncritically nor attempt to ignore it. The remaining disagreement is mainly about framing and emphasis: whether the policy should present AI chiefly as a source of risk requiring narrow and cautious accommodation, or as a durable new condition that requires guarded but affirmative preparation within FCCPS. That framing choice matters because it will shape how the rest of the policy is interpreted.

WS-POL-A – Guiding Principles and Motivation – John Black

FCCPS should adopt an AI policy that is protective without being purely prohibitory. Current evidence does not support unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. Recent research raises credible concerns that AI can undermine students’ learning, cognition, and development when overused or poorly governed (Bastani et al., 2024; Burns et al., 2026, p. 12; Kosmyna et al., 2025). Those concerns should be taken seriously.

At the same time, FCCPS cannot responsibly assume that school-only non-engagement is an adequate educational strategy. AI tools are already widely available outside school, but access to those tools, adult guidance, and opportunities for practice is uneven; school policy should therefore consider whether it mitigates or unintentionally widens differences in AI literacy, confidence, and productive use (Beckman et al., 2025). For that reason, FCCPS should regulate AI from a design-first posture: begin with learning goals, developmental needs, human-accountability requirements, and equity concerns; design tasks, workflows, and safeguards accordingly; and then impose targeted restrictions where those goals cannot otherwise be protected (Burns et al., 2026, pp. 13–14, 19).

# Virtues We Are Trying to Capture

FCCPS policy should be evidence-informed but built for uncertainty. The district should be guided by evidence where evidence exists and proceed cautiously where it does not. The district should avoid both technological enthusiasm untethered from outcomes and a standard of proof so absolute that it prevents bounded learning, pilot-based governance, and policy adaptation.

FCCPS policy should be IB-aligned and learning-goal-first. AI should be introduced, limited, or withheld according to whether it supports the knowledge, habits, and human capacities FCCPS seeks to cultivate. School is not merely a workplace pipeline. It is a protected environment in which students develop reasoning, authorship, collaboration, and self-trust. In that sense, AI literacy includes learning when not to use AI and when human effort is educationally indispensable (Arlington Public Schools, 2025; International Baccalaureate, 2023).

FCCPS policy should be tailored to different grade bands and developmental stages. Younger students require stronger protection of foundational cognitive, social, and interpersonal development. Older students should gradually receive more guided exposure, more responsibility, and clearer expectations around disclosure, evaluation, and appropriate use. District policy should establish principles, while implementation should remain sensitive to age, subject, and task. See also the related grade-banded treatment in the WS-POL-H contribution by Adriana Palacios and the WS-POL-F contribution by David Berol.

FCCPS policy should be human-centered in design and implementation. Teachers should remain responsible for instruction and evaluation, students should remain responsible for their own learning, and AI should support rather than replace human reasoning and professional judgment. This principle is especially important in assessment, discipline, IEP/504-related processes, and other contexts where fairness and legitimacy depend on genuine human accountability. See also the related discussion in the WS-POL-E contribution by David Berol on staff and teacher use.

FCCPS policy should be transparent to students, families, and staff. Students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditionally allowed, or restricted. Families should receive accessible explanations of what kinds of AI uses occur in FCCPS and what safeguards apply. Staff should understand the difference between approved assistance and impermissible delegation. Transparency is a condition of trust, consistency, and fair enforcement. See also the WS-POL-H contribution by Adriana Palacios.

FCCPS policy should be equity-aware from procurement through implementation. AI can widen inequality by giving disproportionate advantage to those with outside access and fluency, while also exposing vulnerable students to weaker guardrails or lower-quality automation. At the same time, withholding structured opportunities to build AI literacy may itself produce inequitable outcomes. FCCPS should therefore treat equity as a full-lifecycle obligation spanning procurement, access, accommodations, instructional use, assessment, family communication, monitoring, and review. See also the WS-POL-M contribution by Adriana Palacios.

Finally, FCCPS policy should be flexible and evolving. AI capabilities, interfaces, and risks are changing too quickly for static rules to remain sufficient for long. Stable values and decision rules belong in policy; more detailed use guidance, examples, and approval criteria should be maintained administratively so they can evolve as evidence, tools, and risks change. See also the WS-POL-O contribution by Adriana Palacios.

# Idealized Goals for Regulating GenAI in FCCPS

FCCPS should articulate a policy logic that includes both permission and restriction.

First, policy should explain how allowing some uses of GenAI can support AI literacy, responsible digital citizenship, selected learning objectives, and educator effectiveness when paired with human oversight and clear pedagogical purpose. Students and staff need guided opportunities to understand how these tools work, where they fail, what they are good for, and what they should not be trusted to do.

Second, policy should explain how restricting some uses protects independent reasoning, authorship, assessment validity, fairness, privacy, and student well-being. Some educational tasks should remain intentionally non-AI, not because FCCPS is anti-technology, but because the value of those tasks lies in the human thinking and judgment they require (Bastani et al., 2024; Burns et al., 2026, pp. 14, 19; Kosmyna et al., 2025).

These goals are not contradictory. A strong policy should state clearly when AI use serves learning and when it interferes with learning. FCCPS should neither normalize AI everywhere nor attempt to wall it off categorically. Instead, FCCPS should create conditions in which students learn when AI is useful, when it is misleading, when it is inappropriate, and when non-AI work is educationally essential.

# Challenges This Policy Must Address

FCCPS must govern under uncertainty. Risks of AI adoption are already visible, while benefits are often context-dependent and difficult to generalize. Capabilities may improve rapidly, but harms may also increase in scale and subtlety (Burns et al., 2026, p. 12).

FCCPS must also address enforcement reality. AI use already occurs outside school-controlled environments. In unproctored contexts, rules based primarily on prohibition or detection may be difficult to enforce consistently and may encourage concealment rather than honest engagement. Overly rigid rules may also generate unfairness, including false accusations and uneven burdens on teachers. See the related concerns in the WS-POL-E contribution by David Berol regarding detector reliability and due process, and the WS-POL-H contribution by Adriana Palacios regarding practical implementation and communication.

Finally, FCCPS must address a strategic challenge. The district is responsible not only for preventing harm, but also for preparing students for a changing world. Emerging education research suggests that students with higher AI literacy are better positioned to use and benefit from GenAI, and workplace evidence suggests that access to generative AI can help less-experienced users improve faster; FCCPS should therefore consider whether complete school non-engagement leaves students’ AI-related preparedness to outside access and informal experimentation (Beckman et al., 2025; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023).

# Decision 1 — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee broadly agrees that policy must protect student learning and assessment integrity. There are differing perspectives on the relative emphasis between prioritizing restrictions to mitigate risk and enabling thoughtful integration of AI to support learning outcomes. These perspectives reflect a meaningful policy tradeoff for Board consideration.

In my view, FCCPS should adopt a design-first posture. Under a design-first approach, the district begins by asking: What is the learning objective? What human capacities should this task develop? What developmental stage are the students in? What risks to fairness, privacy, trust, or well-being are present? What human review and accountability are required?

Only then should the district decide whether AI use should be permitted, structured, limited, or prohibited. In some settings, the right answer will be prohibition or tightly bounded use. In others, the better answer will be teacher-guided integration, clearer disclosure expectations, better assessment design, stronger approval criteria, or bounded pilots with close monitoring.

# Pragmatic Constraints on Restriction of GenAI Applications

FCCPS should recognize that restriction has practical limits. In unproctored settings, full enforcement of GenAI restrictions is often unrealistic. Some summative assessments can be moved into proctored environments, but not all, and doing so carries instructional and operational costs. AI is also increasingly embedded in common productivity tools, making bright-line enforcement more difficult over time.

FCCPS should also recognize that fairness matters on both sides of policy. Students should not be expected to compete against widespread, non-transparent AI use by peers, but they also should not be subjected to unreliable accusations or opaque machine-mediated judgments. The district should therefore prefer control strategies that improve validity by design: clearer disclosure rules, better assessment design, more opportunities for demonstrated understanding in controlled settings, and governance mechanisms that reduce dependence on after-the-fact suspicion. See also the related discussion in the WS-POL-H contribution by David Berol on use categories and control strategies.

# Decision 2

FCCPS should steer between two extremes: unchecked adoption and impractical overrestriction.

That middle course should not be understood as compromise for its own sake. It should be understood as disciplined governance. FCCPS should restrict AI where learning, fairness, privacy, safety, or human dignity require it. FCCPS should also create structured pathways for responsible use where doing so advances educational goals, builds student and staff capability, and reduces the likelihood that opportunity will be determined by unequal access to AI fluency outside school.

In this sense, thoughtful integration is not the opposite of protection. Done well, it is part of how protection, preparedness, and equity are achieved together.

WS-POL-A – Guiding Principles, Motivation, and Challenges – Thomas Colvin

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

How and when students learn to use and understand AI as a foundational tool for future study and work

how and when our schools integrate AI into the design and delivery of education to enhance teaching and support to educators

personalized learning and the overall student experience

how to capture the benefits that AI may create and how to mitigate the risks and community concerns posed by AI

# Virtues we are trying to capture

Evidence-informed. The policy should clearly articulate the human skills, competencies, and other outcomes it prioritizes. Recommendations should be clearly connected to these outcomes and supported by evidence. The strength of recommendations should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate.

Alignment with IB values. The policy should align with the values and learning goals for the IB program, which is designed to build the following five skills:

Thinking (critical, creative, and ethical)

Research (comparing, contrasting, validating and prioritizing information)

Communication (written and oral, effective listening, and formulating arguments)

Social (forming and maintaining positive relationships, and conflict resolution)

Self-management (organizational skills, such as managing time and tasks, and affective skills, such as managing state of mind and motivation)

Age-appropriate. The policy should account for differences in learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age.

Integrated with our community. The policy should ensure that students, parents, and educators receive all relevant information promptly and transparently. Further, their feedback should be gathered and prioritized for assessments of new AI-policy actions. The policy created by the school system should be revised frequently, because AI technologies and community experiences with them are evolving rapidly.

Risk mitigation. Community members and Ai experts can precisely demonstrate the risks of AI in K-12 education more easily than they can demonstrate the potential benefits. The policy should directly address potential risks of adopting AI in the FCCPS school system and ensure that sufficient mitigations are in place. Potential risks include, but are not limited to, academic integrity, data privacy, and inequitable access.

# Challenges this policy must address

The benefits of using AI in K-12 education are uncertain. If the benefits were clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions to take would be clear. In reality, some schools have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI, while the broader literature contains many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users.

The use of AI introduces risks. AI exacerbates existing risks related to academic integrity by allowing the creation of long-form text. It also introduces new risks, such as easily creating media with content that is harmful to students or teachers, preventing kids from seeking human mental health support, or student inputs getting leaked by being used for training purposes. Whether adequate safeguards can be put in place remains is an open question.

The future quality of AI tools is uncertain. The AI community generally believes that their tools are improving and will continue to improve. However, “platform decay” is a documented trend for software systems to get worse over time as companies reduce service costs and quality to maximize profit. It is impossible to know whether specific tools the school adopts will improve or decay in the future.

Access to AI is ubiquitous and its use is not reliably detectable. Students and educators will have access to AI regardless of school policy. This means that those who choose to adopt the technology may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those that do not adopt AI; this applies to both students and teachers alike. Likewise, educators may use the technology against the wishes of students and parents who do not want their work run through an AI system. It is generally not possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI, making any rule or policy potentially difficult to enforce.

WS-POL-B — Adoption vs. Restriction Posture

WS-POL-B – Adoption vs. Restriction Posture – Chair Preface

Shared Premises About Protection, Preparation, and Enforceability
This section addresses how FCCPS should position itself between broader adoption and stronger restriction in responding to AI use in educational settings. The Committee broadly agrees that FCCPS must protect student learning, assessment integrity, privacy, and human judgment, and that current evidence does not justify careless or unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. The Committee also broadly agrees that AI tools are already widely accessible outside school, that total suppression is unrealistic, and that FCCPS must therefore address not only risk mitigation but also student and staff preparation for a changing environment.

Competing Default Postures for AI Use in FCCPS
Within that shared foundation, members differ on the district’s default posture. One position gives priority to mitigating harms from currently available tools and treats broader FCCPS-sponsored adoption as secondary until benefits are clearer and risks are better controlled. Another favors a guardrailed, design-first posture in which FCCPS begins with learning goals, developmental needs, and accountability requirements, then determines where AI use should be prohibited, limited, or affirmatively supported. A third, narrower position places still greater weight on guided integration and school-governed opportunities for responsible use, emphasizing that students should learn to use AI critically and transparently rather than encounter it only as a prohibited tool.

Core Tradeoff for Board Decision
The Committee agrees that FCCPS should neither normalize AI use without guardrails nor rely on unenforceable restrictions as its only response. The remaining disagreement is about default posture: whether policy should begin from stronger caution and limited adoption until benefits are demonstrated, or from a more design-first model that creates bounded opportunities for use wherever educational value, developmental appropriateness, and human accountability can be protected.

WS-POL-B – Adoption vs. Restriction Posture – John Black

# Decision 1 — Committee perspective (no full consensus)

The Committee is correct that this section presents a genuine policy tradeoff and not merely a disagreement over emphasis. I agree that FCCPS policy must protect student learning and assessment integrity, and I also agree that current evidence does not justify careless or unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. Recent research and policy guidance support caution: Brookings concluded that, at this stage, the risks of generative AI in children’s education outweigh its benefits, and Virginia’s statewide guidance stresses both “do no harm” and “augment, not replace humans.” At the same time, neither source supports a purely prohibition-based posture. Brookings also argues for holistic AI literacy and well-designed tools that teach rather than tell, while Virginia’s guidance explicitly calls for innovation, experimentation, and equitable access alongside guardrails. In my view, those sources point toward a design-first posture, not a restriction-first default. FCCPS should begin with learning goals, developmental needs, and human-accountability requirements, then decide where AI use should be permitted, structured, or restricted. (Brookings)

A restriction-first posture is understandable, and I take its concerns seriously. The strongest case for it is that harms independent thinking, assessment validity, and student self-trust are already visible, while benefits remain uneven and context dependent. I share those concerns. But I do not believe they justify making broader adoption a secondary priority until benefits are proven in advance across all use cases. The more realistic and educationally responsible approach is to distinguish between contexts. One view gives priority to mitigating harms from currently available tools and treats additional FCCPS-sponsored adoption as secondary until benefits are clearer. Another view favors creating bounded, school-governed opportunities for use where learning goals, accountability, and safeguards are explicit. (Burns et al., 2026, p. 124-126)

My position, therefore, is that FCCPS should adopt a design-first posture. That means protecting assessment validity primarily through assessment design, human review, process expectations, and instructional clarity, while using restrictions where those measures are insufficient. It also means recognizing that AI literacy includes learning when not to use AI. A design-first posture is not permissive by default. It is more demanding than either uncritical adoption or blanket prohibition, because it requires FCCPS to be explicit about educational purpose, developmental appropriateness, and accountability.

## Pragmatic constraints on restriction of GenAI applications

Any serious policy in this area must begin with enforcement reality. AI use is already occurring outside school-controlled environments, and in unproctored contexts FCCPS cannot rely on prohibition alone to create fair or consistent outcomes. Policies that depend mainly on detection or blanket bans are likely to be unevenly enforced and may create incentives for concealment rather than honesty. The Committee’s own Meeting 4 packet reflects this reality by treating the central validity question as a choice between “design/process evidence first” and “restriction-first in high-stakes,” while also advancing a risk rubric, a guidance library, advisory-only detection, and guardrailed drafting as surrounding governance tools. That combination suggests that differentiated design and governance are necessary even for those who favor stronger restrictions in some settings.

There are also practical limits on how much summative work can be moved into proctored settings. Proctored environments are sometimes the right answer and should be used more intentionally for tasks where unaided performance is central to the learning objective. But they are not a complete answer. Overreliance on proctoring can narrow assignment design, increase workload, and shift rather than solve the underlying challenge of how students learn in an AI-rich world. FCCPS should therefore use proctoring selectively, where it is instructionally justified, while also redesigning assignments so that prohibited AI uses are less educationally useful and less attractive. That is a more sustainable response than trying to police every possible misuse after the fact.

Trust is another practical constraint. FCCPS should protect students from two unfair conditions at once: competing against rampant non-transparent AI use by peers and being falsely accused based on weak or opaque evidence. The Committee has already recognized broad concern about detector reliability and fairness. I agree that detectors should not be treated as primary enforcement tools and should never serve as sole evidence. A policy posture built too heavily on suspicion risks harming the student-teacher relationship that the Committee has repeatedly identified as central to learning. Design-first policy is not trust without verification; it is trust supported by clearer expectations, better assessment design, proportionate disclosure, and human review.

Restriction-first policy also has an equity problem if it is not accompanied by meaningful in-school opportunities for guided AI literacy. Recent reporting on Anthropic data suggests that the gap may not be simply between AI users and non-users, but between experienced users and newcomers. If that pattern holds, then students with more access, practice, and adult support outside school will continue building fluency even if FCCPS adopts a more restrictive posture inside school. In that environment, a school-only restriction strategy can unintentionally widen differences in preparation rather than narrow them. That does not mean FCCPS should rush into broad adoption. It does mean the district should not assume that less in-school exposure is necessarily the safer or more equitable long-term choice.

# Decision 2

For these reasons, I support the Committee’s conclusion that FCCPS should steer between two extremes: unchecked adoption and impractical overrestriction. I would state that posture more affirmatively as follows: FCCPS should adopt a guardrailed, design-first approach to AI integration. Under this approach, restrictions remain necessary in higher-risk contexts, especially where independent reasoning, authorship, fairness, privacy, or developmental protection are central. But those restrictions should be targeted, intelligible, and tied to clear educational purposes. Outside those contexts, FCCPS should create structured opportunities for students and educators to build AI literacy and responsible habits under human-centered rules and with appropriate safeguards. (Virginia Department of Education)

In practical terms, that means FCCPS should prefer the following sequence. First, define the learning goal and determine whether the task is one in which unaided human performance is educationally essential. Second, design the assignment, assessment conditions, and disclosure expectations accordingly. Third, determine whether AI use in that context should be prohibited, conditionally allowed, or affirmatively supported. Fourth, review outcomes over time and revise policy where evidence shows either harm or benefit. This sequence is more faithful to the Committee’s values than beginning with a broad presumption either for or against use. It is also more consistent with the Committee’s repeated emphasis on human accountability, developmental appropriateness, transparency, equity, and adaptability under uncertainty.

My respectful disagreement with a restriction-first default is therefore not a disagreement about the seriousness of current risks. It is a disagreement about what posture is most likely to protect students while preparing them well. The better path is not to normalize AI use, and it is not to try to suppress it through rules that are difficult to enforce and easy to evade. The better path is to govern it deliberately: teach students where AI is useful, where it is unreliable, where it is inappropriate, and where human effort must remain primary. In that sense, thoughtful integration is not opposed to protection. It is part of how protection, preparation, and equity can be pursued together.

The Committee agrees that FCCPS needs a clear way to define what is in scope, what risks require differentiated treatment, and where AI-specific guidance is needed beyond existing policy. WS-POL-C – Scope, Triaging, and Policy Crosswalk – John Black

I do not think FCCPS should write an AI policy that is so narrow it governs only the most obvious current controversies, nor so broad that it becomes an unadministrable theory of all technology risk. The right approach is a broad but disciplined scope. FCCPS should write this policy to cover AI uses that materially affect student learning, assessment validity, student rights, student well-being, privacy, equity, or district governance. That means the policy should certainly address generative AI and detectors, because those are the most visible and immediate issues. But it should not stop there. It should also be capable of reaching AI-enabled systems, tools, and workflows used in FCCPS contexts when they change the practical risk environment enough that ordinary existing policy language is no longer sufficient. That is the key threshold: not whether a tool is branded “AI,” but whether AI changes scale, ambiguity, evidentiary conditions, or implementation burden in ways the Board should govern explicitly.

In my view, a GenAI-only frame is too narrow for the policy itself, even if GenAI remains the center of gravity in communications and examples. The district will confront not only chatbots and generators, but also AI-enabled screening, translation, personalization, workflow, monitoring, and decision-support systems. Some of those tools will not present as “ChatGPT for school,” yet may have greater consequences for students and staff than classroom prompting alone. FCCPS should therefore write the policy so that it applies more broadly to AI-enabled systems used in FCCPS contexts, while using guidance and examples to keep the document concrete and understandable. This lets the Board avoid writing a policy that is obsolete the moment the product category shifts, while still acknowledging that generative AI is the current focal point of community concern.

I also think this section should say plainly that academic integrity is not the only AI policy problem. Integrity matters, but it is not sufficient as the organizing scope of the policy. FCCPS should explicitly recognize non-academic AI risks where they materially affect the school environment, especially manipulative interaction, quasi-social dependence, harassment, synthetic media abuse, inappropriate content exposure, and contexts in which students may be nudged away from human support or judgment. I am not suggesting that this report attempt to solve every such issue in detail. I am saying the Board should be told that these risks belong within the district’s AI policy perimeter and should not be treated as accidental side effects outside the policy’s concern. If the committee omits them from Section C, the policy shell will understate the real problem FCCPS is trying to govern.

At the same time, I would not write this section as an invitation to policy sprawl. The policy should not attempt to prescribe a separate rule for every conceivable AI-enabled feature or classroom edge case. Instead, FCCPS should adopt a triage structure. In my judgment, the district needs a practical way to sort AI uses by risk, consequence, and context. The policy should establish that not all AI uses are alike and that different uses warrant different levels of scrutiny, restriction, disclosure, or approval. A low-risk assistive use that does not touch student records or displace core learning work should not be governed in the same way as an identity-linked system, a detector, a student-facing conversational tool, or an AI-supported process affecting discipline, placement, accommodations, or other consequential decisions.

I therefore support a triaging approach that asks at least four questions. First, what is the educational or operational function of the AI use? Second, what is the risk level, including privacy, fairness, developmental, and well-being risk? Third, who is affected and in what context, including grade band and vulnerability of the user population? Fourth, does the use alter a decision or workflow that already carries heightened legal, ethical, or trust implications? Those questions are more durable than a policy built around product labels alone. They allow FCCPS to remain flexible as technologies evolve while still giving administrators and educators a usable structure for judgment.

I would also distinguish here between policy-level triage and implementation-level triage. At the policy level, the Board should state that uses may be categorized and treated differently based on risk, consequence, and developmental appropriateness. At the implementation level, the district can then build the supporting matrices, tool-review criteria, examples, and approval pathways. That distinction matters because I do not think the Board-facing report should be overloaded with operational detail. But I do think the Board should be told that triage is a core design principle of the policy, not a back-office administrative preference.

On the relationship to existing FCCPS policy and regulation, I support an overlay model, not a replacement model. FCCPS does not need a single AI policy that tries to supersede academic integrity rules, student conduct standards, privacy practices, vendor review, bullying and harassment protections, discipline procedures, accessibility obligations, and staff guidance all at once. It needs an AI policy that works alongside those frameworks and clarifies where AI changes the problem. In some areas, existing policy may already govern the underlying conduct, and AI mainly changes evidence, scale, speed, or enforceability. In other areas, AI may introduce enough novelty or ambiguity that additional guidance is needed. Section C should say that explicitly and should recommend a formal appendix or crosswalk identifying where existing FCCPS policies already apply, where clarification is needed, and where future revision should be considered.

In practical terms, I would have the crosswalk do three things. First, identify existing policy areas that already govern conduct or obligations implicated by AI. Second, identify where AI creates a meaningful change in implementation burden, evidentiary uncertainty, or governance risk. Third, identify where AI-specific guidance, regulation, procurement standards, or administrative procedures need to be layered on top. That is a better approach than pretending the Board can resolve every operational dependency in one policy section. It is also more honest about the district’s actual policy architecture.

My own position, then, is that WS-POL-C should recommend a policy scope that is challenge-based, governance-aware, and future-resilient. FCCPS should not write this section as though the only live issue is whether students use ChatGPT on essays. Nor should it write the section so expansively that no one can tell what falls inside the policy. The right middle path is to say: the policy applies where AI materially affects learning, assessment, rights, well-being, privacy, equity, or governance; uses should be triaged according to risk and consequence; and AI-specific guidance should be cross-walked to existing FCCPS policy rather than treated as a freestanding replacement for the district’s broader rules.

That, in my view, is the most defensible way to write Section C. It is broad enough to govern the real problem, narrow enough to remain usable, and structured enough to support later guidance, procurement, communications, and implementation work without forcing the Board-facing document to do all of that work itself.

WS-POL-D – Definitions – David Berol

# Generative-AI

See the definition provided by the Brookings Institute Report on AI in the schools: “Generative AI, the focus of this report, refers to a class of technologies that generate novel outputs such as text, images, audio, code, or video in response to user prompts. These technologies include LLMs, diffusion models, and other neural network architectures designed for content generation. Generative AI is a form of machine learning that creates new content rather than simply analyzing or classifying existing data. These systems are typically trained on vast datasets composed of publicly available digital content—words, images, and sounds . . . . Contemporary models increasingly incorporate curated, domain specific, and proprietary data tailored to specific applications, including educational ones.”1

# AI Literacy

AI literacy comes from technical instruction about AI (e.g., training an AI model in computer science class) and from pragmatic instruction on the social, safety, and ethical issues involved in using AI.2   The core of AI literacy is understanding where AI use is and is not appropriate. Reasonable restrictions on AI use and encouraging AI literacy are complementary objectives. To the extent AI literacy is a call for students to use AI extensively in school, merely on the grounds that they will eventually need to use AI at work, note the following: When students graduate to the workforce, efficiency demands may very well oblige them to regularly use AI tools as a skill-multiplier. But school is fundamentally a place where students need to develop their innate skills, precisely so that they will have skills capable of being multiplied. It is essential to prioritize the development of students’ innate skills and school is a protected space for learning that need not always conform to the efficiency logic of the workplace.

# Constructive feedback

Constructive feedback is information about a student's work-in‑progress that is intended to guide revision and improvement prior to assessment. It focuses on how the work can be developed or corrected, typically by identifying strengths, weaknesses, and concrete possibilities for change, rather than by assigning a grade, score, or other evaluative verdict on the quality of the work at its current stage of development.

# Evaluative feedback

Evaluative feedback is an assessment of a student's work treated as a finished product that assigns a judgment of its quality as measured against specified criteria or standards. It typically takes the form of a grade, score, or evaluative judgment that summarizes how well the work meets those criteria or standard, accompanied by additional comments or remarks that serve to justify that evaluation.

# Assessment

The activity of reviewing a student's work for the purpose of providing that student with evaluative feedback.

# AI Detector

A software tool that uses statistical methods to predict the relative likelihood that a particular block of text was authored by a Generative AI model, typically considering factors such as whether word choice, syntax, and organizational structure are characteristic of Generative AI output. AI detectors are typically trained by exposure to a corpus of texts that were pre-labeled with known authorship status.

# Citation

Citation fulfils an ethical obligation to not steal ideas from the author’s sources. The author acknowledges the labor of those from whom they inherit the ideas they are working with. In addition, citation often serves to lend authority to claims made in the present and it maintains a traceable genealogy of where human knowledge comes from.

# Disclosure

By contrast, disclosure aims to fulfill an ethical obligation to the author’s teacher and peers. The point is to allow the teacher to understand what tasks the student off-loaded onto the Generative AI, whether GenAI use was consistent with the assignment guidelines, and thus to fairly assess student achievement. Without disclosure, students can only assume that all their peers are making liberal use of AI assistance. This engenders a demoralizing race-to-the-bottom in which all are ultimately deprived of the educational benefit of learning to read, write, and think for themselves.

In various circumstances, disclosure could consist of:

Informing the instructor that GenAI was somehow used on an assignment

Providing a log of all chat prompts to the GenAI

Providing the full chat log

Providing a narrative description of the general nature of GenAI reliance

WS-POL-D – Definitions – John Black

This contribution is intended to supplement the definitions already proposed elsewhere in this section by adding terms that help the rest of the policy operate clearly across instruction, assessment, privacy, governance, and implementation. The goal of this section should be to provide plain-language definitions that are usable in practice and durable enough to support later policy sections without preempting every substantive disagreement.

AI
Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computational systems that perform tasks commonly associated with human intelligence, such as generating language, classifying patterns, making predictions, recommending actions, or interacting conversationally. Because what counts as AI changes over time, FCCPS should avoid definitions that are too narrow or tied to one generation of tools.

Generative AI
Generative AI refers to AI systems that generate novel outputs, such as text, images, audio, video, code, or combinations of these, in response to prompts, context, or user interaction. For FCCPS purposes, generative AI is a distinct category because it raises particular issues of authorship, disclosure, overreliance, privacy, and trust.

Approved tool
An approved tool is an AI-enabled tool, application, platform, or feature that FCCPS has authorized for a specified use, user group, and risk context under its review and governance process. Approval should not be understood as blanket permission for all uses. It may be limited by grade band, purpose, data practices, or required safeguards.

High-stakes use
A high-stakes use is an AI-supported or AI-affected use case that may materially affect formal eligibility, placement, discipline, final evaluative judgment, access to services, accommodations, or another consequential determination that is difficult to reverse.

AI assistance
AI assistance means the use of an AI system to support, shape, revise, generate, recommend, evaluate, classify, summarize, translate, or otherwise influence a task, process, or work product. This definition should be broad enough to support later rules on disclosure, restrictions, and integrity, while allowing later sections or guidance to distinguish between minimal assistive features and more substantive AI contribution.

Meaningful AI assistance
Meaningful AI assistance is AI assistance that materially contributes to ideas, structure, analysis, language, reasoning, recommendations, or output in a way that would matter to fair evaluation, authorship, or decision-making. This term is useful because not every low-level automated feature should trigger the same disclosure or restriction requirements.

Human decision-maker
A human decision-maker is a qualified person who retains actual authority, responsibility, and accountability for a decision, judgment, or action, and who is expected to understand the basis for that decision rather than merely approve an AI-generated output. Human decision-making should mean meaningful review and contextual judgment, not passive ratification.

Disclosure
Disclosure is communication of relevant AI use to the appropriate audience so that expectations, authorship, fairness, and accountability can be understood. Depending on context, disclosure may be owed to a teacher, student, family, administrator, reviewer, or the district. Disclosure is broader than citation because it concerns the role AI played in producing work, informing a decision, or shaping an interaction.

Citation / attribution
Citation or attribution is the acknowledgment of human-authored or source-based material on which a person relied, consistent with the norms of the task, discipline, and grade band. Citation is primarily about intellectual honesty and traceability of sources. Disclosure and citation are related, but they are not identical and should not always be treated as interchangeable.

Process evidence
Process evidence is documentation or observable evidence that helps show how work was produced or how a decision was reached. Depending on context, this may include drafts, checkpoints, annotations, oral explanation, revision history, teacher observation, or other forms of visible thinking or workflow evidence.

Equivalent alternative
An equivalent alternative is a non-AI or differently structured pathway through which a student can meet the same learning goal, expectations, and evaluation standard without penalty when policy or context requires or permits such an alternative. Equivalent does not mean identical in format, but it should mean comparable in rigor and fairness.

Routine educational use
A routine educational use is a use that falls within ordinary, authorized instructional or operational practice and does not, by itself, expand data use, vendor rights, or student risk beyond what FCCPS has already reviewed and approved.

Vendor
A vendor is an outside company, organization, or provider that offers an AI-enabled product, platform, feature, or service used or proposed for use in FCCPS. This includes both stand-alone AI tools and AI features embedded within broader platforms.

Data processor
A data processor is a vendor or other outside entity that stores, transmits, analyzes, trains on, or otherwise processes data on behalf of FCCPS or in connection with FCCPS-related use. This definition should be interpreted functionally: if an outside entity touches the data in a way relevant to privacy, retention, model training, or governance, it should be treated as a data processor.

Notice
Notice is a plain-language communication by FCCPS that explains an AI-related use, practice, or safeguard to affected parties in advance or at an appropriate point of use. Notice may concern tool use, data practices, disclosure expectations, risk posture, or available alternatives.

Consent terms
Consent terms refers to the conditions under which permission, notice, opt-in, or opt-out expectations apply to a tool, workflow, or use case involving students, families, staff, or their data. Later sections should specify when notice alone is sufficient, when consent is required, and when an equivalent alternative should be available.

AI-integrated task
An AI-integrated task is a task for which policy or instruction explicitly permits, expects, or structures some defined form of AI use as part of the work. This term is helpful because later sections may need to distinguish between AI-optional tasks, AI-prohibited tasks, and tasks in which bounded AI use is intentionally built into the learning design.

AI-optional task
An AI-optional task is a task for which AI use is permitted but not required, and for which students should be able to complete the work successfully through an equivalent non-AI pathway unless the policy or instructional context clearly states otherwise.

Closing note
Definitions should reduce confusion, support consistency, and make later sections more operational. Where the committee still has substantive disagreement, the definitions section should clarify terms without pretending those disagreements have already been resolved.

This section addresses how FCCPS should govern staff and teacher use of AI in planning, instruction, feedback, evaluation, integrity-related review, and other consequential school functions. WS-POL-E – Staff and Teacher Use of AI – David Berol

# Staff Use of AI to Provide Feedback to Students

## Policy Recommendations:

For purposes of making policy relating to the permissible use of AI tools by teachers, it is important to distinguish constructive feedback from assessment and evaluative feedback.

Students typically acquire constructive feedback from a variety of sources. With appropriate guardrails (e.g., teacher approval, age-appropriateness) it is reasonable for a Gen AI tool to be one such source.

By contrast, assessment and evaluative feedback that purports to be from the teacher should not (in general) incorporate substantive GenAI content.

Substantive GenAI content might nonetheless be acceptable in teacher-authored assessments and evaluative feedback if:

The teacher adequately reviews the GenAI content before publishing under their own name;

The teacher obtains prior consent from the student’s parents;

The presence of substantive GenAI content is disclosed in the assessment/feedback; and

The teacher is able and willing to disclose, on request, evidence of the raw GenAI inputs and the extent to which they were modified before ratification by the teacher.

The Board should develop policies and regulations to distinguish substantive GenAI content from other applications of AI that do not raise the same issues. For example:

Image-recognition tools that convert student handwriting to text or that record multiple choice answers without the need for bubble sheets.

AI tools that merely fix spelling and grammar errors and make minor stylistic improvements to human-originated text.

## Rationale:

Most adults would be demoralized if their performance reviews were drafted in the first instance by AI, followed by an unknown degree of human editing. Students and parents should be treated with the same respect that one would want in one’s own workplace.

AI tools are touted as relieving extraneous drudgery and freeing humans to focus on their core responsibilities. But assessments are not extraneous drudgery.  They are a core responsibility of the teaching role that does not need to be off-loaded.

Precisely because they are authoritative, summative assessments and evaluative feedback need to be the teacher’s authentic work product. Injecting GenAI content into the workflow risks compromising (or at least appearing to compromise) the document’s authenticity. A teacher’s bare assertion that they reviewed the GenAI does not fix the problem. Here’s why:

GenAI content is designed to be persuasive and superficially appealing, even when it doesn’t really make sense.

Grading is already a repetitive and time-consuming task. Adequately reviewing GenAI content makes the task even more repetitive and time consuming. Thus there is a strong temptation to defer to the GenAI content, simply to save time and effort.

Without more transparency, affected students and parents will tend to assume the worst: that the teacher is merely fronting for the AI model.

Any professional benefit accruing to teachers from incorporating Generative AI into their assessment workflows is likely to be short-term. Letting GenAI contribute to student assessment will foreseeably become a justification for teacher workload creep and reduced staffing levels. Unless walled off (see WS-POL-N) the net effect would merely be a one-way ratchet of less human involvement in student assessment.

# Staff Use of AI Detectors

## Policy Recommendation

The School Board should provisionally bar all use of AI Detectors in the school system, with the potential for this prohibition to be lifted after the Board:

Implements a framework for the resolution of positive AI-detection results. This framework should:

Explain what sorts of evidence are appropriate to confirm or rebut AI detection results

Explain where the burden of proof lies in such circumstances

Adequately suppress confirmation bias (i.e., the teacher’s natural tendency to seek out evidence that will confirm what they have already been led to believe by the AI model)

Adequately mitigate the psychological and reputational harm to students who are falsely flagged by the AI detector.

Determines that the specific software to be used:

Is accompanied by raw training and validation data, sufficient for an independent audit of statistical performance and resistance to bias

Has been found, through independent audit:

To have a false positive rate below 0.1%

To be resistant to foreseeable efforts to disguise AI authorship

To be sufficiently free from bias affecting subgroups of the student population, such as non-native speakers of English and neurodivergent students.

Will be subject to annual re-audits so that ongoing performance can be monitored as Generative AI models evolve

Handles essay data in a manner that complies with student privacy rights and all other student rights pertaining to the use of student creative content.

## Rationale:

The potential for AI detectors to have unacceptably high false positive rates, to be evadable by technology-savvy users, to be biased against non-native English speakers, and to provoke confirmation bias against accused students, has been raised by numerous observers in the last several years.1 While we are not in a position to definitively resolve these technical and ethical concerns, they are sufficiently weighty to justify a precautionary approach. Under this approach, AI detectors are specifically excluded until such time as satisfactory answers to the concerns raised have been reviewed and approved by the Board. This recommendation is furthermore aligned with the IB Academic Integrity Policy:

“The IB aims to avoid joining the “arms race” between AI tools that claims to be able to tell the difference between AI and human authors. It is better to ensure that students can speak about their work with their teachers rather than accusing them of misconduct based on increasing small differences in the predictability of their word choices.”2

The proposed false positive rate (0.1%) is an order of magnitude below what is commonly promised by the current generation of AI detectors. This approach is intended to reasonably address the risk that students will be subjected to false AI detection results at any point in their school career. Supposing that the false positive rate is merely 1% per essay, that each essay is an independent risk, and that students write 30 substantial essays over the course of their schooling, that would mean that about a quarter of students would have to confront a false accusation from an AI detector at some point in their school careers. Demanding an additional order of magnitude at least gets this fraction to less than 3% of students.3 In any event, note that ascertaining the actual false positive rate of detectors, as applied in City schools, will be difficult because it depends in part on estimating the baseline prevalence of Generative AI use among students who know that their work is being screened.4

Furthermore, in many cases teachers will opt to allow certain bounded use of Generative AI tools by students (e.g., to suggest edits to an essay initial drafted by the student). It is not clear to that AI detectors would currently be able to reliably diagnose whether any detected Generative AI use was in accordance with teachers’ allowed parameters.5 If not, then a detector would only be useful in the narrow subset of cases where absolutely no Generative AI is permitted in a students’ work. This will be difficult standard for students to achieve in a world where every-day writing tools incorporate Generative AI. For such scenarios, a proctored assessment environment with either pen-and-paper or a locked-down assessment word-processor is a more practical solution than an un-proctored assessment followed by an AI detector.

WS-POL-F – Student Use – PreK-5 – F Chair Preface

# Broad Agreement on Strong Caution for Younger Students

This section reflects one of the Committee’s strongest areas of shared caution. Members broadly agree that AI use for younger students must be developmentally appropriate, that foundational cognitive, social, and relational learning should remain primary, and that routine direct student use in PreK–5 should be limited and educator-mediated. The Committee also broadly agrees that FCCPS should emphasize AI literacy in this band while maintaining stronger boundaries around conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating AI uses that may blur the distinction between human and machine interaction or encourage younger students to treat AI as a buddy, coach, co-teacher, or authority. Meeting 5 reinforced this general posture by emphasizing more restricted exposure for younger students, developmentally appropriate framing, and a policy-level recommendation structure rather than operational detail in the report itself.

# Differences Over Whether Limited Applied Use Should Be Permitted

Within that shared foundation, members differ on how much room FCCPS should leave for school-sponsored applied uses of AI in PreK–5. One view would keep school-sponsored applied GenAI use in PreK–5 very rare and strongly disfavored, reserving student exposure mainly for AI literacy, demonstration, and closely supervised exceptions. Another would preserve a narrow pathway for educator-directed applied use when the instructional value is clear, adult supervision is close, and foundational development is not displaced. These positions do not mainly reflect disagreement about whether younger children need protection; they reflect different judgments about whether limited, highly governed applied use is ever educationally justified in the elementary years.

# Remaining Question About the Proper Elementary-School Posture

The Committee agrees that FCCPS should be especially cautious with AI in PreK–5 and that younger students should not be treated as routine direct users of generative AI. The remaining disagreement is whether that caution should be expressed mainly as near-minimization of applied student use, or as a narrow, tightly governed pathway for educator-directed exposure and selected instructional uses under strong developmental guardrails.

WS-POL-F – Student Use – PreK-5 – David Berol

Younger children differ markedly from adults in their capacity to interpret technology tools that appear to be engaging with them socially (e.g., chatting or talking to them)1 They are prone to “plac[ing] too much trust in an AI partner” and struggle to “distinguish between simulated abilities and genuine human abilities.”2 A recent review by pediatricians at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia concluded that children under 6 “have a limited ability to distinguish between AI and human interaction.” Children in the 6-12 age band are characteristically prone to the following GenAI risks: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”3

Considering these risks, school-sponsored use of Generative AI in grades pre-K through 5 should be primarily oriented towards teaching AI Literacy. That is, GenAI should primarily be used for demonstration purposes in instruction about Gen AI. It should be used minimally in contexts where the primary purpose is to provide instruction on traditional subjects such as math and language arts (i.e., “Applied Uses”). In all cases, GenAI use by students in this grade band should closely supervised by adults.

When the school is considering implementing Applied Uses of GenAI tools in this grade band, it should consider in particular:

Whether there is clear and convincing evidence of significant educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained.

Whether the intended educational objective can be achieved while still maintaining close adult supervision of all student use of the GenAI.

Whether the tool in question claims to be the student’s friend (i.e., to be an “AI buddy”) or to have authority over the student (i.e., to be an “AI co-teacher”). Such tools are inappropriate for younger students because they may be taken at face value.

Whether the tool would deprive children of motivation to reach educational milestones that were considered fundamental prior to the availability of GenAI.

WS-POL-F – Student Use / PreK–5 – John Black

The PreK–5 section should begin from a more protective posture than later grade bands. Younger students are still developing foundational literacy, numeracy, attention, self-regulation, social understanding, and trust in human relationships. For that reason, I agree with the Committee’s baseline conclusion that FCCPS should teach AI literacy in this band while globally minimizing routine direct student AI use. I also agree that FCCPS should be especially cautious with conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating systems that may lead younger students to confuse AI interaction with human relationship or authority. (Dietz et al., 2023)

At the same time, I would not write this section as a posture that would leave little or no room for school-sponsored applied use beyond tightly supervised exceptions. The better policy is a design-first, literacy-first, adult-mediated posture. In this band, FCCPS should presume that foundational non-AI learning and human interaction remain primary. Any departure from that presumption should be narrow, educator-directed, and justified by a clear instructional purpose that cannot be met as well through simpler, lower-risk means. In other words, younger students should not be expected to use GenAI routinely as a personal learning companion, drafting tool, or independent problem-solving aid. But there may be limited cases in which teacher-scripted, school-governed, closely supervised exposure serves legitimate educational purposes, including AI literacy itself (Burns et al., 2026, pp. 18–19, 27–32, 126).

The strongest case for caution is developmental. Research cited in committee materials and recent pediatric guidance both suggest that younger children may interpret conversational AI through ordinary social-agent concepts and may have difficulty distinguishing between AI and human interaction. CHOP’s March 2026 review states plainly that AI should be treated as a tool, not a friend, and recommends close supervision and shared review of AI-generated content for younger children. That guidance is consistent with the Committee’s concern about anthropomorphic framing and with the need to protect social development, critical thinking, and trust in teachers and caregivers as the primary human authorities in school. (Dietz et al., 2023)

## For that reason, I support the following posture for PreK–5.

First, FCCPS should prioritize AI literacy over routine applied use. In this band, AI literacy should mean age-appropriate understanding that AI is a machine-generated tool, that it can be wrong, that it should not be treated as a person, and that human judgment remains primary. Literacy in this context is not a call for broad direct student use. It is a call for careful, educator-led introduction to what AI is and is not. (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia)

Second, FCCPS should maintain a strong presumption that direct student interaction with GenAI is limited and educator-mediated. The early grades are not the place for routine independent chatbot use, free-form prompt experimentation, or unsupervised AI-supported task completion. Where students do interact directly with a system, that interaction should be brief, bounded, and supervised, with the teacher or another adult framing what the tool is doing and helping students interpret the output. (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia)

Third, FCCPS should restrict anthropomorphic and quasi-social uses. Younger students should not be encouraged to relate to AI as a buddy, coach, therapist, co-teacher, or other human-like authority. Conversational role-play, historical-figure simulation, or emotionally suggestive companion framing should be treated with particular caution in this band, because the developmental and relational risks outweigh the speculative benefits. (Dietz et al., 2023)

Fourth, FCCPS should leave narrow room for carefully bounded, school-sponsored uses only when clear conditions are met. Those conditions should include: a defined learning objective; close adult supervision; district-approved tools and privacy review; no anthropomorphic framing; no displacement of foundational skill-building; and a credible rationale for why the activity adds value beyond non-AI alternatives. This is where I respectfully part company with a near-categorical minimization approach. I do not think policy should foreclose every possibility of teacher-directed applied use in grades 3–5, for example, if the use is limited, transparent, developmentally appropriate, and tied to a genuine instructional purpose. But the burden of justification should remain high. (Brookings)

That distinction matters because “minimal use” should not be interpreted as “no structured exposure.” In my view, FCCPS should avoid two errors. The first is overexposure: normalizing direct AI interaction so early that children come to rely on it before they have built foundational human capacities. The second is underpreparation: acting as if younger students can be fully protected simply by withholding structured exposure while AI continues to exist in their homes, media, and future school environments. A well-designed policy can avoid both errors by keeping the elementary posture highly protective while still allowing educators to introduce AI in narrow, intentional, human-centered ways. (Burns et al., 2026, pp. 27–32, 126)

In practical terms, this means FCCPS should use a simple decision rule for PreK–5: if the educational value of the activity depends on children practicing foundational human skills, building social understanding, or engaging directly with teachers, peers, texts, or concrete materials, then AI should generally not play a direct role. If the activity is instead primarily about introducing AI literacy, illustrating a concept under teacher control, or supporting a tightly bounded instructional task that does not displace core developmental work, then a limited and supervised use may be justified. This is a more defensible posture than either broad adoption or blanket prohibition. It is cautious where caution is most warranted but still leaves room for educator judgment within strong guardrails. (Burns et al., 2026, pp. 19, 126)

My respectful disagreement with the stronger restriction-first position for PreK–5 is therefore narrower than in WS-POL-B. I agree that this band should be treated differently from older students, that direct use should be rare, and that anthropomorphic or socially suggestive systems are especially problematic. Where I differ is that I would preserve a carefully governed pathway for limited educator-directed exposure and selected applied use when it clearly serves learning goals and does not displace foundational development. That approach better reflects the Committee’s shared principles of developmental appropriateness, human-centered design, transparency, and flexibility under uncertainty.

WS-POL-G – Student Use – Grades 6-12 – Chair Preface

# Shared Expectations for Gradual Expansion of Student AI Use

This section reflects broad agreement that students in Grades 6–12 should encounter more structured opportunities to use AI than younger students, but under clearer, more differentiated expectations. The Committee generally agrees that use should become broader as age, task complexity, and student judgment increase; that learning goals remain primary; that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted; and that policy in this band should distinguish among categories of use, interaction boundaries, and disclosure expectations. The Committee also broadly agrees that integrity and fairness cannot be protected by detector-driven policing alone and that expectations for older students must be explicit because they will encounter AI both inside and outside school.

# Differences Over Restriction, Scaffolding, and Guided Integration

Within that shared foundation, members differ on default posture and on how much AI should be allowed in ordinary academic work. One view treats GenAI in this band mainly as scaffolding: allowed in some contexts, but often restricted where it may displace core thinking, especially in writing-heavy and higher-stakes work. Another view favors broader guided-use opportunities, arguing that school should help students build fluency, judgment, and ethical habits under transparent rules rather than rely mainly on restrictions. A smaller but real position is more innovation-forward still, placing greater weight on teacher judgment and on adopting AI where it appears to improve learning, even when the evidence base is incomplete.

# Core Policy Choice for Older Student Use

The Committee agrees that Grades 6–12 should receive broader and more intentional AI exposure than younger students, but it does not agree on how broadly AI should be permitted in routine student work or how much policy should rely on restriction versus guided integration. The disagreement is best understood as a difference in posture: how to protect learning and integrity while also preparing older students for a world in which AI fluency is increasingly consequential.

WS-POL-G – Student Use – Grades 6-12 – David Berol

Within this grade band, it is appropriate to expect that students will start using GenAI tools to obtain assistance in learning and completing their schoolwork.  Teachers need to impose pragmatic and nuanced restrictions on GenAI uses so that they function solely as scaffolding for the desired learning goals. Note a critical feature of scaffolding: it is intended to be eventually removed. By contrast, certain GenAI use cases seem likely to train students to have life-long tool dependency.1

This concern is particularly pronounced with respect to at-home student writing in the humanities. There is evidence that writing done in “collaboration” with AI is analytically passive and less rewarding to students.2

Furthermore, there is no analogy from the impact of calculators on mathematics instruction to the impact of Generative AI on writing instruction. De-centering the skill of performing rapid mental calculations is appropriate to conserve time and energy for learning more abstract mathematical concepts.  But Generative AI can now de-center the abstract work of figuring out what one wishes to say in an essay, finding evidence to support one’s views, and arguing persuasively from evidence to conclusion.  To what supposedly more important end are we relieving students from this labor?

The following corrective measures are recommended:

Teachers should recognize that they are de-centering whatever aspect of the writing process they allow to be AI-assisted. Depending on the learning goals of the assignment, teachers should establish precise restrictions on GenAI usage. These restrictions should vary from assignment to assignment to avoid training students into long-term habits of AI-dependency for any given step of the writing process.

Teachers should be particularly sparing in allowing GenAI at the initial stages of the writing process (i.e., brainstorming, suggesting organization, suggesting argumentation strategies). At this stage, students using AI are missing out on the most fundamental thinking processes that writing is intended to develop.

Develop GenAI disclosure policy and regulations that will motivate students to comply with the teacher’s restrictions on usage. Most students in this grade band are not so intent on cheating that they would stoop to developing falsified chat logs for purposes of deceiving their teachers about their use of GenAI.

Increase the amount of writing work that occurs in-class, where it can be directly proctored.

Introduce additional in-class components to writing assignments. See discussion under WS-POL-H (“Control Strategies”).

Other subjects and learning scenarios may be especially well suited to assistance from GenAI. There, student usage ought to be less restricted. The Board should direct the schools to identify these learning scenarios in detail. Here are two illustrative examples:

Formative assignments in STEM and Foreign Language: For most students in this grade band, it will be clear that summative assessment will eventually occur in a proctored environment without the use of GenAI (i.e., a paper and pencil exam). Thus, savvy students have an intrinsic motivation to avoid becoming dependent on tools that will be unavailable to them in the actual summative. Furthermore, it is common for students to discover that they are confused about math and science concepts (or foreign language grammar/vocabulary) as they are working through their homework. Gen AI holds promise as a tool for providing on-the-spot clarification and tutoring in these circumstances. Teachers should support students in how to craft GenAI queries that are less likely to “spoil” the homework problems and should rely on AI-free summatives as the principal integrity control measure.

Computer science assignments: To a certain extent, learning to write computer code could be analogized to writing essays in the humanities.  But in recent years the practice of writing computer programs has become pervasively and permanently AI-integrated. Therefore, there is less reason to be concerned about fostering AI-dependency here than in the case of an English or History essay.

WS-POL-G – WS-POL-G Student Use – Grades 6-12 – John Black

Students in Grades 6–12 should be prepared not only to avoid harmful or inappropriate uses of AI, but to understand and use AI well. For this grade band, I support a more permissive and innovation-forward posture than the one I would apply in PreK–5, while still preserving strong guardrails around learning goals, transparency, fairness, and human judgment. I agree with the Committee’s progression that older students should receive increased autonomy with clear expectations for disclosure and evaluation and that use should become broader as age, task complexity, and student judgment increase. I also agree that AI use in this band must support learning rather than replace it. Where I differ from the more restrictive position is that I do not think FCCPS should treat student AI use in Grades 6–12 primarily as a temporary exception to traditional schooling. I think FCCPS should treat it as an increasingly normal part of the environment students are entering and should therefore give students structured opportunities to build fluency, judgment, and responsibility under school-governed conditions.

My starting point is that restrictions without structured opportunities for guided practice is not a realistic or sufficient response for this age group. The Committee has already recognized that AI tools are widely accessible outside school and are increasingly part of students’ everyday academic and social environments. In Grades 6–12, that reality matters more, not less. Students in this band are closer to college, work, and civic settings in which AI tools will be part of ordinary problem-solving, research, drafting, coding, planning, and communication. A school policy that focuses primarily on limiting use, without also creating guided opportunities to practice effective and ethical use, risks leaving skill development to outside access, informal experimentation, and unequal support at home. FCCPS should instead make school the place where students learn how to use these tools critically and responsibly.

I therefore support a guided-use posture for Grades 6–12. In this band, FCCPS should permit progressively broader categories of student AI use, but only with explicit instructional framing. Students should learn how AI works, where it is unreliable, how to challenge or verify it, when it is educationally useful, and when it is inappropriate. AI literacy at this level should not be defined only as caution or abstention. It should include practical fluency: knowing how to ask useful questions, comparing outputs, test assumptions, identify hallucinations, preserve authorship, and disclose meaningful assistance honestly. Older students should graduate from FCCPS not only knowing that AI can be risky but also knowing how to use it with discernment.

That does not mean all uses should be treated alike. The Committee is right to call for clearer consensus by age, subject, and learning goal. I support that framework. In some contexts, especially those involving foundational reasoning, close reading, original argument formation, or high-stakes assessment, stronger limitations remain justified. But in many other contexts, especially formative work, feedback-rich practice, selected STEM and language-learning tasks, research support, coding, brainstorming, revision planning, and teacher-approved task support, AI can be allowed more openly. The key question should not be whether AI was present at all. The key question should be whether the use served the learning objective or displaced it.

I respectfully disagree with the more restrictive view to the extent that it treats most student AI use in this band as merely acceptable scaffolding that should ideally disappear. In some contexts, yes, scaffolding is the right model. But in other contexts, especially for older students approaching college and work, AI use is not just a temporary support. It is part of the environment in which they will think, write, code, analyze, and collaborate. FCCPS should not design policy around the assumption that the healthiest outcome is for students to become less able to work with AI over time. The healthier outcome is for students to become more selective, more self-aware, more transparent, and more capable of deciding when AI adds value and when it weakens their learning.

For that reason, I would encourage FCCPS to identify and publish affirmative use cases for Grades 6–12, not merely prohibited or conditional ones. Students and teachers need examples of responsible, educationally sound uses, not only boundaries. The committee’s own student-use work points in that direction by supporting clear categories, task-support boundaries, disclosure expectations, and flexible implementation tied to instructional goals. A policy that articulates only restrictions will not build the habits or fluency the district says it wants. A policy that identifies approved, model use cases will be more educational, more equitable, and more practical.

I also believe the Committee’s categories framework is especially useful for this band. Older students should encounter a policy environment in which some uses are clearly prohibited, some require teacher permission, and some are allowed unless restricted for a particular assignment or course. That model is better than either blanket allowance or blanket prohibition because it creates a shared language for students, teachers, and families. It also supports innovation. Teachers who want to experiment with well-designed AI-supported learning experiences should be able to do so within a transparent framework rather than feeling that every use is presumptively suspect.

Disclosure expectations should be strongest where the learning objective depends heavily on independent student reasoning, authorship, or process. I support requiring students to disclose meaningful AI assistance when it contributes to ideas, structure, or content, but I do not support treating disclosure as a universal demand for chat logs in ordinary classroom practice. The Committee is right that there is no consensus on requiring logs, and I agree that over-standardizing process evidence across all contexts would create unnecessary burden and would likely be educationally counterproductive. For Grades 6–12, FCCPS should instead require clear, assignment-specific disclosure expectations and reserve more demanding process-evidence requirements for contexts where they meaningfully support integrity or learning assessment.

Equity is another reason I favor a more permissive, structured approach in this band. If AI fluency becomes increasingly important and if access outside school is already uneven, then a policy that relies too heavily on restriction risks widening differences in preparation. The Committee’s student-use and equity drafts both recognize that access outside school may create unintended advantages and that limiting access alone does not solve overreliance. A better approach is to combine access with structured expectations, guidance, disclosure, and review. In Grades 6–12, that means FCCPS should not simply ask which uses to prevent; it should also ask which uses all students should have the chance to learn under adult guidance.

My position is therefore this: for Grades 6–12, FCCPS should adopt a more permissive, innovation-forward, but clearly governed posture. AI should be integrated into student learning where it supports educational goals, builds literacy and judgment, and prepares students for the environments they are entering. Restrictions remain necessary in some contexts, especially where unaided performance is central. But the district should not define older-student policy mainly by what students must not do. It should define it by what students should learn to do well, ethically, transparently, and with increasing independence. That approach better reflects the Committee’s commitments to developmental progression, human-centered learning, transparency, adaptability, and student preparation.

# Decision — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee generally agrees that student use of AI should be developmentally appropriate and aligned with learning objectives. There is broad agreement that AI use must support, not replace, student learning, and that expectations should be clear and consistent.

The committee also recognizes that AI tools are already widely accessible outside of school and are increasingly part of students’ everyday academic and social environments. The committee recommends that FCCPS adopt a balanced approach that prioritizes student learning while also preparing students to use AI tools thoughtfully, transparently, and responsibly.

The committee emphasizes that expectations must be clearly communicated to students.

when it is restricted

The committee recognizes that access to AI tools outside of school is uneven and may create unintended advantages for some students.

Policy should define clear expectations at a high level, while allowing educators to adapt implementation based on grade level, subject, and instructional goals.

There are many dimensions along which a particular AI student use case may be characterized for the purpose of responsible regulation. These include, for example:

A specification of the generative AI technology or technologies that will be used.

A description of the manner in which those AI technologies will be used (e.g., students may be supplied with a set of permissible prompts or prompt-forms to use when interacting with an AI chatbot; or their at-home use of an AI technology may require adult supervision).

A description of the sort of work or activity for which one will be using AI (e.g., generating a set of practice exercises and solutions, summarizing a passage from a text, assisting with the creation of images for an art project).

A description of how the work or activity for which one will be using AI is to be evaluated (e.g., how much does the assignment contribute to a student’s grade; will it be evaluated in comparison to similar work produced by other students in the class).

A description of how the use of AI technology is to be documented and reported (e.g., a student may be required to submit the transcript of their interaction with an AI chatbot; or they may be required to submit the specific prompts that were used to generate an image or other media).

This is because the real-world scenarios that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Consequently, the concepts and categories by which generative AI use cases are sorted and the specific controls implemented to regulate their adoption by students should maximize the ability of teachers to exercise intelligent discretion on a case-by-case basis. At the same time, owing to the rapid rate of technological advance and the associated challenge of maintaining AI literacy over time, teachers must be given concrete and specific guidance as to how to exercise responsible control over the use of AI by students.

The use of generative AI technologies should be allowed in all and only those cases in which the use of AI can be given a clear justification in terms of existing standards of sound pedagogy, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

Text generated by AI doesn’t qualify as a source and so it shouldn’t be directly cited.1

If Generative AI leads students to human sources, those human sources must be cited if the ideas therein are not common knowledge and the student reads and relies on the ideas in those sources for their work.

With respect to disclosure of Generative AI, the Board should recognize that the core of the issue is how much needs to be disclosed to the teacher in order enable fair assessment of the assignment at hand. This may vary depending on the learning objectives, the age of the students, and the subject being taught. In lieu of a comprehensive matrix of all possible scenarios, here are some heuristic principles:

In contexts where highly iterative and interactive reliance on Generative AI is expected and allowed (e.g., certain computer programming assignments) it is impractical to ask students to retain a chat log. For similar reasons, one would not expect students to keep track of minor grammar/style corrections that their word processor automatically suggests to them.

When the assignment is merely formative (i.e., as opposed to summative), teachers may be able to get by with a lesser standard of disclosure from their students regarding their GenAI use on the assignment. Nonetheless, it would be good for teachers to have a general awareness of what AI their students are using on the formatives, to help explain a sudden drop in performance from the formatives to the summative.

WS-POL-I – Citation & Disclosure – Anubav Vasudevan

Responsible citation practices are reinforced by many independent factors apart from norms of academic integrity. For example, responsible citation serves to honor intellectual ownership by giving credit where it is due; it helps to establish the credibility and rigor of written work by grounding it in informed research rather than uninformed opinion; it enables the independent verification of factual claims by others; and it situates the writer’s ideas within a broader conversation by positioning those ideas relative to existing debates.

Many of these factors simply do not apply to the output of generative AI technologies. Consequently, teachers and students are both likely to be in the dark as to how AI generated content ought to be cited. The first point to be clear about is that, despite their inevitable integration into information processing workflows, generative AI technologies should not be treated as reliable sources of information. AI literacy programs should be devised, in part, to discourage students from interacting with these technologies as if they were accessing reliable reference materials, and any policies adopted by the schools involving the citation of AI generated material should reinforce this cautious view. In fact, for this reason, it may well be that citation is not a particularly suitable lens through which to view policy regulating responsible AI usage. Disclosure is the more appropriate notion.

The main aim of a disclosure policy is to help maintain a culture of honesty, trust, and responsibility in the academic community by requiring students to fully disclose any external aids, resources, or borrowed material that they utilize in their schoolwork. It may be tempting for the schools to simply enact a general policy requiring full disclosure of any student use of generative AI technology for school-related tasks or activities. Apart from being impractical, however, there are potential flaws with such a policy. For example, a 2025 study found that any AI disclosure—whether voluntary or mandatory—reduced trust in the person using AI, even when their AI usage was permitted. The researchers who conducted the study concluded that “the observed effect can be attributed primarily to the act of disclosure rather than to the mere fact of AI usage”. This effect creates a challenge: schools must require the disclosure of AI usage to ensure transparency and fairness, but that very requirement may end up penalizing students who abide by the rules.

For this reason, rather than trying to regulate responsible AI usage exclusively through disclosure policies, teachers and staff should also be encouraged to explore alternative methodologies that could circumvent the need for disclosure. For example, “reverse lecturing” or the flipped classroom paradigm—where students are delivered instructional content outside the classroom and produce their work in class—can reduce the need for AI disclosure because it shifts the tasks that would be most benefited by AI usage into a supervised environment where AI assistance is unavailable. Studies on flipped classrooms observe that, in addition to its general efficacy, this structure discourages academic dishonesty by allowing teachers to monitor students’ work habits closely, provide individual assistance, and clarify what counts as plagiarism and misuse in real time.

Ultimately, AI disclosure policy should be designed to empower teachers to make assignment choices on pedagogical, not policing, grounds. The primary question underlying their assignment choices should be: “What kind of task most effectively develops students’ understanding, skills, and judgment?” not “What kind of task is hardest to outsource to a generative AI system?” Framing policy around fear of academic dishonesty risks pushing instruction away from rudimentary work—such as short summarizing essays, take‑home reflections, problem sets, etc.—that, despite being easy to outsource to AI systems, are essential building blocks for higher learning.

# Commitments to Consider

## Guiding Principles for Student Privacy and Data Governance

FCCPS should adopt the following principles as the foundation for AI-related privacy and data governance:

Compliance first. All AI tools and data practices must comply with FERPA, COPPA (where applicable), IDEA and Section 504 (for students with disabilities), and Virginia student data privacy law. Compliance is a baseline, not a ceiling.

Data minimization. AI tools should collect and retain only the student information necessary for their approved educational function. Vendors should not receive data beyond what is required to deliver the service.

Purpose limitation. Student data collected through AI tools shall be used only for legitimate educational purposes. Use of student data for advertising, commercial profiling, or AI model training is prohibited unless expressly authorized by FCCPS.

Transparency. FCCPS shall maintain transparency about which AI tools are in use, what data they collect, and how that data is handled. Families and staff should have access to clear, plain-language information about AI data practices.

Vendor accountability. Vendors processing student data must comply with FCCPS governance requirements, including contractual data processing agreements, notification of material changes, and data deletion upon contract termination.

Human oversight for consequential decisions. AI tools used to inform decisions about student placement, grading, intervention, discipline, or special education eligibility must include human-in-the-loop oversight. AI recommendations inform; they do not decide.

# Tiered Risk Model for AI Tool Approval

Every AI tool used in FCCPS classrooms or operations should be classified into one of three risk tiers based on how it handles student data. Each tier triggers a different level of review, approval, and ongoing oversight. This tiered approach is consistent with the VDOE AI Guidelines recommendation for differentiated governance based on risk level.

# Escalation Rules

The following conditions automatically escalate a tool to Tier 3 review regardless of vendor assurances:

Identity linkage: Any tool requiring student accounts or connecting to FCCPS identity systems (SSO, SIS, LMS) is treated as Tier 3.

Consequential use: Any tool used to inform placement, grading, intervention, discipline, or special education decisions is treated as Tier 3.

Student artifacts: Student work samples (writing, images, audio/video) can be identifying even without names. Tools receiving student artifacts should not be classified below Tier 2.

Third-party data sharing:  Any tool whose vendor shares student data with subprocessors, third-party AI services, or downstream analytics providers is treated as Tier 3 unless the vendor contractually binds all subprocessors to equivalent data protections.

Embedded AI features: When an already-approved platform introduces new AI capabilities (e.g., generative AI features, predictive analytics, automated recommendations), those features must be reviewed independently and classified under the tiered framework. Existing platform approval does not automatically extend to new AI functionality.

AI tools used with students receiving special education services require heightened scrutiny regardless of tier. In addition to FERPA, FCCPS must ensure AI practices align with IDEA, Section 504, and ADA expectations.

FCCPS should recognize that AI tools in special education contexts intersect with the work of a broad range of professionals beyond classroom teachers, including school psychologists, speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, school social workers, school counselors, nurses, and behavioral specialists. These professionals generate, access, and rely on sensitive student data as part of their clinical and educational responsibilities. AI governance for students with disabilities must account for how these professionals may use AI tools to support evaluation, progress monitoring, treatment planning, and family communication — and must ensure that AI use in these contexts enhances individualized planning with human expertise at the center, not replaces it.

The following data categories are especially sensitive:

IEP/504 plan data, including disability classifications, accommodations, modifications, and progress monitoring data

Assistive technology usage patterns, which may reveal disability-related information even when not explicitly labeled

Behavioral and counseling-related data from monitoring systems that may amplify bias

Clinical and therapeutic documentation generated by related service providers (speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, school psychologists, social workers) that may be processed through AI-assisted documentation or reporting tools

Student work artifacts that can reveal disability-related information and re-identify students

This section should not be framed only as a warning against staffing cuts. It should also explain what kind of workload relief is legitimate, what kinds of new burdens AI policy will create, and what the district must be prepared to fund and support if it wants its AI policy to be credible in practice. Workforce and budget implications are not merely downstream implementation details. They are among the clearest tests of whether a proposed AI posture is realistic, educationally sound, and publicly trustworthy.

# AI impacts on teacher workload

AI may reduce some forms of teacher workload. In bounded contexts, it may help educators generate first drafts of routine materials, re-level text, create examples, organize lesson ideas, or prepare low-stakes administrative communications. Used carefully, that can free time for more human work: planning, conferencing, relationship-building, differentiation, and higher-value instructional judgment. A policy that refuses to acknowledge any legitimate gain of efficiency will not match reality and will make it harder to govern the tools honestly.

At the same time, AI very easily creates the illusion of saving time while shifting burden elsewhere. A teacher may save ten minutes drafting something and then spend twenty minutes checking accuracy, bias, tone, privacy implications, and alignment to learning goals. Even where the net effect is positive, the gain is often narrow, fragile, and dependent on staff skills. FCCPS should therefore avoid speaking about “efficiency” in the abstract. The relevant question is not whether AI can make some tasks faster in isolation. The relevant question is whether it reduces total professional burden without degrading quality, trust, or judgment.

That distinction matters especially in education, where some of the most important work should not be optimized away. If a tool helps a teacher create a first draft of a family update, that may be a reasonable use. If it tempts the district to normalize thinner feedback, more automated evaluation, or less thoughtful instructional design, the apparent efficiency is educationally counterfeit. FCCPS should therefore adopt a bounded-efficiency principle: AI-supported workload relief may be pursued in low-risk, non-delegable contexts, but only where the time saved is real, the quality can be maintained, and the result strengthens rather than weakens the human core of teaching.

# AI impacts on teacher staffing levels

FCCPS should not cite speculative AI efficiencies as justification to reduce staffing, delay backfilling, or expand the expected scope of educators’ responsibilities. If the district adopts AI tools and later claims that teachers can therefore do more with less, the policy will immediately lose credibility with staff and families alike. That would not be perceived as innovation in service of learning. It would be perceived, understandably, as labor substitution.

Any verified efficiency gains should be treated first as an opportunity to protect and improve educational quality, not to cut labor costs. In practical terms, that means time saved through bounded AI assistance should be reinvested in human work that schools chronically struggle to provide well: more individualized feedback, better family communication, stronger scaffolding, better accessibility, more thoughtful assessment design, and more consistent student support. The first purpose of efficiency should be to restore capacity, not to eliminate it.

FCCPS should therefore state clearly that AI adoption should not become a basis for reducing the teacher workforce or weakening the expectation of human availability to students and families. If, over time, some operational functions can truly be streamlined, that should be evaluated transparently and cautiously, with a presumption in favor of preserving instructional relationships and human accountability. In a district like FCCPS, trust is part of the value proposition. AI should not be used to erode the human presence that makes the system worthy of that trust.

# Implementation burden on teachers and staff

One of the biggest risks in this entire policy space is burden shifting. It is easy to write elegant policy language that assumes someone else will absorb the cost of interpretation, redesign, and compliance. In practice, that “someone else” is often the classroom teacher, school leader, instructional coach, or technology staff member already carrying a full load. FCCPS should therefore say plainly that any AI policy creates implementation work, and that the district has an obligation to minimize avoidable burdens on front-line staff.

Those burdens are not hypothetical. They include learning new rules, redesigning assessments, deciding when AI use is allowed or prohibited, interpreting disclosure expectations, explaining rules to students and families, reviewing student process evidence where relevant, learning approved tools, reporting issues, and navigating uncertainty when edge cases arise. Even a relatively restrained policy can create substantial friction if the district pushes responsibility downward without adequate structure.

For that reason, the district should centralize as much of the governance burden as possible. Teachers should not be expected to invent their own AI governance regime from scratch. FCCPS should provide shared models, exemplars, common use-case guidance, approved-tool lists, plain-language family communications, and simple disclosure conventions where appropriate. The more that core governance work is done once at the district level, the less likely it is that every school or classroom will improvise its own inconsistent and burdensome local version.

This is also why policy provisions that sound good in principle but multiply parallel obligations in practice should be viewed cautiously. If a notice, consent, disclosure, review, or documentation rule is too complex to implement consistently, it may create unevenness, frustration, and performative compliance rather than genuine protection. Good policy in this area should be judged partly by whether ordinary educators can follow it without being turned into full-time AI compliance officers.

Capacity requirements for training, support, review, and compliance

FCCPS should not adopt AI expectations without a corresponding capacity plan. If the district wants teachers and staff to implement AI policy responsibly, it must be prepared to support training, review, procurement, privacy/security oversight, family communication, and periodic reassessment. AI governance is not free. Even a cautious, bounded policy requires real institutional capacity.

There are at least five capacities the district must account for. First, staff training: not generic hype-driven AI training, but practical, role-based preparation on what is allowed, what is not, what requires disclosure, how to protect student data, and how to preserve learning goals. Second, implementation support: exemplars, guidance sheets, shared language, and usable decision frameworks. Third, governance and review: tool vetting, vendor oversight, privacy/security review, and some mechanism for incident triage and policy updating. Fourth, communications capacity: plain-language notice to families, staff-facing clarity, and a way to respond to predictable concerns without making every teacher carry the whole explanatory load. Fifth, monitoring and adaptation: the ability to determine whether the district’s AI use is improving practice, creating harm, or simply moving burden around.

This is why a phased and disciplined approach to AI adoption makes the most sense. FCCPS should not authorize new uses faster than it can support them. Where the district lacks the capacity to train, review, monitor, or communicate well, that is not merely an implementation inconvenience. It is a policy signal that the use should be narrowed, delayed, or piloted more carefully. Policy ambition should be matched to institutional readiness.

The overall implication for WS-POL-N is straightforward. First, AI may deliver bounded efficiencies, but those gains are not automatic and should not be overstated. Second, FCCPS should reject the use of AI as a rationale for reducing staffing or normalizing thinner human support. Third, the district should treat workload protection and implementation capacity as design constraints from the outset: centralize what can be centralized, simplify what can be simplified, and adopt only what FCCPS can responsibly support. Framed this way, the section becomes more than a warning. It becomes a realistic framework for protecting staff, protecting trust, and ensuring that any AI use the district permits is sustainable.

The committee recommends that FCCPS define clear criteria for evaluating the impact of AI use.

identifying anticipated benefits (e.g., instructional support, efficiency, access)

establishing baseline conditions prior to implementation

measuring outcomes over time

Evaluation should consider both benefits and unintended effects, including impacts on student learning, equity, and teacher workload.

The committee agrees that FCCPS should have a clear process for responding to incidents involving AI tools.

false accusations related to academic integrity

privacy or data exposure concerns

harmful or inappropriate outputs affecting students

clear escalation pathways

defined responsibility for review and action

timely communication where appropriate

Incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, and transparency.

## Purpose of AI Policy

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

how students learn to use AI as a foundational tool for future study and work

how our schools integrate AI into the design and delivery of education to enhance

This policy will provide guidance regarding how to capture the benefits that AI may create and how to mitigate the risks and community concerns posed by AI.

## Principles for Developing Policy

As the FCCPS develops its policy, the policy must be developed with the following principles:

Evidence-informed. The policy should clearly articulate the human skills, competencies, and other outcomes it prioritizes. Recommendations should be clearly connected to these outcomes and supported by evidence. The strength of recommendations should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate.

Alignment with IB values. The AI policy should align with the values and learning goals for the IB program, which is designed to build the following five skills:

Thinking (critical, creative, and ethical)

Research (comparing, contrasting, validating and prioritizing information)

Communication (written and oral, effective listening, and formulating arguments)

Social (forming and maintaining positive relationships, and conflict resolution)

Self-management (organizational skills, such as managing time and tasks, and affective skills, such as managing state of mind and motivation)

Age-appropriate. Approaches to learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age. For example, the Primary Years Programme encourages PK-5 students “to become active, caring, lifelong learners.” As students age, the Middle Years Programme for grades 6-10 encourages students “to become creative, critical and reflective thinkers.” Finally, in high school, the Diploma Programme and Career Programme equip students in grades 11-12 with skills tailored to the pathway they will choose to pursue after graduation.

Centered on the needs of our students and educators. While we must look to research, policy professionals, and other communities for guidance in creating our policy, such guidance may not always be appropriate for or replicable in our community. We must ensure that feedback from our students, parents, and educators are prioritized when assessing whether a new AI-policy action is appropriate or effective.

Evolutionary. This is the school’s first AI policy and AI is evolving rapidly. The policy created by the school system should be revised more frequently than other policies that deal with slower-moving issues. This will require frequent re-assessment of the policy and its implementation.

## Challenges

We have identified the following challenges that make adoption of AI in FCCPS challenging. A successful policy must clearly address these challenges.

The benefits of using AI in K-12 education are highly uncertain. If the benefits of AI in K-12 were clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions would be clear. In reality, some schools have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI, while the broader literature contains many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users. Just because one school system managed to implement this new technology well, does not mean that another school system will necessarily achieve a similar result.

The use of AI introduces risks. AI introduces novel risks associated with academic integrity; for instance, by making it easier to cheat on writing assignments. Students may be less likely to reach out to a human for mental health issues. AI makes it easy to create hurtful or otherwise inappropriate images, videos, and sound recordings, including of other students and teachers. There are privacy concerns associated with student information being uploaded to servers the school does not control; student inputs may get used for training purposes, which present a pathway for leaking sensitive information to the broader community. Whether adequate guardrails can be incorporated into AI systems is currently unknown.

Students and educators will have access to AI regardless of school policy. This means that those who choose to adopt the technology may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those that do not adopt AI; this applies to both students and teachers alike. Likewise, educators may use the technology against the wishes of students and parents who do not want their work run through an AI system.

It is generally not possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI. This makes any rule or policy potentially difficult to enforce.

# Current State of AI and Education

## Working definitions

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a label given to a computing system that can perform a task commonly associated with human intelligence. The specific technologies given this label have varied widely over time as computing systems have evolved. What we consider AI today may not be considered AI in the near future.

Language models are computing systems that predict sequences of words in response to an input. Originally designed to use strict grammatical rules, the language models began to use purely statistical methods for predicting words in the 1980s. Once viewed as a form of artificial intelligence, these methods are now considered old forms of machine learning.

Large language models (LLMs) are the name for the current iteration of language models. They are based on a new computational technique, called the transformer model, that allows language models to be run on GPUs. This allows the models to calculate statistics over larger sets of training data than was previously possible. As with any language model, the outputs are sequences of words based on the statistics from their training data.

Generative AI (GenAI) is the label given to LLMs and the broader set of tools that have extended the functionality of LLMs to create statistical predictions of images, audio, video, code, or other forms of output.

AI Literacy:  “AI literacy is a set of foundational skills and competencies that enable individuals to understand how AI works, responsibly evaluate AI systems, and use AI effectively as a human-centered tool across various contexts, including learning, work, and civic applications, while adapting to its ongoing evolution.” (Digital Promise)

AI Education: The study of how to design, build, and research artificial intelligence systems. It involves developing the technical knowledge and skills necessary to create, improve, and advance AI technologies. AI education is typically associated with computer science and related technical fields, and includes areas such as programming, machine learning, data science, and algorithm development.

AI in Education: The integration of artificial intelligence technologies into educational environments to support student learning, instructional design, assessment practices, and institutional operations. This includes both instructional applications—such as personalized learning and generative AI tools—and administrative uses that improve efficiency and decision-making.

## Efficacy of AI in Learning Environments

### Kosmyna 2025. “Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task.”

Relevance: Using genAI during initial essay writing appears to be far less effective than using a brain-only or search-engine-enhanced approach for cultivating the IB values of critical thinking, creative thinking, and communication. Using genAI during the ideation stage of a project leads to an echo chamber effect where essay topics cluster around the top responses of the LLM, which themselves may be affected by the business priorities of the LLM company. “These findings support an educational model that delays AI integration until learners have engaged in sufficient self-driven cognitive effort.”

Summary: This Time article provides a plain-language summary. Adults (54) were assigned 4 20-minute essay writing tasks that are based on SAT writing prompts. While writing, their brain activity was measured by EEG. The adults were sorted into three groups; those that could write their essay 1) with the help of only chatgpt, 2) with the help of internet resources but no AI assistance, and 3) without any outside help, similar to the environment of taking the SAT. The research found the group that was allowed to use ChatGPT had the lowest brain engagement and “consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels.” In subsequent interviews, this group struggled to recall what they had written, whereas the other groups had retained substantial recall of their work. The essays created by the chatgpt users were more homogeneously written in terms of the concepts used to respond to the prompt and the language used to write the essay. The group that was forced to use only their brains–no outside help–showed the highest neural activity in areas that are associated with creativity, memory, and the ability to process meaning. This paper is not peer-reviewed.

Scenario Bank

Below are several grade-banded vignettes describing AI use-cases that illustrate how the values and principles endorsed by this committee, as documented in [V&P doc ref], can be applied in actual classroom settings. Each scenario is followed by a brief description of its potential benefits and risks, and a list of scenario specifications in which the described AI use-case should be allowed, restricted, or prohibited, in accordance with this committee’s values and principles.

These determinations reflect a human-centered approach to AI. The use of AI technologies should be allowed and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but the people involved in administering the use of such technologies must remain accountable to existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

The real-world scenarios involving the use of AI that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Accordingly, the examples described below should be taken as illustrative not prescriptive, and with the acknowledgement that educators must be afforded full allowance to exercise their own discretion on a case-by-case basis.

## 1) Grades K–2 : “Read-Aloud Buddy at Home”

Scenario: A 1st grader uses an AI chatbot and voice assistant at home to generate a story from a prompt , interacting with the AI chatbot to practice letter sounds, vocabulary, and spelling .

Benefits: Build confidence and engagement through the use of personally curated content.

Risks: Privacy exposure. Generation of inaccurate or age-inappropriate material.

Allowed:

The student employs an AI tool that is supplied by the school and engages with the AI chatbot using only approved teacher-scripted prompts and with adult supervision.

The student’s at-home use of the AI chatbot reinforces in-class instructional programming and aligns with sound pedagogical practices. If, for example, it is determined that excessive use of the chatbot discourages students from engagement with in-print materials, or inhibits creative forms of word-based experimentation  that are deemed important to achieve early literacy goals, its use should be limited.

Not Allowed:

The student uses the AI-chatbot to generate a story from prompts that reference the names of other students, their photos, or other identifying information.

The student uses a tool that records and collects the child’s voice or personal information without parental approval and FCCPS sanctioned controls.

The student uses a tool that is not supplied by the school or uses that tool without adult supervision.

## 2) Grades 3–5 : “AI Assistance with Images for a Science Poster”

Scenario: A 4th grader is assigned the task of creating a poster for a science class that depicts in visual form the differences between weathering, erosion, and deposition. The relevant information about the topic can be obtained from the various materials that have been officially sanctioned for class use . The purpose of the assignment is for students to exercise their creativity in order to visually represent science concepts presented in class.

Benefits: Supplement irrelevant skills , which, given the format of an assignment, may have a differential impact on the final product.

Risks: Obviate core skill-building activities. Produce polished work that can misrepresent student knowledge or abilities.

Allowed:

After researching the topic, the student drafts detailed descriptions of the images to be included on the poster and then uses an AI image-generation tool to produce these images and to assist in the layout and design of the poster . When submitting the poster, the student is required to clearly indicate that an image was generated using AI and to include the specific prompt used to generate that image.

Conditional:

The student uses AI to assist in organizing or summarizing officially sanctioned class materials . Especially in earlier grades, when assignments are not just meant to test content knowledge but also to help students acquire core skills deemed essential to later learning, more restrictive limitations on the use of AI may be called for. In this case, part of the purpose of the assignment could be for students to practice navigating larger documents, like a textbook or online resource, in search of specific information. Even if AI systems can reliably perform such preliminary organizational tasks, students should not be encouraged to offload this work to AI systems.

Not Allowed:

The student uses AI to generate a part or the whole of the poster directly from the assignment prompt (The student uses AI to generate a part or the whole of the poster directly from the assignment prompt (“Draw some pictures illustrating the difference between weathering, erosion, and deposition?”, “Create a poster that illustrates the difference between weathering, erosion, and deposition”) )

## 3) Grades 6–8 : “Study Coach for a History Exam”

Scenario: A 7th grader uploads course material to an AI tool and asks it to deliver a concise lecture reviewing the main points covered in the material. The student then asks this AI tool to generate practice questions about the material in the notes in a similar format to the questions that will be included on an upcoming exam. The student asks the AI for feedback assessing the quality of their submitted answers and providing advice as to how they can be improved.

Benefits: Potential to balance inequities created by high-cost instructional aids like extra-curricular classes or private tutors. Provides immediate feedback, tailored to student needs.

Tradeoffs: Overreliance on AI generated answers undermines deep engagement with course content. Possible misinformation and bias in AI generated summaries, questions, and feedback.

## 4) Grades 9-12 : “Writing Support versus Writing Replacement”

Scenario: An 11th grader is assigned a literary analysis essay that requires citing textual evidence and developing an original argument about a novel studied in class. The student uses an AI tool to brainstorm possible thesis statements and outline counterarguments. The student then drafts the essay on their own, occasionally revising it in response to targeted queries to the AI tool about organization, structure and style, while working to ensure that the essay is still written in the student’s own voice, and makes reference to the assignment and in-class discussions.

Benefits: Encourages a structured approach to writing that utilizes auxiliary tasks such as brainstorming, outlining, and multiple draft revision. Potential to improve the structure, clarity, and style of the final product through immediate, targeted feedback. Reduce writing anxiety and offer support to multilingual learners and students with writing challenges.

Tradeoffs: Overreliance on AI-generated language can obviate the core analytical and compositional skills needed to produce quality written work. Increases the difficulty of assessing academic integrity and may exacerbate inequities if only some students are adept at using AI writing tools effectively.

## 5) Grades 9-12 : “Synthetic Media, Deepfakes and Harm”

Scenario: In a digital citizenship unit, 12th graders examine how generative AI can be used create synthetic media and deepfakes that convincingly alter audio, images, or video of real people. The students use an AI tool to generate or refine short, clearly labeled synthetic clips  and then analyze how such media could mislead viewers. The students then discuss and develop guidelines for verifying authenticity and responding ethically when they encounter or are targeted by synthetic media.

Benefits: Builds critical media literacy by helping students recognize the capacity of AI to alter context, identity, and evidence in synthetic media, and by giving them strategies for verification and source-checking. Provides a relevant platform  for ethical reflection about consent, privacy, and harm, equipping students to resist participating in or amplifying deepfake-based bullying, harassment, or misinformation

Tradeoffs: Exposure to realistic synthetic content, even in controlled settings, may seem to normalize or trivialize the use of deepfakes, and may be distressing to some students depending on the examples selected. If safeguards are weak, students might attempt to create or share unauthorized deepfakes of other students or staff, leading to serious privacy violations, reputational harm, and disciplinary or legal consequences.

## 6) Teachers : “Lesson Planning and Differentiation”

Scenario: A 3rd  grade teacher inputs information to an AI lesson-planning assistant about student reading levels, known learning needs, and language backgrounds, and asks the tool to create multiple versions of core materials  while maintaining common learning objectives. [Allowed?: The teacher then reviews, edits, and adapts the AI-generated materials, discarding those that are pedagogically unsound or misaligned with local curriculum, and explicitly documents how AI was used in preparing the lesson].

Benefits: Reduces routine planning time thus improving the quality of work-life for teachers. Generates varied entry points, scaffolds, and enrichment options that support differentiated instruction at scale. Can surface new activity formats or question types that teachers might not have otherwise come up with, broadening their instructional repertoire.

Tradeoffs: Over time, increased dependence on AI-produced lesson plans may enervate teachers’ professional judgment, leading to in-class assignments that are misaligned with students’ actual needs, local standards, or up-to-date pedagogical practices. Inaccurate, biased, or developmentally inappropriate content may be introduced into lessons if teachers lack time or expertise to thoroughly review AI-generated materials before classroom use.

## 7) Teachers : “Grading, Feedback and Fairness”

Scenario: A 9th grade teacher uses an AI-assisted grading tool to generate draft rubric scores and feedback comments on short written responses. Students submit work through a learning platform that forwards anonymized responses to the AI system, which produces suggested scores and comments aligned with a teacher-defined rubric. [Allowed: The teacher then reviews a sample of AI-scored work, adjusts scores and comments where necessary, and retains final authority over grades. The teacher may choose to use AI feedback as a first pass that students see quickly, followed by more selective, human-written comments on key assignments.]

Benefits: Speeds up routine scoring and enables more timely, specific feedback, freeing teacher time for higher-value instruction, small-group meetings, and individual work with students. AI-supported scoring can increase consistency in rubric application and help identify patterns of misunderstanding or inequitable outcomes across student groups.

Tradeoffs: Algorithmic bias, rubric misinterpretation, or over-general feedback may unfairly advantage or disadvantage certain students, especially multilingual learners or those with atypical learning styles. Students and parents may perceive AI-mediated grading as impersonal or opaque, undermining teacher trust and professional responsibility.

## 8) Teachers/Admin : “AI for Parent Communication of Sensitive Information”

Scenario: A school counselor or administrator uses an AI writing assistant to draft emails to parents about sensitive issues, such as a student’s pattern of concerning behavior, or a student’s academic struggles. [Allowed: The educator inputs only high-level, non-identifying details into the AI tool  and requests help crafting a message that is clear, empathetic, and free of inflammatory language. The educator then revises the draft to include student-specific details outside of the AI environment, ensuring it reflects school policy, legal requirements, and professional judgment before sending.]

Benefits: Supports clear, professional, and empathetic communication in high-stakes situations, especially for educators who may feel uncertain about tone or language when delivering difficult news. Can help standardize parent communication, reducing the likelihood of omissions or the inadvertent disclosure of personal information. Lowers the cost of creating accessible parent communication systems that are accessible to families with diverse language backgrounds.

Tradeoffs: Inputting identifiable or highly sensitive student information into third-party AI tools can create privacy, confidentiality, and data-governance concerns, including potential non-compliance with FERPA or FCCPS policy. Overreliance on AI-generated language for delicate conversations may flatten nuance, overlook cultural context, or unintentionally convey a tone that damages relationships.

Engagement Methods 
Effective community engagement requires multiple methods tailored to different audiences because stakeholders interact with schools—and technology—in very different ways. Parents may prefer town halls or written feedback opportunities, educators often respond best to structured surveys and listening sessions, and students provide valuable insight through advisory groups where they feel comfortable sharing their perspectives. Using a variety of engagement approaches improves perception and trust, signaling that the district is not making decisions in isolation but is actively listening to the community it serves. It also strengthens buy-in, because stakeholders are more likely to support policies they had an opportunity to understand and shape. For complex issues involving technology, student safety, academic integrity, and classroom practices, the perspectives of many groups must be considered early. When engagement is broad and structured, it helps surface concerns, identify unintended risks, and clarify misconceptions before policies are finalized. In contrast, relying on a single feedback channel can leave important voices unheard and allow misunderstandings

or concerns to grow unchecked. A diverse engagement strategy therefore acts as both a trust-building mechanism and a risk-management tool, ensuring the district gathers balanced input and prevents issues from escalating or “snowballing” as new technologies are introduced into schools.

Message Pillars
Clear, balanced, and consistent messaging will be essential to successfully introduce and govern AI use in schools. Because artificial intelligence touches on sensitive areas such as student safety, academic integrity, technology access, and classroom practices, stakeholders will naturally have questions and concerns. Establishing a set of defined message pillars ensures the district communicates with clarity, transparency, and alignment across all channels, whether information is coming from school leaders, teachers, board members, or district communications. Consistency helps prevent confusion, misinformation, or mixed signals, while repetition reinforces understanding over time as the community becomes more familiar with the topic. When the same core messages are delivered repeatedly—across town halls, staff discussions, student forums, and written communications—it builds confidence that the district is approaching AI thoughtfully and responsibly. A disciplined message track therefore supports shared understanding, trust, and informed participation, ensuring stakeholders clearly understand both the opportunities and the safeguards guiding AI policy in the distri

ct.

FCCPS K–12 AI policy messaging must be guided by rules with contextual flexibility to accomplish five objectives:

Demystify AI

Address safety and risk head-on

Clarify guardrails

Empower responsible use

Create feedback loops

There are 5 Pillars to guide message creation which will flow to assets such as a website, fact sheets, flyers, talking points, etc.

## Pillar 1 — Safety and Protection First

The district’s first responsibility is protecting students.

Key messages:

AI access must be age-appropriate and supervised.

Safeguards will prevent exposure to harmful or inappropriate content.

Student privacy and data security will be protected.

Core idea: Innovation will never come before student safety.

## Pillar 2 — Academic Integrity and Learning

AI must support learning, not replace it.

Key messages:

Students must demonstrate original thinking.

AI-assisted work must be appropriately attributed.

AI cannot replace foundational learning skills.

Core idea: AI should enhance learning—not undermine it.

## Pillar 3 — Clear Guardrails for Responsible Use

Students and educators need clear expectations.

Key messages:

Grade-band expectations will define appropriate AI use.

Approved tools will be identified by the district.

Teachers will guide appropriate classroom use.

Core idea: Clear rules help students use powerful tools responsibly.

## Pillar 4 — Equity and Access

AI should not widen learning gaps.

Key messages:

All students should have fair access to learning tools.

AI literacy will be integrated into digital citizenship education.

Schools will ensure equitable opportunities for skill development.

Core idea: Responsible AI should expand opportunity, not create advantage for only some students.

## Pillar 5 — Transparency and Community Partnership

AI policy will be shaped with community input especially educators and parents.

Key messages:

Families will be informed before major classroom changes.

Community feedback will be actively reviewed.

Policy updates will be shared publicly.

Core idea: AI governance must be transparent and accountable.

# Decision — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee broadly agrees that AI policy implementation must be phased, that professional development is essential before broad adoption, and that governance infrastructure should be in place before higher-risk tools are deployed. There is strong alignment that unphased adoption creates inequitable implementation and that staff training is a prerequisite, not an afterthought.

There are differing perspectives on how prescriptive the phasing requirements should be.

Some members favor explicit gate requirements between phases, where Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 infrastructure is confirmed operational.

Others prefer a more flexible approach that allows building-level discretion on readiness.

These perspectives reflect a legitimate tradeoff between consistency and responsiveness. There is also meaningful disagreement on whether special education-specific professional development should be mandated in policy or left to administrative discretion. This draft recommends the former, but the Board should be aware this is a contested position.

# Commitments to Consider

## Phased Implementation Model

The committee recommends a phased implementation approach with explicit requirements between phases. This model is designed to align with FCCPS’s existing Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS), extending a familiar and proven tiered framework into the AI governance domain. Staff and administrators will recognize the logic immediately, which significantly improves feasibility and reduces the learning curve.

Gate requirement: Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 governance and training infrastructure is confirmed operational. This is not a slowdown mechanism. It is a quality assurance requirement. Piloting higher-risk tools without governance infrastructure in place creates exactly the equity gaps the committee is trying to prevent.

Phasing principle:

Implementation should be phased by risk tier, not by school or grade level.

Piloting high-risk tools in school buildings with higher concentrations of students with IEPs and 504 plans, or buildings experiencing staff shortages, without full safeguards in place would create inequitable conditions for the students most in need of protection.

### Professional Development Framework

The committee recognizes that effective AI governance requires professional development as infrastructure, not as an add-on. CDT’s 2025 survey found that only 22% of teachers have received formal training on AI use and risk management — even as 57% of special education teachers report already using AI for IEP development. This gap between adoption and training must be closed before broader deployment.

The PD framework should include differentiated tracks based on role and responsibility:

PD should be designed to accommodate the full range of FCCPS staff — from experienced educators and support personnel (paraprofessionals, counselors, nurses, custodians, office staff), to librarians and language staff (ELL specialists, district substitutes), to early-career teachers who may use AI tools extensively but without governance awareness. Training should meet staff where they are, not assume uniform starting knowledge.

### Policy Review Cadence

The committee recommends establishing a structured review cycle that balances adaptability with governance efficiency:

Annual administrative review: The superintendent or designee reviews the AI tool inventory, risk tier assignments, incident log, and equity indicators each year. Updates at this level should not require full Board action.

Biennial Board review: The full Board reviews the AI policy every two years, incorporating updated research, technological developments, legal and regulatory changes, and community feedback.

Trigger-based accelerated review: Immediate review required upon:

significant federal or state regulatory change affecting student data privacy or special education, such as updated VDOE guidance following Virginia HB1186

documented harm or inequitable impact on students

emergence of new AI capability categories not covered by existing risk tiers

Tradeoff for Board consideration: Annual versus biennial Board review is a live tradeoff. More frequent review keeps the policy current but requires sustained Board time and staff capacity. Less frequent review provides stability but risks the policy lagging a technology landscape that is moving very fast. The trigger-based mechanism ensures the policy cannot become dangerously outdated between scheduled review cycles regardless of which cadence is selected.

### Pilot and Innovation Pathway

The committee recommends that new or higher-risk uses of AI begin as bounded pilots rather than immediate system-wide implementation. Pilots should have clearly defined goals, include guardrails appropriate to the level of risk, be limited in scope and duration, and include evaluation criteria established before launch. Piloting should also be used as an opportunity to understand how AI can support learning, not only to identify risks.

Pilot evaluation should include both quantitative outcomes (student performance data, usage patterns, incident reports) and qualitative feedback from educators, students, and families. Tools that cannot demonstrate benefit or that produce inequitable outcomes should not advance to broad deployment.

### Success Metrics

FCCPS should define a limited set of indicators to evaluate the effectiveness of AI policy implementation over time, consistent with outcome evaluation methods used in program evaluation research (Grinnell et al., 2019). Recommended metrics include:

Staff completion rates for AI PD by track (all staff, educators, administrators, SPED)

Reduction in potential compliance violations related to AI tool use

Number and tier classification of AI tools in the district inventory

Time from tool request to approval decision, by tier

Number and type of AI-related incidents reported, and resolution timelines

Equity indicators:

disaggregated incident and outcome data by student disability status, race, and language status (consistent with my capstone that uses the DEIPAR framework principles)

Family notification and consent completion rates for Tier 3 tools

Pilot outcomes relative to pre-established evaluation criteria

These indicators should be reviewed as part of the annual administrative review and reported to the Board during biennial policy review cycles.

#### Incident Response

FCCPS should establish a clear, documented process for identifying, reporting, and responding to incidents involving AI tools. Incidents may be caused by staff, students, vendors, or the tools themselves, and may include:

False or disputed academic integrity accusations generated by AI detection tools

Staff use of AI tools in ways that violate policy (e.g., entering student PII into unapproved tools, using AI to generate IEP content without disclosure)

Privacy or data exposure, including unauthorized sharing of student data with AI vendors

Harmful, biased, or inappropriate AI outputs affecting students

Student misuse of AI tools in ways that affect other students or compromise assessment integrity

Vendor breaches of DPA terms, including unauthorized data retention or model training

Response processes should include clear escalation pathways matched to the severity and risk tier of the incident, defined roles for who investigates and who communicates, documentation requirements for all incidents regardless of resolution, and timely notification to affected families where student data or well-being is involved. Incident data should feed into the annual administrative review cycle to identify patterns and inform policy updates. Incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, transparency, and due process for all parties.

# Decision Points and Tradeoffs for Board Consideration

Phase gate requirements: prescriptive vs. flexible. Should Phase 2 require confirmed operational readiness of Phase 1 infrastructure, or should building-level administrators have discretion to assess their own readiness? Prescriptive gates ensure consistency across the district; flexible gates allow faster movement for buildings that are ready

SPED-specific PD: mandated vs. discretionary. Should special education staff training on AI governance be required in policy or left to administrative discretion? IDEA’s qualified personnel requirements support the case for mandatory training, but mandating it in policy adds implementation burden.

Review cadence: annual vs. biennial Board review. Annual review maximizes responsiveness; a biennial review with strong trigger protocols may be more sustainable for a district of FCCPS’s size.
