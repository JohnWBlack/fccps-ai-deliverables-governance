WS-POL-G chair preface

Neutral narrative paragraph

This section reflects broad agreement that students in Grades 6–12 should encounter more structured opportunities to use AI than younger students, but under clearer, more differentiated expectations. The Committee generally agrees that use should become broader as age, task complexity, and student judgment increase; that learning goals remain primary; that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted; and that policy in this band should distinguish among categories of use, interaction boundaries, and disclosure expectations. The Committee also broadly agrees that integrity and fairness cannot be protected by detector-driven policing alone and that expectations for older students must be explicit because they will encounter AI both inside and outside school.

Range of positions paragraph

Within that shared foundation, members differ on default posture and on how much AI should be allowed in ordinary academic work. One position favors stronger restrictions, especially in writing-heavy courses and higher-stakes contexts, treating AI mainly as temporary scaffolding and emphasizing in-class work, oral defense, disclosure, and a protected non-AI path. Another position favors guided, risk-tiered integration, emphasizing that AI access is already widespread and uneven, that restriction-first approaches may be difficult to enforce consistently, and that some uses—such as tutoring, formative practice, structured feedback, selected STEM and language-learning tasks, and computer science—may be educationally valuable when designed well. A smaller but real position is more innovation-forward still, placing greater weight on teacher judgment and on adopting AI where it appears to improve learning, even when the evidence base is incomplete.

Short dissent-friendly formulation

The Committee agrees that Grades 6–12 should receive broader and more intentional AI exposure than younger students, but it does not agree on how broadly AI should be permitted in routine student work or how much policy should rely on restriction versus guided integration. The disagreement is best understood as a difference in posture: how to protect learning and integrity while also preparing older students for a world in which AI fluency is increasingly consequential.
