# WS-POL-A  –  Guiding Principles and Motivation

FCCPS should adopt an AI policy that is protective without being passive. Current evidence does not support unchecked adoption of generative AI in schools. Recent research raises credible concerns that AI can reduce independent effort, encourage passivity, and weaken durable learning when used inappropriately or too early (Bastani et al., 2024; Burns et al., 2026; Kosmyna et al., 2025). Those concerns should be taken seriously.

At the same time, FCCPS cannot responsibly assume that avoidance alone is an adequate educational strategy. AI tools are already widely available outside school, and the relevant divide may increasingly be between those who develop fluency and judgment in using AI and those who do not (VandeHei & Allen, 2026). If that divide continues to widen, then a policy centered only on restriction may unintentionally deepen inequity by leaving AI literacy and practical fluency to families with greater access, time, and outside support. For that reason, FCCPS should regulate AI from a design-first posture: begin with learning goals, developmental needs, human-accountability requirements, and equity concerns; design tasks, workflows, and safeguards accordingly; and then impose targeted restrictions where those goals cannot otherwise be protected.

## Virtues We Are Trying to Capture

FCCPS policy should be evidence-informed, but built for uncertainty. The district should be guided by evidence where evidence exists and proceed cautiously where it does not. The district should avoid both technological enthusiasm untethered from outcomes and a standard of proof so absolute that it prevents bounded learning, pilot-based governance, and policy adaptation.

FCCPS policy should be IB-aligned and learning-goal-first. AI should be introduced, limited, or withheld according to whether it supports the knowledge, habits, and human capacities FCCPS seeks to cultivate. School is not merely a workplace pipeline. It is a protected environment in which students develop reasoning, authorship, collaboration, and self-trust. In that sense, AI literacy includes learning when not to use AI and when human effort is educationally indispensable (Arlington Public Schools, 2025; International Baccalaureate, 2023).

FCCPS policy should be tailored to different grade bands and developmental stages. Younger students require stronger protection of foundational cognitive, social, and interpersonal development. Older students should gradually receive more guided exposure, more responsibility, and clearer expectations around disclosure, evaluation, and appropriate use. District policy should establish principles, while implementation should remain sensitive to age, subject, and task. See also the related grade-banded treatment in the WS-POL-E contribution by Adriana Palacios and the WS-POL-F contribution by David Berol.

FCCPS policy should be human-centered in design and implementation. Teachers should remain responsible for instruction and evaluation, students should remain responsible for their own learning, and AI should support rather than replace human reasoning and professional judgment. This principle is especially important in assessment, discipline, IEP/504-related processes, and other contexts where fairness and legitimacy depend on genuine human accountability. See also the related discussion in the WS-POL-E contribution by David Berol on staff and teacher use.

FCCPS policy should be transparent to students, families, and staff. Students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditionally allowed, or restricted. Families should receive accessible explanations of what kinds of AI uses occur in FCCPS and what safeguards apply. Staff should understand the difference between approved assistance and impermissible delegation. Transparency is a condition of trust, consistency, and fair enforcement. See also the WS-POL-H contribution by Adriana Palacios.

FCCPS policy should be equity-aware from procurement through implementation. AI can widen inequality by giving disproportionate advantage to those with outside access and fluency, while also exposing vulnerable students to weaker guardrails or lower-quality automation. At the same time, withholding structured opportunities to build AI literacy may itself produce inequitable outcomes. FCCPS should therefore treat equity as a full-lifecycle obligation spanning procurement, access, accommodations, instructional use, assessment, family communication, monitoring, and review. See also the WS-POL-I contribution by Adriana Palacios.

Finally, FCCPS policy should be flexible and evolving. AI capabilities, interfaces, and risks are changing too quickly for static rules to remain sufficient for long. Stable values and decision rules belong in policy; more detailed use guidance, examples, and approval criteria should be maintained administratively so they can evolve as evidence, tools, and risks change. See also the WS-POL-K contribution by Adriana Palacios.

## Idealized Goals for Regulating GenAI in FCCPS

FCCPS should articulate a policy logic that includes both permission and restriction.

First, policy should explain how allowing some uses of GenAI can support AI literacy, responsible digital citizenship, selected learning objectives, and educator effectiveness when paired with human oversight and clear pedagogical purpose. Students and staff need guided opportunities to understand how these tools work, where they fail, what they are good for, and what they should not be trusted to do.

Second, policy should explain how restricting some uses protects independent reasoning, authorship, assessment validity, fairness, privacy, and student well-being. Some educational tasks should remain intentionally non-AI, not because FCCPS is anti-technology, but because the value of those tasks lies in the human thinking and judgment they require (Bastani et al., 2024; Burns et al., 2026; Kosmyna et al., 2025).

These goals are not contradictory. A strong policy should state clearly when AI use serves learning and when it interferes with learning. FCCPS should neither normalize AI everywhere nor attempt to wall it off categorically. Instead, FCCPS should create conditions in which students learn when AI is useful, when it is misleading, when it is inappropriate, and when non-AI work is educationally essential.

## Challenges This Policy Must Address

FCCPS must govern under uncertainty. Risks of AI adoption are already visible, while benefits are often context-dependent and difficult to generalize. Capabilities may improve rapidly, but harms may also increase in scale and subtlety (Burns et al., 2026).

FCCPS must also address enforcement reality. AI use already occurs outside school-controlled environments. In unproctored contexts, rules based primarily on prohibition or detection may be difficult to enforce consistently and may encourage concealment rather than honest engagement. Overly rigid rules may also generate unfairness, including false accusations and uneven burdens on teachers. See the related concerns in the WS-POL-E contribution by David Berol regarding detector reliability and due process, and the WS-POL-H contribution by Adriana Palacios regarding practical implementation and communication.

Finally, FCCPS must address a strategic challenge. The district is responsible not only for preventing harm, but also for preparing students for a changing world. If AI fluency is becoming a meaningful differentiator in higher education, employment, and civic life, then inaction carries risk as well (VandeHei & Allen, 2026).

### Decision 1 — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee broadly agrees that policy must protect student learning and assessment integrity. There are differing perspectives on the relative emphasis between prioritizing restrictions to mitigate risk and enabling thoughtful integration of AI to support learning outcomes. These perspectives reflect a meaningful policy tradeoff for Board consideration.

In my view, FCCPS should adopt a design-first posture. Under a design-first approach, the district begins by asking: What is the learning objective? What human capacities should this task develop? What developmental stage are the students in? What risks to fairness, privacy, trust, or well-being are present? What human review and accountability are required?

Only then should the district decide whether AI use should be permitted, structured, limited, or prohibited. In some settings, the right answer will be prohibition or tightly bounded use. In others, the better answer will be teacher-guided integration, clearer disclosure expectations, better assessment design, stronger approval criteria, or bounded pilots with close monitoring.

## Pragmatic Constraints on Restriction of GenAI Applications

FCCPS should recognize that restriction has practical limits. In unproctored settings, full enforcement of GenAI restrictions is often unrealistic. Some summative assessments can be moved into proctored environments, but not all, and doing so carries instructional and operational costs. AI is also increasingly embedded in common productivity tools, making bright-line enforcement more difficult over time.

FCCPS should also recognize that fairness matters on both sides of policy. Students should not be expected to compete against widespread, non-transparent AI use by peers, but they also should not be subjected to unreliable accusations or opaque machine-mediated judgments. The district should therefore prefer control strategies that improve validity by design: clearer disclosure rules, better assessment design, more opportunities for demonstrated understanding in controlled settings, and governance mechanisms that reduce dependence on after-the-fact suspicion. See also the related discussion in the WS-POL-H contribution by David Berol on use categories and control strategies.

### Decision 2

FCCPS should steer between two extremes: unchecked adoption and impractical overrestriction.

That middle course should not be understood as compromise for its own sake. It should be understood as disciplined governance. FCCPS should restrict AI where learning, fairness, privacy, safety, or human dignity require it. FCCPS should also create structured pathways for responsible use where doing so advances educational goals, builds student and staff capability, and reduces the likelihood that opportunity will be determined by unequal access to AI fluency outside school.

In this sense, thoughtful integration is not the opposite of protection. Done well, it is part of how protection, preparedness, and equity are achieved together.
