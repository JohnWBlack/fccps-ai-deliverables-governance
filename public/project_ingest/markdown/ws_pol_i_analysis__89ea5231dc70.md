# WS POL I Analysis

WS-POL-I – Citation, Disclosure, and Process Evidence Analysis

The strongest overall conclusion is that WS-POL-I is one of the committee’s clearest transparency sections, but not one of its clearest consensus sections. There is broad agreement that students should not hide meaningful AI assistance and that FCCPS needs some disclosure expectations. There is not broad agreement on how much must be disclosed, when, by whom, and whether process evidence such as chat logs, drafts, checkpoints, or other workflow artifacts should be required as a routine expectation or only in selected contexts. The outline captures that directly: “There is general agreement that transparency around AI use is important. However, there is no consensus on requiring submission of chat logs.”

# How the committee got here

Meeting 3 helps explain why this section became so important. The committee repeatedly returned to the problem that AI can produce polished products that obscure what students actually know and can do. Elizabeth Chua warned that when students use AI as a tutor or to polish their writing, teachers may lose the feedback signal they need to understand where students really are in the learning process. John Black then linked that to assessment design and to the risk that poorly structured assignments can create a kind of competitive coercion to use AI. That conversation naturally leads to WS-POL-I: if product alone is no longer enough, what kinds of citation, disclosure, and process evidence are necessary to make learning visible again? (Meeting 3 transcript)

The survey shows that this issue had already crystallized by the time the committee moved into structured tradeoff language. The survey tested principles and postures for student disclosure, use transparency, design before bans, prevent cognitive offloading, visible thinking, and a tradeoff question on disclosure. That means WS-POL-I was never only about citation style. It sat at the junction of academic integrity, fairness, teacher workload, developmental appropriateness, and privacy. (Survey export)

# Where agreement appears strongest

The strongest committee agreement is on transparency as a principle. Multiple survey respondents approved or lightly revised statements like:

“Student disclosure. FCCPS will set grade-banded expectations for when and how students disclose AI assistance,”

and “Use transparency. FCCPS will set clear expectations for when students and staff disclose AI assistance in school work.”
Even respondents who objected to wording often did not object to disclosure in principle. Their objections were mainly about granularity, feasibility, or the wrong variables being used.

The V3 outline reflects that same center of gravity. It states:

students should acknowledge meaningful AI assistance when it contributes to ideas, structure, or content,

expectations should be developmentally appropriate and aligned with assignment goals and subject-area norms,

disclosure expectations should define what counts as meaningful AI assistance,

and communication to students must be clear enough to avoid confusion across classrooms.

A second area of convergence is that one-size-fits-all rules are risky. Even among respondents who favored stronger disclosure, several emphasized that disclosure depends on grade band, stakes, subject, and what the student actually used AI for. One of the clearest survey responses in the middle camp supported grade-banded disclosure, strongest for high-stakes work, and explicitly tied that to developmental appropriateness and feasibility. Another emphasized that expectations should be “simple and teachable,” for example a checkbox plus short reflection when AI meaningfully shaped ideas, structure, or analysis. (Survey export)

A third area of broad agreement is that process evidence can be useful, but that the committee is divided on how formalized it should become. The outline’s own phrasing treats this as a tradeoff rather than a settled norm: transparency versus privacy, practicality and burden on teachers, and the risk of over-standardizing diverse classroom practices.

# Main fault lines and the range of opinions

## 1. Universal disclosure vs. contextual disclosure

This is the clearest split in the accessible record.

A more restrictive and transparency-maximizing view argues for broad disclosure of any AI use. One survey respondent asked bluntly, “What is the argument against disclosure?” Another said the same disclosure rules should apply regardless of grade if a student is writing an essay. That camp sees disclosure as the default ethical baseline and tends to distrust attempts to create too many exceptions.

A more contextual view supports disclosure, but only when AI use is meaningful or material, or especially in high-stakes work. Several respondents preferred grade-banded disclosure or disclosure only when AI meaningfully shapes content, analysis, or structure. This camp is worried that universal disclosure rules can become noisy, burdensome, and insensitive to educational context. That middle position is probably the committee’s center of gravity, because it matches both the V3 outline and several survey responses. (Survey export)

## 2. Grade-banded disclosure vs. common district-wide rules

The committee clearly agrees that age and context matter, but there is real disagreement about whether that should produce different disclosure requirements by grade band.

The outline leans toward grade-banded disclosure. So do several survey responses, especially those aligned with the design-first middle. They argue that disclosure expectations should be developmentally appropriate and proportionate to stakes.

But one more restrictive respondent objected that different grade bands do not necessarily need different disclosure requirements, arguing that if a student is writing an essay, the same disclosure obligation may apply regardless of grade. That is a good example of a recurring committee divide: developmental tailoring versus cross-district clarity. (Survey export)

## 3. Process evidence as design support vs. process evidence as surveillance burden

This is probably the most important conceptual split inside WS-POL-I.

One camp sees process evidence—drafts, checkpoints, annotations, oral defense, reflections, or even chat logs—as part of a design-first approach to preserving validity. In the survey, the strongest middle respondents favored “design before bans,” saying FCCPS should protect assessment validity primarily through assessment design and process evidence, and apply restrictions only where validity cannot otherwise be preserved. For this camp, process evidence is not mainly about catching students after the fact. It is about structuring learning so authentic thinking remains visible.

The opposing camp is highly skeptical of this move. One survey response said process evidence is “easily fakable by AI” and rejected the “design versus restriction” framing as false because design constraints are themselves restrictions. That same respondent saw disclosure and process expectations as too dependent on undefined terms like “high-stakes” and too vulnerable to manipulation. This is a very important critique for your drafting because it means some committee members will not be reassured by arguments that process evidence is a softer alternative to restrictions. They may see it as both weaker and more ambiguous. (Survey export)

## 4. Chat logs specifically: no consensus

This is the one point the outline makes completely explicit: no consensus on requiring submission of chat logs.

The survey helps explain why. Some respondents wanted stronger transparency and appeared open to substantial evidence requirements. But others viewed anything resembling routine workflow submission as intrusive, impractical, or educationally unhelpful. Even many middle-position responses that favored design/process evidence did not endorse blanket log requirements. Their emphasis was usually on proportionate, assignment-specific evidence rather than universal logging.

So the committee’s actual divide is not simply “for or against chat logs.” It is more like:

no logs and no routine process documentation,

selective process evidence in specific contexts,

or stronger, more universal transparency expectations.

The accessible record suggests the middle option has the most support, while routine chat-log submission does not.

## 5. Citation vs. disclosure

This issue is more visible in other committee materials than in the survey alone, but the survey still reinforces it. Several principles and comments distinguish between acknowledging AI assistance and giving educators enough information to evaluate student work fairly. That maps closely onto the conceptual difference the committee has already been drawing elsewhere between citation as acknowledgment and disclosure as evaluative transparency.

The practical implication for WS-POL-I is that “citation” and “disclosure” should probably not be collapsed into a single undifferentiated requirement. The committee seems to want students to do both kinds of work:

acknowledge meaningful assistance,

and make enough of their use visible for teachers to assess learning fairly.

# What the survey says most clearly

The survey gives a fairly clean picture of three clusters.

A restriction-first / maximal-transparency cluster wants strong disclosure, is skeptical of process evidence as a substitute for restriction, rejects detectors, and wants fewer vague or context-sensitive standards.

A design-first / grade-banded transparency cluster supports disclosure and process evidence as part of learning design, wants the strongest expectations in high-stakes contexts, and prefers flexibility calibrated by grade band, subject, and task.

A more permissive / workload-sensitive cluster still supports transparency in principle, but worries more about overburdening teachers and students, and is more open to contextual flexibility, lighter disclosure, and optional use cases so long as learning goals are preserved.

The strongest evidence for the middle cluster comes from respondents who chose:

Option C on policy posture: design/process evidence first,

Option C on detectors: advisory only,

and grade-banded or high-stakes-focused disclosure models.
That cluster appears to be the most aligned with the wording that ultimately made it into V3. (Survey export)

# What this means for your drafting

For WS-POL-I, the strongest committee-grounded common ground is:

FCCPS should require some form of transparency around meaningful AI use.

Students should not present meaningful AI assistance as invisible.

Disclosure expectations should be clear enough to teach and apply.

Expectations should probably vary by grade band, task type, and stakes, even if members disagree on how much.

Process evidence may play a role, but the committee does not support a simple blanket rule requiring chat logs for everything.

Any disclosure/process-evidence model must balance fairness, privacy, feasibility, and instructional value.

Where you will need to choose your own position is on three questions:

whether disclosure should be triggered by any AI use or only meaningful AI assistance,

how far to go on grade-banded and high-stakes differentiation,

and whether process evidence should be treated mainly as a design tool or as an integrity backstop.
