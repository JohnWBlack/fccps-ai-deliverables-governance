WS-POL-H Student Use Categories & Controls

The Board should direct the schools to develop a scheme for categorizing generative AI use cases for the purpose of regulating what counts as permissible and impermissible student use. The adopted scheme should reflect a human-centered approach to AI. The use of AI technologies may be permitted and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but the people involved in ensuring the responsible use of such technologies must remain accountable to existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

There are many dimensions along which a particular AI student use case may be characterized for the purpose of responsible regulation. These include, for example:

A specification of the generative AI technology or technologies that will be used.

A description of the manner in which those AI technologies will be used (e.g., students may be supplied with a set of permissible prompts or prompt-forms to use when interacting with an AI chatbot; or their at-home use of an AI technology may require adult supervision).

A description of the sort of work or activity for which one will be using AI (e.g., generating a set of practice exercises and solutions, summarizing a passage from a text, assisting with the creation of images for an art project).

A description of how the work or activity for which one will be using AI is to be evaluated (e.g., how much does the assignment contribute to a student’s grade; will it be evaluated in comparison to similar work produced by other students in the class).

A description of how the use of AI technology is to be documented and reported (e.g., a student may be required to submit the transcript of their interaction with an AI chatbot; or they may be required to submit the specific prompts that were used to generate an image or other media).

It is not recommended that the Board take it upon themselves to enact specific regulatory rules for the permissible use of generative AI technologies. This is because the real-world scenarios that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Consequently, the concepts and categories by which generative AI use cases are sorted and the specific controls implemented to regulate their adoption by students should maximize the ability of teachers to exercise intelligent discretion on a case-by-case basis. At the same time, owing to the rapid rate of technological advance and the associated challenge of maintaining AI literacy over time, teachers must be given concrete and specific guidance as to how to exercise responsible control over the use of AI by students.

To strike a balance between the need for flexibility and concrete guidance, the Board should provide schools with a list of heuristics and guidelines to assist educators in determining whether or not to permit a specific AI use case. Below are two suggestions for heuristics of this sort:

The use of generative AI technologies should be allowed in all and only those cases in which the use of AI can be given a clear justification in terms of existing standards of sound pedagogy, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

Comment: Teachers should neither adopt a general policy of restricting or permitting all AI usage by students but should judge each case on its own merits. For example, there is now ample evidence indicating that immersion is an effective methodology for language acquisition at the primary school level. If the use of AI technology could lower the economic and psychological barriers to implementing this methodology in the classroom, then its use by students should not only be allowed but should be encouraged by teachers.

At the same time, teachers should not adopt an unquestioningly lax approach to AI usage. One reason for such laxity may stem from the view that all that an AI system can do is rote mechanical work, so that the unrestricted use of generative AI technologies will lead to students spending more time engaged in genuinely creative intellectual activity.  The problem with this view is that we do not yet understand the principles underlying human cognition and it may well be that no small part of our cognition operates on principles not wholly dissimilar from those employed by an LLM. There is a growing body of evidence to suggest, for example, that the ability to produce structured written language is significantly impacted by the language one has previously been exposed to in reading and speech, and not just by repeated practice composing text. Accordingly, teachers may have good reason to restrict AI usage for the purpose of forcing students to engage in what may seem to be mechanical work of the sort that an AI system can easily perform, provided that that work has been shown to promote a student’s intellectual growth and development.

Any informational content produced by generative AI technology should not be assumed to be reliable. Instead, such technologies should be treated as potentially inaccurate, biased, and opinionated sources of information.

Comment: When an LLM is asked a question, its response is not the result of an algorithm that has been devised to correctly answer questions of that sort (this is so, even when the question asked admits of such an algorithmic solution). So, even though they are implemented on a computer, LLMs are not computational devices in the traditional sense. LLMs instead generate output by employing statistical models that have been trained on very large datasets, and, as a result, they have computational limits that do not apply in the context of more traditional computing paradigms. Teachers, therefore, should not conceive of Gen AI tools as mere sources of information or calculating devices. Rather, they must be viewed as informational agents whose feedback must be vetted for both accuracy and robustness. For example, when it comes to making determinations as to when student’s should be allowed to use AI to assist in their school work, teachers should be thinking less about the informationally free vs. informationally restricted (or: open-book vs. closed-book) categorization of assignments, and more about the categorization of assignments into those in which collaboration with another agen

t may be beneficial to achieving learning goals and those in which it is not.
