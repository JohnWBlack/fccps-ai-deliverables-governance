Introduction

# Purpose of This Report

This report provides the Falls Church City Public Schools School Board with the committee’s integrated advice on how FCCPS should govern artificial intelligence in schools. AI now touches teaching, student work, privacy, communications, procurement, accessibility, equity, and district operations. The Board therefore needs a policy approach that is clear enough to guide practice, disciplined enough to protect students and preserve human accountability, and flexible enough to adapt as technology and evidence change.

# How to Read This Report

The first part of this report presents the committee’s policy-facing sections. These are organized around the major decisions the Board will need to consider and are written to highlight the clearest recommendations, the most important guardrails, and the principal tradeoffs that still require Board judgment.

The second part contains the workstream sections. Those sections provide the supporting foundation beneath the policy-facing recommendations: shared baseline assumptions, privacy and governance analysis, academic integrity logic, classroom scenarios, communications considerations, equity review, and implementation constraints. They are included to show the reasoning, evidence, and practical considerations that shaped the committee’s advice.

# Policy and Implementation

This report distinguishes between policy direction and implementation design. Board policy should state what FCCPS intends to protect, require, and uphold. Implementation determines how those commitments are carried out through approvals, workflows, procurement controls, staff training, communications, and oversight.

That distinction matters because AI is evolving quickly, school contexts differ, and some operational details will need to change as new evidence, tools, and risks emerge. Policy should therefore provide durable direction without trying to lock the district into overly specific administrative choices that may soon need revision.

# What This Report Is Designed to Help the Board Do

Taken together, this report is intended to help the Board evaluate how FCCPS should govern AI in a way that protects learning, fairness, safety, privacy, equity, and human accountability while remaining realistic about district capacity and workload.

The committee does not assume that every AI question calls for the same kind of response. Some issues warrant firm boundaries. Others call for structured discretion, phased implementation, or regular review as district experience grows. This report is designed to help the Board distinguish among those cases and adopt an approach that is principled, usable, and sustainable.

WS-POL-01 — Guiding Principles, Motivation, and Challenges

Question Before the Committee
What core values, educational aims, and risk assumptions should guide FCCPS AI policy from the outset?

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

how and when students learn to use and understand AI as a foundational tool for future study and work;

how and when our schools integrate AI into the design and delivery of education to enhance teaching and support to educators;

personalized learning and the overall student experience; and

how to capture the benefits that AI may create and how to mitigate the risks and community concerns posed by AI.

This section sets out the principles that should guide the AI policy and the challenges that a good policy must address.

# Guiding principles for the policy

IB-aligned. The policy should align with the values of the IB program, which seeks to develop thinking, research, communication, social, and self-management skills.

Evidence-informed. The policy should require uses of AI to be clearly connected to measurable outcomes and the ability of AI to reach those outcomes should be supported by evidence. The strength of the policy’s guidance should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate. Clearly defined learning goals drive decisions.

Risks-mitigated. The risks of incorporating AI into K-12 education are demonstrable and have life-long consequences. The policy should prioritize risk mitigation over the pursuit of speculative benefits.

Age-appropriate. The policy should account for differences in learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age.

Human-centered. Some responsibilities require reasoning, judgement, relationships, or accountability that can only be provided by a human. This includes responsibilities for instruction, learning, evaluation, and discipline. The policy should ensure that AI protects or enhances these inherently human roles.

Community-integrated. The policy should ensure that students, parents, and educators receive all relevant information promptly and transparently. It is better to over-share than to under-share. Community feedback should be prioritized for assessments of new AI-policy actions.

Equity-aware. The policy should treat equity as a developmentally sensitive obligation that spans procurement, access, accommodations, instructional use, assessment, family communication, monitoring, review, issue detection, and course correction. In performing these functions, the district should account for the disparate outcomes that decisions may have on different student demographics, including those with disabilities.

Evolutionary. The policy created by the school system should be revised frequently, because AI capabilities, interfaces, risks, and their associated community experiences are evolving rapidly.

# Challenges this policy should address

Benefits are uncertain for K-12 education. Some schools and researchers have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI. However, benefits are often context-dependent and difficult to generalize, with many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users. Since the benefits of AI in K-12 education are not clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions to take are uncertain.

AI introduces risks. AI exacerbates existing risks related to academic integrity by allowing the creation of long-form text, data privacy by aggregating large amounts of deeply personal data, and inequitable education outcomes as some adopters may be able to gain unfair advantages over their peers. Depending on how they are used, AI tools may also contribute to degradations in creativity, critical thinking, and memory retention of its users, which can lead to dependence on the tools. AI capabilities also introduce new risks, such as creating realistic media with content that is harmful to students or teachers, preventing people from seeking human mental health support, and novel cyber vulnerabilities such as students using prompt-injection attacks to influence their grades or private information getting leaked if the AI models use it for training. Whether adequate safeguards can be put in place remains an open question.

AI is ubiquitous and not reliably detectable. Students and educators will have access to AI, especially as it is increasingly integrated into productivity software, regardless of school policy. Those who adopt AI may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those who do not. It is not reliably possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI, making rules and policies difficult to enforce.

The future quality of AI tools is uncertain. The AI community generally believes that their tools are improving and will continue to improve. However, “platform decay” is a documented trend for software systems to get worse over time as companies reduce service costs and quality to maximize profit. Tools are likely to be updated rapidly. It is impossible to know whether specific tools the school adopts will improve or decay in the future.

# Overall implementation guidance

The use of AI in K-12 education is neither inherently beneficial nor harmful. As such, the committee does not support a blanket ban that may exclude students from AI literacy, though banning certain uses for specific age groups may be appropriate. Likewise, the committee does not support a rush to adoption that may expose students to harms which are poorly mitigated or currently unknown.

The policy should create processes that lead to an appropriate balance between preventing harm and preparing students for a rapidly changing world. These processes should prioritize being protective and mitigating harm but cannot be overly prohibitory or rigid. Rigid restrictions can make it harder to experiment with new approaches that may create benefits, leaving students and educators to develop AI literacy through means that are informal and potentially unsafe, rather than guided practice in a safe environment. The following recommendations will help the district find and maintain balance.

The policy should clearly articulate the major challenges posed by AI adoption in FCCPS and ensure that every policy action explicitly describes how it addresses one or more of the major challenges. Requiring explicit justifications for actions raises the quality of the decision-making process by incentivizing carefully reasoned actions and giving broader stakeholders concrete justifications to evaluate and respond to. This also leads to more balanced outcomes since actions that are overly permissive or restrictive are hard to justify. Further, this will ensure that the resulting actions are addressing major challenges, rather than just minor challenges or feel-good issues. The school district will create a much stronger set of actions and a far more compelling story to push for effective changes.

The policy should require an experimentation process for FCCPS to assess the effectiveness of any new AI action. This includes defining the specific benefits it expects to achieve, determining how those benefits will be measured, establishing a pre-implementation baseline, and assessing outcomes afterward. With this discipline, the district will produce decisions that are defensible, visible in their effects, and correctable when they fall short. Importantly, this experimental process enables iteration to converge on solutions that appropriately balance risks and rewards.

Taken together, these principles and challenges define the policy problem FCCPS must solve. The sections that follow provide guidance for addressing some of these policy issues, scoped to the intersection of AI with academic integrity, learning outcomes, equity, staff decision-making, and general community values. While critically important, there are many other AI-related issues that were beyond our scope, especially related to AI-risks that are not directly academic in nature. We have acknowledged some of these risks in the previously discussed challenges and we encourage the district to take their mitigation seriously. The specific policies and their implementation chosen by FCCPS will determine whether AI generates benefit or harm in our schools. Accordingly, the district should move deliberatively and with caution.

WS-POL-02 — Definitions

Question Before the Committee
Which definitions are necessary to make the policy clear, usable, and enforceable?

The following definitions establish shared language for this policy document. Where possible, definitions align with the Virginia Department of Education AI Guidelines (2024), the NEA AI Glossary (2025), existing FCCPS Regulation IIBEA-R, and applicable federal law. These definitions are intended to be functional rather than exhaustive and should be revisited as technology and regulatory language evolve.

Artificial Intelligence (AI). Any software system that uses machine learning, large language models, predictive algorithms, or similar techniques to generate content, make recommendations, assess student work, analyze student data, or support instructional or administrative functions. This includes both generative and non-generative applications.

Generative AI. A subset of AI that creates new content—including text, images, audio, video, or code—based on patterns learned from training data. Examples include large language models (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) and image generators (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney).

AI Detector. A software tool that uses statistical methods to assess the likelihood that a particular piece of content was authored by a generative AI model. AI detectors are probabilistic, not deterministic, and have documented limitations including elevated false positive rates for English language learners and students with disabilities (see WS-POL-03 for detector-use policy).

Approved Tool. An AI tool that has been reviewed and authorized for use in FCCPS through the district’s tiered risk approval process (see WS-POL-07). Approval may be conditional, time-limited, or restricted to specific use cases, grade bands, or user roles.

High-Stakes Use. Any use of AI where the output informs, contributes to, or could reasonably influence consequential decisions about a student, including but not limited to grading, placement, disciplinary action, IEP or Section 504 determinations, special education referrals, or eligibility decisions. Teacher-designated assignments may also be classified as high-stakes based on a rubric considering the stakes involved, the authenticity sensitivity of the task, and student developmental stage.

AI Assistance. Use of AI that meaningfully contributes to the substance, structure, or content of student work. This includes generative AI output, AI-enabled editing or paraphrasing tools, and AI tutoring or coaching interactions. It does not include predictive text, autocomplete, standard spell-check, or accessibility tools embedded in standard platforms.

Disclosure. A student’s acknowledgment that AI assistance was used in completing an assignment, including a description of how the tool was used and which portions of the work reflect AI contributions. Disclosure expectations are developmentally appropriate and defined by the classroom teacher in alignment with district guidance (see WS-POL-05).

Human Decision-Maker. A qualified FCCPS professional—such as a classroom teacher, school psychologist, speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, school social worker, counselor, or administrator—who retains final authority over consequential decisions affecting students. AI may support but not replace human judgment in these contexts (see WS-POL-06).

Vendor / Data Processor. Any third-party entity that provides AI tools or services to FCCPS and that receives, processes, stores, or has access to student data in connection with those services. Includes sub-processors to whom a vendor transfers student data.

Consumer-Grade AI Tool. An AI application designed for the public that has not been reviewed or approved through FCCPS’s governance process. Consumer-grade tools may not meet FERPA, COPPA, or FCCPS data governance standards and are not authorized for use with student data.

Algorithmic Bias. Systematic, unwanted unfairness in how an AI system detects patterns or makes decisions, often reflecting existing inequities related to race, disability, language, gender, or socioeconomic status. In an FCCPS context, algorithmic bias may manifest in AI detectors disproportionately flagging English language learners or neurodivergent students, or in predictive tools that reproduce historical patterns of over-identification or under-referral (see WS-POL-09).

Data Processing Agreement (DPA). A binding contract between FCCPS and a vendor specifying how student data may be collected, used, stored, shared, and deleted; the vendor’s obligations under FERPA, COPPA, and Virginia law; sub processor restrictions; breach notification requirements; and retention and deletion schedules. Required for all Tier 2 and Tier 3 AI tools (see WS-POL-07).

Tiered Risk Classification. A three-level framework for categorizing AI tools based on the sensitivity of data they process and the consequentiality of their use, ranging from Tier 1 (low risk, no student-identifiable data) to Tier 3 (high risk, personally identifiable information or consequential decisions). Classification determines governance, approval, consent, and monitoring requirements (see WS-POL-07).

Embedded AI Feature. An AI capability introduced within an already-approved platform (e.g., generative AI added to Google Workspace, Microsoft Teams, or Canvas) that processes student data in ways not covered by the original approval. Embedded AI features must be reviewed independently under the tiered framework; existing platform approval does not automatically extend to new AI functionality (see WS-POL-07, Escalation Rules).

Routine Educational Purpose. Under FERPA, an educational interest that a district has identified in its annual notification to families as a legitimate reason for accessing or sharing student education records without additional consent. FCCPS should determine which AI-related data uses fall within the routine-use exception and which require separate parental consent, particularly for Tier 3 tools (see WS-POL-07).

Bounded Pilot. A time-limited, scoped implementation of an AI tool or practice in a defined number of classrooms, grade bands, or buildings, with pre-established evaluation criteria, a defined endpoint, and a decision framework for expansion, continuation, or termination (see WS-POL-11).

AI Literacy. The knowledge and skills needed to understand how AI systems work, to use AI tools effectively and responsibly, and to critically evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, bias, and appropriateness. AI literacy is both a learning objective across grade bands and a professional development requirement for staff (see WS-POL-04 and WS-IPC).

Cross-References: WS-POL-07 (tiered risk model, DPA requirements, and vendor governance); WS-POL-06 (human decision-making safeguards); WS-POL-05 (disclosure and citation); WS-POL-03 (AI detector posture); WS-POL-09 (equity and algorithmic bias); WS-POL-11 (bounded pilots and reassessment); WS-IPC (AI literacy and PD); VDOE AI Guidelines (2024); FCCPS Regulation IIBEA-R; NEA AI Glossary (2025).

WS-POL-03 — Staff and Teacher Use of AI

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS govern educator use of AI so that it remains assistive and does not displace professional judgment?

# Purpose and Guiding Principles

The committee recommends that Falls Church City Public Schools govern staff and teacher use of artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI, in ways that preserve professional judgment, protect students, and maintain trust with families. The committee believes AI may play a bounded, assistive role for educators, and should augment rather than replace human responsibility for teaching, learning, and student-facing decisions.

# Appropriate Uses of AI by Staff and Teachers

The committee agrees that staff and teachers may use AI tools for supportive, preparatory, and operational purposes, provided that educators exercise meaningful human judgment and take responsibility for final outputs. Areas of broad agreement include permitting AI use for:

Lesson planning, brainstorming, and preparation of instructional materials, with human review and ownership.

Translation and creation of accessible materials, subject to human verification prior to distribution.

Administrative and operational support, such as scheduling or summarizing non-sensitive information.

Assistive technologies that do not generate substantive new content, such as spelling and grammar correction or transcription tools.

The committee believes these uses align with professional practice when they enhance effectiveness, equity, or access and do not displace educator responsibility.

The committee also agrees that staff and teachers should receive appropriate training before using AI tools with students and that equity, inclusion, and data-protection considerations must inform AI use across all contexts.

# AI Use in Student Feedback, Assessment, and Evaluation

If the school wishes to use AI for student evaluations, the district should advance cautiously via a pilot program. The committee agrees that the school may use AI for student evaluations under certain circumstances. However, the policy should prioritize clarity and trust over efficiency gains. Teachers and administrators must be fully accountable for the evaluation process and resulting judgements, regardless of AI use. Grading, assessment, integrity-related determinations, and other high-stakes decisions are core responsibilities of educators that should not be delegated to automated systems.

Even if it is possible to responsibly perform some aspects of these evaluations using AI tools, risks may still exist. For example, research shows that when others know a person has used AI for a task, their confidence in that person is undermined, even when the uses of AI are permitted (Schilke, O., & Reimann, M. (2025)). In the context of FCCPS, the use of AI for evaluations may erode the relationships among teachers, students, and parents.

We leave it to the district’s discretion whether the potential rewards may outweigh the risks for certain types of evaluation. If the district does proceed, it should go slowly and cautiously to ensure that the resulting implementation balances potential benefits to teachers with the concerns of the community and the costs of responsibly mitigating risks. Further, committee members had disagreement regarding the following issues that should be addressed as part of a pilot.

Evaluative vs. constructive feedback. Most committee members draw a distinction between these two categories (see earlier definitions section). Committee members have a greater degree of concern with integrating AI into the evaluative feedback process. Students obtaining constructive feedback from an AI tool is less controversial, assuming there will be transparency about the AI origins of such feedback.

Parent’s ability to opt-out. Most committee members believe that parents should have the option to opt-out of AI use in student evaluations. Their reason is that many parents and students may have strong practical or ethical reasons to avoid the use of AI. Such concerns would likely not be allayed by assurance that teachers were reviewing the AI material. One implementation option could be an annual form distributed to parents that allows them to choose which uses of AI may be applied to their child.  Some committee members could not support such a recommendation because the administrative burden of its implementation is unknown and potentially high if not done properly.

Disclosure records. Most committee members believe that stakeholders should have the right (1) to know when AI substantively contributed to student evaluations and (2) to inspect the record of AI use that contributed to specific evaluative decisions of students. A robust disclosure policy provides transparency that mitigates risks of human over-reliance on potentially biased AI-generated outputs. It also mitigates the degradation of trust in staff’s evaluations of students. Some committee members could not support such a recommendation for the same reason as the opt-out policy.

# Use of AI Detectors and Academic Integrity Tools

The policy should prohibit the use of AI detection tools. The committee recommends a precautionary approach, initially prohibiting the use of AI detectors due to risks of false positives, algorithmic bias against certain student groups, confirmation bias when used by staff, and psychological or reputational harm to students. Further, the utility of an AI detector is undermined in situations where the application of AI to assignments is partially permitted; for example, if a teacher may use AI to give feedback or a student may use AI for grammar assistance, the presence of these permitted AI signatures confounds the ability of a detector to find misconduct. The committee also notes that the IB Academic Integrity Policy discourages use of AI detection software. Instead, academic integrity is better supported through human-centered practices such as conversation, assessment design, and clear expectations.

The prohibition could be lifted in a future version of the policy, provided certain conditions are met. Such conditions may include 1) a tool that keeps up with rapidly evolving AI models and is regularly certified as accurate by independent auditors, 2) false positive rates that are very low across all relevant demographic groups, not merely low ‘on average’, 3) a method for distinguishing between acceptable and unacceptable uses of AI within a single document, and 4)  a process for student integrity determinations that does not rely on the AI-detection tool as its primary evidence.

WS-POL-04 — Student Use

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS structure developmentally appropriate student AI use so that AI supports learning without displacing authorship, reasoning, or integrity?

The committee recommends that FCCPS organize student AI-use policy by grade band, with strong limits on AI use with younger children and broader use of AI, with rules and guardrails, with older children. The committee’s guidance is discussed in terms of younger and older students, and encourages the individual schools to use their expertise and discretion to determine what is developmentally appropriate at the specific grade bands band (PreK, K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12), especially in the middle grade bands.

Across grade bands, the committee supports clear categories of permitted, conditional, and prohibited student AI use, with concrete examples published for students and teachers. For further detail see WS-POL-05.

The committee consistently emphasized that FCCPS must continue to prioritize foundational cognitive, social, and relational learning, and uses of AI must be safe, ethical, developmentally appropriate, and consistent with learning and educational goals. The Board should consider both academic and mental health in their student use policy.  The committee considered several broad areas of student use of AI: 1) AI Literacy, 2) Personalized Learning, and 3) Direct Student Use: Educator-mediated and Independent use of GenAI. The committee also recommends a monitoring framework for Student Use.

# AI Literacy

There was agreement among the committee that AI Literacy is critical for all students (PreK-12) and FCCPS needs to integrate AI Literacy into the curriculum in developmentally-appropriate ways.  For younger grades AI Literacy would focus on learning about AI and technology, rather than using AI. There are many AI Literacy frameworks that use the FCCPS school bands (e.g., UNESCO, AI4K12) and the committee encourages education practitioners to consider these frameworks when adopting an AI Literacy curriculum.

The committee recommends FCCPS integrate developmentally appropriate AI Literacy across all grade bands and engage in vertical planning across grades and schools. UNESCO defines AI literacy as “the knowledge, skills, and attitudes that enable individuals to understand, engage with, and critically evaluate AI technologies in a responsible and ethical manner.” Core components include: 1) Understanding AI Fundamentals (data, algorithms, machine learning), including its applications and limitations, 2) Ethical Evaluation (bias, privacy, accountability, social justice), 3) Responsible Engagement (interacting safely and effectively, evaluating AI generated content), and 4) Human-Centered Mindset (understanding when and how to use AI to ensure that AI supports rather than replaces human decision-making). AI Literacy includes foundational concepts needed for understanding AI and is more than interacting with GenAI.  For example, in the AI4K12 (ai4k12.org)  framework, K-2 students are taught about human sensory organs and also about sensors on computers, phones, and other devices.

Of note, foundations for AI Literacy was a student use case that the committee agreed was appropriate and important for students in the younger grade bands (PreK, K-2, and 3-5), whereas many other areas of student AI use had more caution for younger students. AI Literacy in younger grade bands involves learning about AI and does not mean that students will be directly interacting with AI. Indeed, the committee felt that the main student use of AI in younger grade bands should be related to learning about AI as part of AI Literacy and any direct usage required education-mediation and should be limited.

# Personalized Learning

Personalized learning through approved software packages were generally viewed as acceptable to the committee.  Support for the use of broad GenAI tools for personalized support was not acceptable for younger students, but could be acceptable, with rules and guardrails, for older students. Personalized learning may occur in the context of specific AI-driven software (e.g., DreamBox, Lexia, Zearn) as well as broad GenAI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude). For software packages, vendor/procurement guidelines from relevant policies should be followed.

Notably, there were strong concerns in the younger grade bands about students placing too much trust in an AI partner and having trouble distinguishing humans and AI.  For this reason, the committee favored maintaining stronger boundaries around conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating AI uses that may blur the distinction between human and machine interaction or encourage younger students to treat AI as a buddy, coach, co-teacher, or authority.  These concerns apply to both specific software packages and broader tools.

For students in older grade bands, there was recognition that GenAI holds promise as a tool for providing on-the-spot clarification and tutoring when students are working through homework.  The committee encourages teachers to support students in how to craft GenAI queries that are less likely to “spoil” the homework problems so that GenAI may be used in a manner that promotes learning.

# Direct Student Use: Educator-Mediated and Independent Use of AI

The Committee generally agreed that: 1) learning goals remain primary, 2) AI use should become broader as age and student judgment increase, and 2) that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted. Broadly speaking, the committee recommends that any interactions with AI in younger children are educator-mediated and very limited, whereas a mix of educator-mediated and independent use of AI is appropriate in older children.

# Younger Children

For younger grade bands, there was general agreement among the committee that educator demonstrations of AI use in the context of learning about AI and AI Literacy was acceptable. The committee recommends that the default posture for younger students interacting with GenAI is to prohibit it. Other uses may be acceptable when there is evidence of an educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained, when there is close adult supervision, when learning and motivation remain, and there are minimal risks to the students in terms of safety, ethics and learning (e.g., mistaking AI tutors for humans, mental health risks).

In general, the committee recommends student interaction in younger grades with GenAI should be very limited and when used it must be educator-mediated. The early grades are not the place for routine independent chatbot use, free-form prompt experimentation, or unsupervised AI-supported task completion. Where students do interact directly with a system, that interaction should be brief, bounded, and supervised, with the teacher or another adult framing what the tool is doing and helping students interpret the output. It is particularly important, given that younger children may be prone to: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”

# Older Students

In addition to educator-mediated use of GenAI tools, the committee recognized that many students in older grade bands will encounter or use GenAI both in and out of school for learning support and schoolwork. For older grade bands, much of the committee discussion focused on independent use of GenAI and how permissible or restrictive policies should be. Regardless of whether committee members were restrictive or permissive, there was agreement that: 1) AI use in this band must support learning rather than replace it, and 2) students needed clear communication about when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted.  There was also a recognition that some learning scenarios may be especially well suited to assistance from GenAI and others may be impeded by assistance from GenAI.  The Committee recommends that the Board direct the schools to identify these learning scenarios in detail. Where AI use is optional, students should have a meaningful non-AI path or equivalent alternative.

Notably, there was a range of views on how permissible and restrictive student use policies should be in these grade bands.  On the one hand, there were deep concerns that GenAI can take over the essential thinking that writing is meant to teach.  Accordingly, it was recommended that teachers should set careful limits to ensure GenAI acts as a temporary support rather than a permanent replacement for fundamental thinking.  One specific recommendation was that educators vary their restrictions across assignments to prevent students from becoming dependent on technology for any single part of the writing process. There was a call to limit GenAI during early stages, like brainstorming and outlining, to endure students don’t skip the fundamental thinking to develop their own ideas.

On the other hand, there  is also value in providing structured opportunities for students to build AI fluency and judgment under teacher guidance in preparation for the future. Some on the committee recommended that AI should be integrated into student learning where it supports educational goals, builds literacy and judgment, and prepares students for the environments they are entering. Restrictions remain necessary in some contexts, especially where unaided performance is central. But the district should not define policy for older grade bands mainly by what students must not do. It should define it by what students should learn to do well, ethically, transparently, and with increasing independence.

# Monitoring Student AI Use: Audit Framework for Equity & Accountability Monitoring Student AI Use: Audit Framework for Equity & Accountability

The long-term risks and benefits of AI usage by students is currently an active area of research, and the committee recommends that FCCPS monitor and evaluate student AI use and conduct pilot programs.  Attention should to be given to the tension between giving students guided opportunities to practice effective and ethical use of AI versus the concern that students become dependent on AI and AI eroding mental health and human-centered, “durable skills”, such as learning, resilience, creativity, and critical thinking.

Consistent with the audit framework established for special education compliance, student AI use should be monitored systematically across grade levels and subjects. This ensures:

Transparency: Families understand when and how AI is used in their child's learning

Equity: Disaggregated data reveals disproportionate impact on vulnerable populations (students with disabilities, multilingual learners, students of color, low-income students)

Accountability: Schools can demonstrate whether AI use advances learning goals or creates unintended harm

This monitoring framework should be integrated into regular compliance reviews and updated annually as part of the district's assessment cycle.

WS-POL-05 — Student Use Categories, Controls and Disclosure

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS categorize student AI use cases so that expectations are clear, consistent, and workable across a variety of classroom settings?

What controls and disclosure policies are needed to distinguish acceptable AI use cases from those that undermine learning objectives?

# Committee Recommendations

The Committee recommends that the Board:

Define a simple set of broad categories into which AI use cases can be sorted for the purpose of providing a common regulatory language in which to enact clear and straightforward policies regulating AI use.

Refrain from enacting specific rules determining how specific AI use cases should be categorized, so as to maximize teacher flexibility and discretion.

Provide schools with a list of heuristics and guidelines, targeted to each grade band and developmental stage, to assist educators in exercising responsible control over the student use of AI.

Advise the schools against implementing policies that focus on citation as the primary mechanism for responsible attribution of authorship to generative AI systems.

Direct the schools to enact a disclosure policy for AI use that strikes a balance between the competing considerations of practicability, student privacy, incentivization of compliance, and teacher empowerment.

# Committee Brief

The Committee recommends that the Board direct the schools to develop a scheme for categorizing generative AI use cases for the purpose of regulating what counts as permissible and impermissible student use. The adopted scheme should reflect a human-centered approach to AI that aims to strike a reasonable balance between the potential risks and benefits that come with the utilization of AI technologies in the classroom. The use of AI technologies should be permitted and even encouraged where it supports developmentally appropriate learning and productivity, but the responsible use of such technologies must be judged against existing standards of sound pedagogical decision-making, evaluative accuracy, and equity and fairness.

The aim of a categorization scheme is to reduce confusion and reinforce consistent expectations across a variety of classroom settings. Thus, every effort should be made to communicate policies regulating the student use of AI in ways that are understandable and accessible to families. Overly complex rules or disclosure requirements may create unnecessary obstacles to compliance, resulting in new inequities and limiting meaningful engagement. The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Board should define a simple set of broad categories into which AI use cases can be sorted, such as:

No AI usage without administrative exception

No AI usage without express teacher allowance

AI permitted unless otherwise restricted

Such a broad-level categorization scheme will help the schools to devise uniform policies by providing a common framework in which to draw boundaries around potential AI use cases. It will also provide a common regulatory language in which to communicate these policies, in the clearest terms possible, to both students and families.

Apart from specifying such an overarching regulatory framework, however, the Committee does not recommend that the Board take it upon themselves to enact more specific rules determining what should count as a permissible use of generative AI. This is because the real-world scenarios that educators will confront in the classroom will bring with them their own set of context-specific challenges. Consequently, the concepts and categories by which generative AI use cases are sorted and the specific controls implemented to regulate their adoption by students should maximize the ability of teachers to exercise intelligent discretion on a case-by-case basis.

Nevertheless, owing to the rapid rate of technological advance and the associated challenge of maintaining AI literacy over time, teachers must be given concrete and specific guidance as to how to exercise responsible control over the use of AI by students. To strike a balance between the need for flexibility and concrete guidance, the Board should provide schools with a list of heuristics and guidelines, targeted to each grade band and developmental stage, that can assist educators in exercising responsible control over the student use of AI. The following sample heuristics are not prescriptive, but are merely meant to illustrate the level of generality at which guidance to the schools should be supplied: :

The use of generative AI technologies should be allowed only in those cases in which the use of AI can be given a clear justification in terms of existing standards of sound pedagogy.

Example: There is some evidence to suggest that immersion may be  an effective methodology for language instruction in certain educational settings. If the use of AI technology can lower the economic and psychological barriers to implementing this methodology in the classroom, then its use by students may be allowed.

Example: There is some evidence to suggest that the ability to produce structured written language is significantly impacted by the language one has previously been exposed to in reading, and not just by repeated practice composing text. Accordingly, teachers may have good reason to restrict AI usage for the purpose of forcing students to read whole texts, and not just AI generated summaries, since this has been shown to promote a student’s intellectual growth.

Instead of regulating AI usage exclusively through restrictions on student use and disclosure policies, teachers and staff should be encouraged to explore alternative pedagogical methodologies that could circumvent the need for such controls.

Example: Instead of enacting policies restricting the use of AI to aid in the composition of written work, teachers might instead assign more frequent in-class writing exercises to assess students’ unaided writing skills in a controlled environment. Additionally, they may explore a “reverse lecturing” or “flipped classroom” paradigm, where students are delivered instructional content outside of the classroom and produce their work during class in a supervised setting where AI assistance is unavailable.

The Committee recommends that the Board advise the schools against implementing policies that focus on citation as the primary mechanism for responsible attribution of authorship to generative AI systems. Text that is generated by AI doesn’t qualify as a source, and so should not be directly cited. In fact, AI literacy programs should be devised, in part, to discourage teachers and students from interacting with these technologies as if they were accessing reliable reference materials.

Disclosure, rather than citation, is the more appropriate frame in which to address the responsible disclaiming of the sources of student work. The main aim of a disclosure policy is to help maintain a culture of honesty, trust, and responsibility in the academic community by requiring students to fully disclose any external aids, resources, or borrowed material that they utilize in their schoolwork. That being said, the Committee acknowledges that a general policy requiring full disclosure of any student use of generative AI technology for school-related tasks or activities may not be practicable. In addition, there may be other flaws with such a policy, including its potential to infringe upon student privacy, the risk of over-standardizing classroom practices, and the incentivization for non-compliance due to negatively biased perceptions of AI-assisted work.

The issue of responsible disclosure of AI use is a nuanced policy question about which the Committee did not arrive at a shared consensus. In the view of the Committee, however, any such policy must:

Acknowledge the increasing ubiquity of AI technologies and their inevitable integration into content generation workflows;

Protect student privacy;

Discourage dishonesty and non-compliance through equitable enforcement; and

Empower teachers to make assignment choices on pedagogical rather than policing grounds.

WS-POL-06 — Students with Disabilities and Human Decision Safeguards

Question Before the Committee
What safeguards are needed to ensure AI remains assistive and never substitutes for qualified human judgment in sensitive or consequential contexts affecting students?

Protect Students with Disabilities and Multilingual Learners
Ensure AI use does not conflict with IEPs, 504 Plans, or language supports. Legitimate uses such as translation, text-to-speech, or paraphrasing for access should not be broadly prohibited through AI policies or integrity rules.

Concerns were raised that blanket bans or rigid integrity rules may unintentionally penalize students who rely on assistive or language supports. Equity requires distinguishing misuse from legitimate access needs.

Possible Implications:

Require Special Education and English Learner expertise in AI policy review.

Prohibit AI guidelines that negate approved accommodations without formal review – this could be an overarching rule when adopting AI related policy.

# Students with Disabilities and Human Decision Safeguards

IEP and 504 Plans must be developed by qualified professionals. Consistent with FERPA, student information or PII may not be put into a generative AI tool.  Staff may use generative AI as a starting point to brainstorm about potential goals and accommodations, but staff cannot rely solely on this output. WS-POL-07 — Protection of Student Privacy, Data Governance, and Vendor/Procurement Governance

Question Before the Committee
Protection of Student Privacy, Data Governance, and Vendor/Procurement Governance

What privacy, data-governance, and vendor-review requirements should FCCPS impose before AI tools are approved or adopted?

The committee recommends that FCCPS establish a structured governance framework for AI tools that protects student privacy, ensures responsible data practices, and creates clear accountability for vendors and staff. This section draws on WS-DPS workstream deliverables, Virginia HB1186/SB394 (enrolled March 2026), FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, Section 504, and VDOE AI Guidelines (2024).

# 1. Guiding Principles

The committee believes that student data protections for AI should meet or exceed existing FERPA and state-law requirements. The following principles should guide all AI-related data practices in FCCPS:

All AI tools processing student data must comply with FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, Section 504, and Virginia student data privacy statutes.

Data minimization: AI tools should collect only the data necessary for their approved educational function and retain it no longer than required.

No student data collected through AI tools may be used for non-educational purposes, including advertising, profiling, or sale to third parties.

FCCPS must maintain transparency with families about which AI tools are in use, what data they collect, and how that data is protected.

Vendor compliance is a condition of continued use, not a one-time checkpoint.

# 2. Tiered Risk Model for AI Tool Approval

The committee recommends a three-tier classification framework, consistent with VDOE tiered approval guidance, to match governance requirements to risk level:

Tier 1 — Low Risk: Tools that do not collect, store, or process any student-identifiable information. Require department-level approval and basic instructional-use documentation.

Tier 2 — Moderate Risk: Tools that process de-identified or aggregated student data, or that receive student work artifacts. Require IT review, a signed Data Processing Agreement (DPA), and annual recertification.

Tier 3 — High Risk: Tools that collect, store, or process personally identifiable student information, connect to FCCPS identity systems, or inform consequential decisions. Require full privacy impact review, executed DPA with sub-processor restrictions, parental notice, and governance-office approval.

# 3. Escalation Rules

The following conditions automatically escalate a tool to Tier 3 review regardless of vendor assurances:

Identity linkage: Any tool requiring student accounts or connecting to FCCPS identity systems (SSO, SIS, LMS).

Consequential use: Any tool used to inform placement, grading, intervention, discipline, or special education decisions.

Third-party data sharing: Any tool whose vendor shares student data with sub-processors or downstream analytics providers, unless all sub-processors are contractually bound to equivalent protections.

Embedded AI features: When an already-approved platform introduces new AI capabilities (e.g., generative AI, predictive analytics), those features must be reviewed independently. Existing platform approval does not extend to new AI functionality.

Consumer-grade tools: AI applications not reviewed through the FCCPS governance process are prohibited for use with student data. This is both a governance and equity issue—consumer-grade tools lack the privacy protections, accessibility standards, and bias review that FCCPS requires.

# 4. Vendor and Procurement Requirements

The committee recommends that FCCPS require the following as conditions of AI tool approval for Tier 2 and Tier 3 tools:

Executed Data Processing Agreement specifying data use limitations, retention and deletion schedules, breach notification procedures, and sub-processor restrictions.

Vendor commitment that student data will not be used for model training, advertising, or any purpose beyond the contracted educational function.

Annual vendor recertification confirming continued compliance, with provision for accelerated review if the vendor’s data practices, ownership, or product capabilities materially change.

Documented accessibility review for tools used with students with disabilities, ensuring alignment with IDEA and Section 504 (see WS-POL-06).

# 5. Non-Negotiable Constraints

The committee identifies the following as non-negotiable:

No AI tool may process student-identifiable data without an executed DPA on file with FCCPS.

No vendor may use student data for model training, product development, or any purpose outside the contracted educational function.

No AI-generated output may serve as the sole or primary basis for consequential decisions about a student (see WS-POL-06).

Tools used in special education contexts require additional review—including clearance by qualified professionals (school psychologists, SLPs, OTs, social workers, counselors), bias assessment, and family notification specific to AI use in the student’s program.

# 6. Decisions for Board Consideration

The committee identifies three tradeoffs for Board resolution: (1) whether FCCPS should adopt a proactive annual notice to families identifying all AI tools and their data practices, or affirm that existing FERPA compliance is sufficient; (2) what the consequence should be when staff use a consumer-grade or unapproved AI tool with student data; and (3) who within FCCPS is responsible for ongoing governance, vendor review, and compliance monitoring—and what capacity is needed to sustain this work.

Cross-References: WS-POL-02 (Definitions); WS-POL-06 (Students with Disabilities and Human Decision Safeguards); WS-POL-09 (Equity in Our Community); WS-POL-11 (Reassessment and Updating of Policy); WS-DPS workstream deliverables (tiered risk model, procurement checklist, top risks); Virginia HB1186/SB394 (2026).

WS-POL-08 — Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback

Question Before the Committee
What notice, explanation, consent, and feedback processes should FCCPS provide to build trust and accountability around AI use?

The committee believes that responsible adoption of AI in schools requires strong accountability structures for governance and incident response, plain-language communication about how AI is being used, and effective mechanisms for students, families, and staff to provide feedback when concerns, harms, or new risks emerge.

# Accountability

The policy should create an accountability structure that gives a single high-level official the responsibility for the success or failure of AI in FCCPS. Compared to diffuse responsibility, where no one is clearly accountable, connecting a single person’s reputation to final outcomes creates incentives for better decisions, faster problem recognition, and stronger drives to correct course when needed. The accountable official should have an enterprise-level view of AI-related implementation issues (e.g., learning outcomes, academic integrity, mental health, privacy, procurement, tech support). Second, they should have authority to approve or veto AI-related actions; responsibility without authority is a recipe for failure. Third, they should have sufficient tenure that they will still be in the role when the effects of their decisions come to fruition.

# Consent

The policy should establish a process for identifying the uses of AI that should be consent-based. Even if the use of AI is broadly accepted by the community, some parents will not want their children using AI tools or having AI tools used on their children in any significant capacity. FCCPS should recognize that gathering consent is a mechanism to protect the interests of minority stakeholders, particularly in student-facing AI uses that materially shape a student’s learning experience, evaluation, or support, including conversational, tutoring, or coaching usesy

# Disclosure

The policy should grant parents and students the right to know the role that AI played in critical decisions, such as those contributing to final grades, disciplinary actions, and IEPs. For example, such decisions could be labeled to indicate whether it was produced (A) without any AI involvement, (B) with only non-substantive AI contributions, or (C) with substantive AI contributions. For Category C documents, stakeholders should have the right to inspect the record of AI use that contributed to the decision. This policy would require new retention and request procedures for AI-related documents. This type of disclosure policy encourages accountability within the school and increases community trust.

# Notice and Feedback

The policy should adopt the Rapid-Alert & Re-Assessment Pathway, which is a mechanism for gathering and responding to concerns from teachers, administrators, parents, and students. It was created with support from State Council of Higher Education for Virginia (SCHEV) for use with AI-adoption. It proceeds as follows.

Invite reports from every stakeholder through a one-click online form and a dedicated helpline.

Triage submissions within 48 hours (e.g., data-privacy breach, discriminatory outputs, instructional harm) and posts a public “under review” notice for the tool or issue in question.

Convenes an expert review panel to decide whether to (a) maintain approval, (b) approve with conditions, or (c) suspend clearance pending vendor remediation.

Publishes an outcome summary so that stakeholders have a shared understanding of decision.

Logs all incidents in an open dashboard to spot systemic risks and guide future policy updates.

WS-POL-09 — Equity and Accessibility in AI Use Across FCCPS Community

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS ensure that AI use advances equity and accessibility rather than reinforcing existing disparities?

As AI tools increasingly influence instruction, assessment, and administration, FCCPS must ensure that their use advances equity, access, and student well-being. Equity considerations should be embedded from the start of all AI-related decisions, not added after tools or practices are already in place. The following policy recommendations outline priority considerations for those with authority over district-level equity and technology policy.

The committee also recognizes that access to AI tools already varies significantly outside of school environments. As a result, policies that rely primarily on restrictions within school may unintentionally widen gaps between students who have access to AI outside of school and those who do not.

The committee recommends the current working policy statement surrounding equity in community:

FCCPS will evaluate AI tools and AI usage for equity and accessibility from the procurement stage to the classroom, including administrative uses. FCCPS will monitor and evaluate AI usage in terms of equity and access. FCCPS will collect data on reported bias incidences. Monitoring and evaluation should be incorporated into relevant existing school structures. The committee must include, but is not limited to, School Technology Professionals, Special Education and English Language Learner coordinators.

Topics related to equity will be included as part of AI Literacy for students and staff. This will include understanding how models are trained and how to identify algorithmic bias.

# Key Policy Recommendations

Make Equity a Foundational Requirement
Adopt a clear policy statement affirming that equity, accessibility, and disability accommodations are core criteria for all AI-related decisions—from procurement through classroom practice and administrative use. Students are a protected and vulnerable population requiring heightened safeguards.

Adopt Guiding Principles with Structured Review
Pair district-level AI Equity & Access Guiding Principles with a equity review process for AI policies, tools, and workflows. Guiding principles articulate intent; the Board, in consultation with school experts, should consider the merits of a structured review to ensure consistent application without overburdening staff.

The committee recommends the following as a working guiding principle: How can we ensure that our approach to AI sufficiently continues to uphold the standards and values on learning that align with the IB program, and that AI is used in the service of those goals, including equity?

Prohibit High-Stakes Decisions Based Solely on AI Outputs
Disallow disciplinary, academic integrity, or profiling decisions based only on AI detection tools or automated risk indicators. Require human judgment, contextual evidence, and equitable dispute processes.

## Recognize AI Literacy as an Equity Issue

Commit to district-supported learning that helps students and staff understand AI limitations, bias, and responsible use—without assuming advanced technical expertise or creating unfounded expectations.

Equity should be part of the AI literacy curriculum and integrated with IB mindsets at an appropriate age. Sample issues include:

How are models trained? How does bias enter AI models? We need to teach our kids when to trust themselves and in doing so, how to better trust AI results where appropriate or where there’s a logical human/computer intersection.

AI as a tool could be an early education message. Let’s put our kids in the driver’s seat here.

## Access and Opportunity

The committee recognizes that unequal access to AI tools outside of school may create unintended advantages for some students.

Policies that restrict AI use within school, without addressing differences in access outside of school, may reinforce or widen these disparities.

The committee recommends that FCCPS consider how to provide equitable access to AI tools and guided experiences within the school environment, so that all students have the opportunity to develop AI literacy and related skills.

Implement Feasible Monitoring and Feedback Loops
Track equity indicators (e.g., access barriers, accommodation conflicts, integrity disputes, bias reports, disparities in outcomes across student populations) using existing structures where possible and use findings to revisit policy when risks emerge.

FCCPS should ensure adequate resources for monitoring with school input on most metrics.

See WS-POL-07 for a detailed risk and mitigation table identifying specific monitoring responsibilities and suggested owners, including equity-related risks such as algorithmic bias in assessment and unreviewed AI features in already-approved platforms.

# Closing Note

Equitable AI policy is not about maximizing AI use, but about deciding when, how, and whether AI should be used when considering the risks and benefits. By centering equity early, FCCPS can reduce unintended harm, support educators, and uphold its values while responsibly navigating rapid technological change.

WS-POL-10 — Workforce and Budget Implications

Question Before the Committee
What staffing, workload, training, and budget realities must FCCPS account for in adopting and governing AI?

The Committee agrees that it is technically feasible for FCCPS staff to use Generative AI to substantially reduce the amount of staff time spent on a variety of work-related tasks (e.g., writing lesson plans, administrative materials, and e-mails to parents). However, we believe it is premature to forecast budget benefits from this capability.

This is due to ongoing uncertainty about:

The degree to which the community will tolerate AI-generated content in various school settings.

The amount of extra user effort necessary to assure work quality and authenticity when AI tools are introduced into the user workflow.

The overhead costs of AI governance, such as:

Staff training

Implementation support

Vetting tools

Vendor oversight,

Privacy/security review

Incident triage

Evaluating and updating AI policy

The Committee suggests investing resources in centralized AI governance for FCCPS. This standing committee should reflect multidisciplinary membership to ensure comprehensive oversight across the district, including department chairs from core and elective subjects, representatives from special education and mental health services, K-12 grade band representatives, and community stakeholders. This structure ensures that technology policy decisions account for diverse student needs, developmental stages, and subject-area expertise. Committee members whose work occurs outside of contracted hours should receive stipends; work that can be integrated into existing professional development time or department chair responsibilities need not require additional compensation. Staffing and budget allocation should include dedicated support for committee operations and may require additional FTE position(s), to be determined based on pilot results and district assessment of implementation needs.  We hope that sustained attention will avoid inconsistency, avoid duplication of effort, and thereby decrease the costs associated with using AI tools.

We foresee the following trade-off: As greater effort is invested in aligning AI use with community expectations, anticipated efficiency gains may evaporate. As efficiency gains are prioritized, staff will feel pressure to cut corners in their implementation of AI governance.

Assuming there are net efficiency gains from AI use, the Board should not treat them as justification to reduce the teacher workforce or increase teacher responsibilities. AI-driven net efficiency gains should be reinvested in: (1) Improved student learning experience and 2) Improved working conditions for FCCPS staff.

WS-POL-11 — Reassessment and Updating of Policy

Question Before the Committee
How should FCCPS reassess and update AI policy as technology, evidence, and school practice evolve?

The committee recommends that AI policy be adaptive and regularly updated in response to evolving technology, emerging risks, and observed outcomes in practice. AI policy should not be treated as static. Instead, the district should establish a clear and predictable approach for reviewing new uses, evaluating impacts, responding to incidents, and revising policy and guidance over time.

The committee recognizes that AI capabilities are developing rapidly and that students and staff are already interacting with these tools both within and outside of school environments. As a result, policy reassessment should support both risk mitigation and the district’s responsibility to prepare students to use AI tools thoughtfully and responsibly.

To support effective governance, the committee recommends a structured but flexible approach to reassessment. Review processes should be stable enough to support trust and consistent implementation, while remaining responsive to new evidence, technological change, and classroom realities. The district should define how AI uses are evaluated over time, what outcomes or incidents trigger review, and how feedback from students, families, and staff informs updates.

The committee recommends that new or higher-risk uses of AI begin as bounded pilots rather than immediate system-wide implementation. Pilot programs should include clearly defined goals, appropriate safeguards, and limits on scope and duration. Piloting should serve not only to identify risks, but also to better understand how AI can support learning, efficiency, and access.

The committee further recommends that the district establish clear criteria for evaluating the effectiveness and impact of AI use. Evaluation should consider anticipated benefits, baseline conditions prior to implementation, and outcomes over time. It should also include attention to unintended effects, including impacts on student learning, equity, and staff workload.

The committee emphasizes the importance of clear processes for responding to incidents involving AI tools. These may include issues related to academic integrity, privacy or data exposure, or harmful or inappropriate outputs affecting students. Response processes should include defined escalation pathways, clear responsibility for review and action, and timely communication where appropriate. In all cases, incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, and transparency.

The committee recommends that implementation occur in phases, recognizing that some elements of AI use can begin immediately while others require additional infrastructure, training, or evaluation. A phased approach supports thoughtful adoption and reduces disruption, allowing the district to sequence more complex implementations over time.

The committee also recommends establishing a regular review cadence for AI policy and related guidance. This may include the establishment of a standing committee to conduct periodic administrative review of AI policy and provide guidance to the Board, School Board review at pre-defined intervals, and an accelerated review mechanism that can be triggered by significant incidents, technological developments, or new and significant evidence. Review processes should be devised to ensure accountability, predictability, and continuity, while allowing flexibility when needed.

Ongoing feedback should be an integral part of policy reassessment. Input from students, families, and staff should inform continuous improvement, alongside observed classroom outcomes, implementation challenges, and emerging risks or opportunities. Feedback mechanisms described in related sections (e.g., communication and feedback processes) should be integrated into reassessment efforts.

Finally, the committee recognizes that continuous reassessment must be balanced with the need for clarity and stability. Frequent or poorly communicated changes may create confusion, while delayed updates may limit the district’s ability to respond effectively. The policy should therefore establish a clear framework for reassessment that supports timely, practical updates without undermining consistency.

WS-RSB — Research and Shared Baseline Workstream

# Purpose and Role of This Workstream

This workstream establishes the shared baseline that underlies the policy package. It is not itself the policy shell. Its role is to capture the common framing, recurring realities, and research-informed cautions that shaped later committee discussions about student use, staff use, governance, privacy, equity, communications, and implementation.

In practical terms, the baseline work had four aims: clarify what problem FCCPS is trying to solve; identify principles that should discipline policy choices; summarize the current state of AI in educational settings; and surface recurring use cases and tradeoffs that later workstreams would need to govern in more detail. Sources referenced in earlier drafts of this workstream are consolidated in the report’s annotated references section.

# Strategic Issues

## Purpose of AI Policy

The Falls Church City Public Schools School Board committed to developing a comprehensive AI policy during the 2025–26 school year. From the beginning, the workstream treated that policy as broader than a question about whether students may use chatbots on assignments. It was understood to include how students learn about AI, how and when schools use AI in instruction and operations, how personalized or adaptive tools are governed, and how FCCPS captures potential benefits while mitigating risk and responding to community concerns.

That framing matters because AI is not only another classroom tool. It can affect authorship, assessment, privacy, accessibility, communications, procurement, workload, and trust. The baseline work therefore treated AI as both an instructional issue and a governance issue.

## Shared Principles for Policy Development

Several principles repeatedly shaped the baseline discussion and later informed the consolidated policy sections.

Evidence-informed. The policy should identify the human skills, competencies, and outcomes it intends to protect or advance, and its guidance should be proportionate to the evidence available. Strong claims about benefit should require real support; absence of certainty should not be used to justify either unchecked adoption or careless dismissal.

Alignment with IB values. The policy should remain aligned with the values and learning goals of FCCPS’s IB programs, including thinking, research, communication, social development, and self-management. AI policy should be judged in part by whether it supports or displaces those aims.

Age-appropriate design. Approaches to learning and risks of AI use vary by age. The baseline work consistently treated developmental stage as a primary policy variable rather than as an implementation detail.

Centered on FCCPS students and educators. Research, peer districts, and outside frameworks are useful, but they are not self-applying. Policy choices must be credible in FCCPS classrooms and responsive to the district’s students, staff, and families.

Evolutionary governance. Because AI capabilities, interfaces, risks, and norms change quickly, the district’s first policy should be built for reassessment rather than treated as static or permanent.

## Baseline Challenges the Policy Must Address

The baseline work also identified a set of recurring challenges that any credible FCCPS policy would need to address.

Benefits in K–12 remain uncertain. Some schools and commentators report promising uses of AI, especially for efficiency, tutoring, or personalization. At the same time, broader research and experience do not support the conclusion that AI reliably improves learning outcomes across contexts. Benefits appear highly dependent on task design, user maturity, tool quality, and implementation conditions.

Risks are real and varied. The workstream treated academic integrity, privacy, harmful content generation, cyber misuse, overreliance, and displacement of human judgment as distinct risk domains. AI was not framed as creating only one problem. Rather, it changes the scale, speed, and ambiguity of several existing school responsibilities while also introducing new forms of harm.

Access is already widespread and uneven. Students and educators can reach AI tools regardless of district policy, especially as AI features become embedded in ordinary productivity software. This creates two simultaneous concerns: school policy cannot assume non-use, and unequal access or fluency outside school may create unequal advantages.

Detection and enforcement are limited. The workstream did not assume that FCCPS can reliably tell when AI has been used. That has implications for integrity policy, fairness, and assignment design. Rules that depend mainly on perfect detection are unlikely to be durable or equitable.

Teacher preparation and implementation capacity matter. Educator use is already variable, and some uses occur without formal training or standardized governance. A policy that assumes readiness without professional development, approved tools, and administrative support will not perform as intended.

Equity and access cannot be an afterthought. AI may widen opportunity gaps if some students receive better tools, more practice, or more adult guidance than others. It may also create accommodation and language-access issues if rules are written without considering how students legitimately use translation, accessibility, or support technologies.

# Working Concepts and Terminology

The baseline work distinguished several related but non-identical concepts.

Artificial intelligence. A broad label for computing systems that perform tasks commonly associated with human intelligence. The label shifts over time as technologies mature, so policy language should avoid being tied too narrowly to one product generation.

Large language models and generative AI. The current wave of AI tools is centered on models that generate text and, increasingly, images, audio, video, and code. For school policy, these tools deserve special attention because they can produce work-like outputs that blur lines around authorship, assistance, evaluation, and disclosure.

AI literacy. The workstream used AI literacy to mean more than technical familiarity. It includes understanding how AI works at a practical level, where it is unreliable, what ethical issues it raises, how to evaluate outputs, and when not to use them.

AI education and AI in education. The baseline distinguished between teaching students about AI as a topic of study and using AI within educational settings to support instruction, assessment, or operations. FCCPS policy needs to address both.

# Current State of AI in Education

The workstream proceeded from a practical observation: AI is already part of the environment in which FCCPS operates.

Student use is already happening. Students use public tools for brainstorming, drafting, summarizing, problem solving, coding, and creative work. The existence of these uses outside school means policy must govern real conditions, not a hypothetical future adoption.

Educator use is highly variable. Educators may already be using AI for lesson planning, material preparation, translation, drafting, feedback, or administrative support. The baseline work treated that reality as a reason to establish guardrails rather than leave practice entirely informal.

Formal governance has lagged practice. Training, consent, disclosure, and approval processes were incomplete or inconsistent relative to the pace of AI adoption. This mismatch between actual use and formal governance is one reason the committee regarded policy development as urgent.

The baseline also identified three broad ways AI affects education. First, AI is a topic students need to understand. Second, AI is a class of tools that may be adopted or restricted for instructional and operational purposes. Third, AI is a system-level governance challenge because it affects learning, assessment, privacy, equity, communications, and implementation capacity at the same time.

# Policy-Relevant Use Cases and Evidence Summary

The baseline work did not treat “AI use” as a single category. It repeatedly separated use cases by function, learning objective, developmental fit, and level of consequence.

## Student Uses

The workstream consistently distinguished between uses that may support entry, feedback, or bounded practice and uses that can substitute for core reading, writing, reasoning, or problem-solving. Brainstorming and revision may have some value, but only if students remain responsible for the substance of the work. AI-generated first drafts, iterative essay rewriting, and other agentic production uses posed the clearest risks to authorship, cognitive effort, and fair assessment.

Summarization and synthesis were treated as potentially useful but fragile uses because they can flatten nuance, misstate emphasis, or generate polished but conceptually weak output. Personalized or gamified tools may provide feedback and differentiated practice, but they should not be assumed effective or safe simply because they are adaptive. Media and code generation can support bounded creative or technical tasks, especially in older grades, but they require stronger attention to disclosure, safety, verification, and developmental fit.

## Teacher and Staff Uses

On the staff side, the most defensible uses identified in the baseline work were planning-related. AI may help generate draft lesson structures, examples, translations, differentiations, or accessibility supports. The plausible upside is productivity and option generation; the corresponding risk is that inaccurate, biased, generic, or developmentally misaligned material may enter practice if educators do not have the time and support to review it carefully.

Feedback and grading raised sharper concerns. Timeliness and efficiency may be attractive, especially for formative work, but risks to trust, authenticity, and fairness rise quickly when AI contributes to evaluative judgments. That tension helped set up a later distinction between bounded assistance and impermissible delegation of professional judgment.

# Detailed Functional Review

The baseline work also reviewed recurring AI functions in more operational detail. The goal was not to pre-write policy for every classroom scenario, but to identify which uses appear more pedagogically defensible, which ones raise the clearest concerns, and which ones require differentiated treatment by grade band, assignment type, and level of consequence.

## Brainstorming and Early Ideation

The workstream recognized that AI may help some students get started, generate candidate questions, or see multiple ways into a task. That potential value is narrow and conditional. The educational risk is that students may bypass the very struggle through which ideas, ownership, and confidence develop. For that reason, early ideation was treated as more defensible when students have already engaged in some independent thinking and remain responsible for choosing, evaluating, and developing the final ideas.

## First-Draft Writing and Agentic Composition

The clearest line in the baseline work concerned uses in which AI generates a first draft, successive rewrites, or a near-complete work product under light student supervision. These uses were repeatedly treated as the point at which support collapses into substitution. In writing-heavy contexts, especially where authorship, reasoning, and communication are central learning goals, the workstream viewed AI-generated drafting as one of the least defensible categories of use.

That conclusion was not based only on academic-integrity concerns. It also reflected a deeper educational concern that the processes of confronting the blank page, organizing thought, testing claims, and revising in one’s own voice are themselves part of what students are meant to learn.

## Summarization and Synthesis

AI summarization may offer convenience, preview, or review value, especially when students are overwhelmed by long or complex material. At the same time, the workstream treated summarization as a high-risk shortcut when close reading, source interpretation, or nuanced comparison is itself the learning goal. Synthesis raised an additional concern: AI can produce polished, plausible output that smooths over disagreement, blurs attribution, or invents coherence not supported by the source material.

The baseline implication was that these uses may sometimes be allowed, but only where original sources remain available and the assignment design makes clear that students are still accountable for verification, interpretation, and source-based reasoning.

## Revision, Style, and Grammar Support

Revision-oriented uses were treated more favorably than first-draft generation, but still with caution. Style and grammar support may be defensible when a student-authored draft already exists and when the assessment is not primarily about mechanics. Even here, the baseline work emphasized that “revision” can quietly become ghost authorship if students adopt substantive changes without understanding their effect on meaning, structure, or voice.

Accordingly, the workstream pointed toward more permissive treatment of bounded revision support in later grades and final-polish stages than in early drafting or foundational literacy contexts.

## Personalized and Adaptive Learning Tools

The workstream distinguished broad consumer generative AI tools from narrower, district-approved adaptive learning applications. Personalized or gamified tools may provide immediate feedback, flexible pacing, and differentiated practice in bounded domains. But the baseline work did not assume that adaptive tools are effective by default. Their value depends on instructional fit, oversight, privacy protections, accessibility, and whether they improve learning rather than merely increasing screen time, convenience, or compliance.

This distinction mattered because some uses that are pedagogically questionable in open-ended generative tools may be more acceptable in approved, bounded applications. Even so, such tools still require governance rather than blanket trust.

## Media Creation and Code Generation

The workstream also treated image, audio, video, and code generation as distinct from ordinary writing assistance. These functions can support prototyping, multimodal composition, media-literacy instruction, debugging, or comparison of solution paths. They also raise their own risks: deceptive synthetic media, copyright concerns, age-inappropriate outputs, unsafe code, and student submission of work they do not understand.

The baseline implication was not categorical prohibition. Rather, these uses require bounded purposes, teacher framing, disclosure expectations, and stronger attention to age, safety, and verification.

## Lesson Planning and Instructional Materials

On the staff side, the most defensible uses identified in the baseline work were planning-related. AI may help generate draft lesson structures, examples, differentiations, and alternative explanations. The plausible upside is productivity and option generation. The corresponding risk is that generic, biased, inaccurate, or developmentally misaligned material may enter classrooms if educators do not have the time, judgment, or support to review it carefully.

The baseline work therefore treated planning uses as assistive rather than authoritative. AI output should be treated as a draft for professional review, not as a ready-made instructional product.

## Feedback, Grading, and Trust

The most sensitive educator uses involved feedback and grading. The workstream recognized the attraction of faster scoring, pattern detection, and first-pass comments, especially for formative work. It also repeatedly returned to the relational and ethical concern that students and families may experience AI-mediated evaluation as inattentive, inauthentic, or unfair.

That tension is one reason the baseline work helped set up a later distinction between bounded assistance and improper delegation of professional judgment. The workstream did not support autonomous grading or AI-determinative evaluative decisions, and it treated trust as a substantive educational value rather than a public-relations issue.

# Implications for Policy Design

## Grade Bands and Developmental Progression

The baseline work strongly supported grade-banded policy. Younger students should encounter more limited, structured, and educator-mediated use, with stronger protections for foundational literacy, numeracy, social development, and human relationships. Older students may receive broader guided exposure and, eventually, more independent use under clearer expectations for integrity, disclosure, and evaluation.

## Human Accountability and Bounded Use

A second recurring implication was that certain school responsibilities remain inherently human. Instruction, evaluation, discipline, disability-related planning, and consequential decisions require reasoning, accountability, and relationship-based judgment that policy should not offload to AI systems. Even where AI plays a support role, a qualified human must remain responsible for the outcome.

## Governance Before Expansion

The baseline work did not support expansion without infrastructure. If FCCPS permits new or higher risk uses, it should be able to name the educational purpose, expected benefits, applicable safeguards, approval pathway, monitoring plan, and conditions for reassessment or discontinuation. In this sense, governance capacity is not downstream of policy; it is part of what makes a policy credible.

# External Guidance and Comparative Inputs Considered

## State, Cross-Sector, and Readiness Guidance

The baseline work considered Virginia’s AI guidance and the SCHEV-supported work on AI readiness in education. These materials reinforced themes of caution, human oversight, professional development, younger-learner safeguards, and the need for structured feedback and review pathways.

## Peer District Examples

Peer districts were useful as reference points, but none was treated as a template. Arlington was helpful for AI literacy and district guidance structure; Alexandria and Montgomery County offered examples of public-facing policy language; Fairfax illustrated the difference between resource hubs and enforceable policy. A recurring lesson from these comparisons was that peer policies often address many of the right topics but still leave unresolved questions about grade bands, implementation burden, and how to scale restrictions or permissions across different use cases.

## National Frameworks and Literacy Resources

TeachAI, Digital Promise, UNESCO, OECD/TeachAI, AI4K12, and related resources informed the baseline understanding of AI literacy, policy design principles, and grade-banded educational planning. These resources were most useful when they reinforced a human-centered understanding of AI literacy rather than being treated as implicit arguments for broad adoption simply because AI exists.

# Summary Takeaways for the Policy Package

Taken together, the baseline work supports the following conclusions for the policy package.

FCCPS should begin with educational purpose, not with technology availability or novelty.

AI literacy should be treated as important across grade bands, but it should not be confused with broad permission for routine student use.

Policy should distinguish among use cases by grade band, stakes, and whether AI supports or displaces the underlying learning goal.

The district should assume uneven benefits, real risks, widespread outside access, and limited detectability when designing guardrails.

Human judgment should remain primary in high-stakes, relational, and evaluative contexts.

Privacy, equity, accessibility, and community trust should be treated as design constraints from the outset, not as after-the-fact considerations.

New or higher-risk uses should be introduced through bounded, reviewable processes rather than broad default adoption.

Professional development and governance capacity are prerequisites for sustainable implementation, not optional add-ons.

WS-AIN – Academic Integrity & Attribution Workstream

# Purpose and Role of This Workstream

This workstream examined how FCCPS should think about academic integrity, authorship, attribution, disclosure, and enforcement in a school environment where generative AI is widely accessible but not reliably detectable. Its role is not to write every classroom rule. Rather, it captures the logic, guardrails, and tradeoffs that informed later policy sections on staff use, student use, disclosure, accommodations, privacy, and implementation.

Earlier drafts preserved a large matrix of scenarios, questions, and provisional positions. This revised section keeps the underlying framework while consolidating detail that now appears more directly in WS-POL-03 and WS-POL-05. The aim is to retain the strongest analytic insights: where support becomes substitution, where assignment design matters more than policing, where developmental stage changes the answer, and where enforcement mechanisms may create harms of their own.

# Conceptual Framework

The workstream consistently separated two different questions. The first is an idealized question: if compliance were perfect, what uses of AI would best protect student learning and authorship? The second is a pragmatic question: what rules can a district enforce without excessive collateral harm, false accusations, or a policing burden that distorts classroom practice? The central insight is that integrity policy must address both questions at once.

That distinction led the workstream to analyze AI use across two additional variables: whether the task is formative or summative, and whether it occurs in a proctored or unproctored setting. The practical result is not a single universal rule, but a framework for deciding when stricter restrictions are educationally justified and when better assessment design is a sounder response than after-the-fact detection.

# Shared Integrity Principles

Learning objectives govern. The question is not whether a tool is impressive or common, but whether its use supports or displaces the human capacity the task is meant to build or reveal.

Support and substitution are not the same. Explanation, bounded feedback, or discovery support may be defensible in some contexts; AI-generated first drafts, iterative rewriting, or agentic production often cross into replacement.

Developmental stage matters. Younger students require stronger protection of foundational reading, writing, problem-solving, and self-management; older students may gradually assume more responsibility under clearer expectations.

Assessment conditions matter. The same AI use may be more tolerable in low-stakes formative work than in a high-stakes unproctored assessment intended to show unaided performance.

Human judgment remains primary. Educators may not delegate consequential evaluative decisions to AI, and accusations of misconduct should not rest on opaque or unreliable tools.

Enforcement should not create larger harms than the behavior it seeks to prevent. Detector-driven suspicion, mandatory logging, or overly standardized disclosure rules may themselves undermine trust, fairness, and instructional quality.

# Student Use Logic

The workstream did not treat student AI use as a single category. It distinguished among uses by grade band, assignment purpose, and how much thinking or authorship the tool displaced. It also recognized a real difference between help that supports a student through a difficult step and help that completes the work in the student’s place.

## PreK–5

For younger students, the workstream generally favored a strong presumption against routine applied generative-AI use for ordinary schoolwork. The reasons were developmental as much as integrity-related: risks of passivity, unhealthy dependency, confusion between human and machine authority, and erosion of foundational reading, writing, and problem-solving habits. AI literacy and tightly bounded teacher-mediated demonstrations remained more defensible than free-form student use.

This did not amount to a view that younger students should never encounter AI. Rather, the baseline logic was that exposure in these grades should be structured, supervised, and tied to conceptual understanding, safety, and classroom goals. Applied use should be narrow, and any exceptions should be justified either by clear instructional benefit or documented accommodation needs.

## Grades 6–12

For older students, the workstream recognized a more complex tradeoff. Many students will use AI regardless of school policy, and some uses may legitimately support learning, productivity, or preparation for later study and work. At the same time, the workstream repeatedly cautioned that open-ended writing, analysis, and other cognitively demanding tasks can be hollowed out quickly when AI is allowed to generate structure, language, or solutions under only light student supervision.

The record shows broad agreement that students in these grades should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted. It also shows a live difference in emphasis around certain middle cases: some members viewed limited brainstorming, organizational help, or single-pass editorial review as potentially manageable scaffolds; others viewed those same uses as the first step toward outsourcing authorship and weakening the habits that writing and reasoning assignments are meant to develop.

## Students with Disabilities and Multilingual Learners

The workstream also warned against writing integrity rules so broadly that they block legitimate accommodations or language supports. AI-facilitated transcription, text-to-speech, reading-level adjustment, translation, or other documented supports may be necessary for access in some contexts. The baseline position was that any AI-related integrity framework should remain subject to 504 or IEP accommodations when documented as necessary and educationally appropriate.

At the same time, the workstream did not treat accommodation as a blank check for automation. A recurring caution was that AI support must not be used to replace human support on cost-saving grounds or to freeze students into supports that supplant skill development rather than enabling it. Periodic review and human oversight remain essential.

# Staff Uses and Evaluative Integrity

Although the workstream focused mainly on student integrity, it also made clear that teacher and staff behavior shapes the credibility of any integrity framework. If students see educators relying on AI in ways that appear to outsource judgment, ghost-write feedback, or shortcut professional responsibility, student compliance becomes harder to defend and harder to enforce.

## Feedback, grading, and preliminary evaluation

The workstream recognized the practical appeal of faster rubric alignment, draft comments, and pattern detection. It also surfaced a deeper concern: evaluative feedback is not only a technical act but a relational one. Students and families may experience AI-mediated feedback as inattentive, inauthentic, or unfair, especially when AI contribution is substantial but hidden. The workstream therefore treated evaluative assistance as a narrower and more sensitive category than planning or translation support.

The record shows a real difference in emphasis here. Some members regarded even AI-assisted drafting of feedback as corrosive unless tightly bounded; others were more open to limited assistive use where the teacher meaningfully reviews, revises, and stands behind the final feedback. What the workstream did not support was autonomous grading or the displacement of teacher judgment in consequential evaluative decisions.

## Detector posture and enforcement fairness

The workstream treated AI detectors with sustained skepticism. It identified due-process problems, false positives and negatives, lack of transparency, bias concerns, confirmation bias in teacher decision-making, and an adversarial effect on classroom trust. It also noted that detector-based enforcement can become an arms race that pressures teachers to rely on unreliable tools rather than redesigning assessments or gathering better process evidence.

That does not mean the workstream denied the integrity problem. Rather, it concluded that detector outputs, if ever used at all, should not serve as sole or primary evidence of misconduct and should not substitute for human review, drafts, conferencing, reflections, or other context-based evidence. The baseline logic pointed toward a precautionary posture, not routine deployment.

# Attribution, Citation, and Disclosure

A central contribution of this workstream was to separate citation from disclosure. The workstream did not treat AI-generated text as a conventional source that can simply be cited in the same way as an article, book, or document. Instead, it generally favored citing the actual human-authored source consulted and using disclosure, where needed, to acknowledge the role AI played in shaping the work.

Even there, the workstream did not reach full consensus on a broad always-on disclosure rule. Members raised concerns about privacy, practicality, over-standardization, and the possibility that mandatory disclosure can reduce trust even when AI use is permitted. The baseline implication was that disclosure expectations should be calibrated to assignment type and degree of substantive influence rather than maximized by default.

# Representative Scenarios

The original workstream included a larger scenario bank. The examples below preserve a few representative edge cases because they show how the framework changes the answer depending on task purpose, student age, and degree of AI contribution.

## Middle-school study support

A student who asks AI to explain material, generate practice questions, or offer feedback on short-answer responses presents a different integrity problem than a student who asks AI to produce the final answer. The workstream generally treated study-support uses as more defensible when they promote later independent performance and when teachers can still check understanding through in-class or otherwise controlled assessment.

## High-school literary analysis essay

The workstream treated essay writing as one of the clearest places where support can slide into substitution. Topic generation, argument structure, and stylistic revision may look like small assists, but in a writing-intensive assignment they may also off-load the very thinking the task was designed to elicit. That is why the workstream placed special pressure on assignment design, teacher-specified permissions, and disclosure expectations in later-grade writing classes.

## Later-grade coding project

Coding was one of the few recurring cases where the workstream suggested the answer may differ from conventional writing. Some members viewed iterative AI assistance as more tolerable in computer science when students still had to understand, test, debug, and explain the code. Even there, the workstream did not endorse blind reliance. The key distinction was whether the student remained accountable for comprehension and proof of correctness, not merely for supervising machine output.

# Assessment Design as an Integrity Strategy

The workstream repeatedly returned to the idea that not every integrity problem should be solved through detection, mandatory logs, or blanket prohibition. In many cases, better assignment design is the more educationally sound response. More in-class writing, oral defense, conferencing, draft checkpoints, process reflections, and flipped or supervised production models can reduce ambiguity while keeping the focus on learning rather than surveillance.

At the same time, the workstream did not assume that every summative task can simply be moved into a proctored setting. That shift can carry instructional, scheduling, and workload costs, and it may not be feasible across all subjects or grade levels. The practical implication is that FCCPS will need a mix of strategies rather than a single enforcement mechanism.

# Summary Takeaways for the Policy Package

Taken together, the academic-integrity and attribution work supports the following conclusions for the policy package.

The district should distinguish clearly between assistance that supports learning and uses that replace authorship, reasoning, or evaluative authenticity.

Restrictions should vary by grade band, assignment purpose, and assessment conditions rather than assuming one answer for every classroom context.

First-draft generation, iterative rewriting, concealment, and other agentic production uses pose the clearest integrity concerns in ordinary classroom writing tasks.

Some middle-ground uses remain live tradeoffs in the record, especially idea generation, organizational help, and bounded editorial feedback.

Teacher conduct matters to student compliance. Staff use that appears to outsource grading or feedback can erode trust and weaken any integrity framework.

AI detectors should not be treated as a routine or sufficient basis for academic-misconduct determinations.

Citation and disclosure should not be collapsed into the same requirement. Students should cite actual sources consulted, while disclosure should be calibrated to material AI contribution and assignment design.

Accommodations and multilingual supports must be preserved, but AI support in those contexts should remain reviewed, documented, and human-supervised.

Assessment design, process evidence, and better communication of expectations are likely to be more durable than policies that rely mainly on after-the-fact detection.

Tradeoffs: Overreliance on AI-generated language can obviate the core analytical and compositional skills needed to produce quality written work. Can help standardize parent communication, reducing the likelihood of omissions or the inadvertent disclosure of personal information. Lowers the cost of creating accessible parent communication systems that are accessible to families with diverse language backgrounds.

Tradeoffs: Inputting identifiable or highly sensitive student information into third-party AI tools can create privacy, confidentiality, and data-governance concerns, including potential non-compliance with FERPA or FCCPS policy. Overreliance on AI-generated language for delicate conversations may flatten nuance, overlook cultural context, or unintentionally convey a tone that damages relationships.

WS-CCI – Communications & Community Input Workstream

# Purpose and Role of This Workstream

This workstream addresses how FCCPS should explain AI policy to the community, gather input in structured ways, and show how that input affects district decisions. Its role is not to serve as a complete standalone communications campaign. Rather, it provides the communications and engagement framework that supports policy legitimacy, implementation readiness, and public trust.

Earlier drafts preserved a longer community-engagement plan, audience appendices, message pillars, feedback taxonomy, and a detailed 90-day rollout. This revised section condenses that material into a shorter board-facing framework plus a compact implementation appendix. The purpose is to retain what is most useful for Board review: who needs to hear what, through which channels, at what point in the process, and how FCCPS should show that community input mattered.

# Why Communications Is a Governance Function

The workstream treated communications as part of AI governance, not as an after-the-fact public-relations exercise. AI touches student safety, academic integrity, privacy, classroom practice, accessibility, equity, and district operations. If students, families, and staff do not understand what FCCPS is allowing, restricting, reviewing, or piloting, even a well-drafted policy will be experienced as confusing, untrustworthy, or unfair.

For that reason, communications in this context must do more than announce decisions. They must explain purpose, describe safeguards in plain language, identify where professional discretion remains, and make it easy for affected stakeholders to raise concerns. Just as important, the district should be able to show that concerns were received, organized, reviewed, and reflected in later decisions or guidance.

The workstream also recognized several communications failures the district should avoid from the outset: fragmented feedback channels, vague language about what is approved, overreliance on broad reassurance instead of concrete guardrails, and rollout sequences in which families or staff first learn of meaningful AI changes only after classroom practice has already shifted.

# Core Communications Objectives

• Explain before expanding. FCCPS should communicate the purpose, scope, and safeguards of AI-related changes before broad rollout, not after confusion has already formed.

• Tailor communication by audience and role. Families, students, educators, principals, and outside partners do not need identical messages; they need messages that answer the questions most relevant to their responsibilities and risks.

• Make boundaries concrete. Communications should clearly distinguish what is approved, what is conditional, what remains under review, and what is not allowed.

• Pair notice with usable feedback. Every major communication about AI should direct people to a simple way to ask questions, report concerns, or identify unintended consequences.

• Close the loop publicly. FCCPS should periodically summarize what it heard, what themes emerged, and whether district guidance changed as a result.

# Priority Audiences

The original workstream included a longer appendix of stakeholder groups and working assumptions. The condensed version below preserves the main audience categories that repeatedly surfaced in committee discussion. The basic organizing idea is simple: communications should be designed around what each group needs from the district, not around a single generic message track.

District leadership and principals need governance clarity: who decides, what escalation paths exist, and what implementation responsibilities are attached to different AI uses. Educators and staff need operational clarity: what they may do, what they may not do, what tools are approved, and where to seek help when a classroom or student-support situation does not fit a simple rule.

Students need understandable expectations about use, misuse, disclosure, and safety. Families need confidence that FCCPS is protecting students, communicating age-appropriate boundaries, and taking privacy, fairness, and mental well-being seriously. External stakeholders can add perspective on workforce readiness, risk, and community norms, but they should supplement rather than override FCCPS educational priorities.

# Engagement Methods and Their Uses

The workstream favored a mixed-channel approach rather than reliance on a single town hall or survey. Different methods reveal different things. Broad public sessions can surface recurring concerns and test whether district language is clear. Smaller listening sessions can uncover implementation detail, ambiguity, or unintended consequences that would not surface in larger forums. Student advisory settings may be especially important because students are often more candid when the setting feels bounded and developmentally appropriate.

The committee also viewed school-based and digital channels as complements rather than substitutes. Faculty meetings, principal-led discussions, and department workshops help with implementation clarity. Written FAQs, webpages, and structured feedback forms help with consistency and traceability. Surveys are helpful for breadth, but they should not be the district’s only listening mechanism because they flatten nuance and rarely capture the reasoning behind stakeholder concern.

# Message Architecture

The workstream converged on five recurring message pillars that can anchor district webpages, FAQs, slide decks, town-hall remarks, staff guidance, and student-facing materials. These are not slogans. They are the core ideas that should remain stable even as specific tools, procedures, or examples change.

Safety and protection first. FCCPS should communicate clearly that student well-being, age-appropriate exposure, and privacy protections come before convenience or novelty.

Academic integrity and learning. The district should frame AI policy around learning goals, original thinking, and fairness, rather than implying that all AI use is either harmless or inherently dishonest.

Clear guardrails for responsible use. Stakeholders should be able to tell, in understandable terms, which uses are allowed, which require teacher or administrative approval, and which are prohibited.

Equity and access. Communications should acknowledge that unequal access, digital fluency, disability accommodations, and language supports affect how AI policy is experienced across the community.

Transparency and community partnership. The district should communicate not only decisions, but also the process by which concerns are gathered, reviewed, and translated into policy refinement.

In practice, these pillars imply that FCCPS communications should avoid both marketing language and panic language. The district does not need to present AI as inevitable progress, and it does not need to frame every use as an emergency. It does need to explain clearly what the district is trying to protect, what it is trying to prepare students for, and what guardrails make that balance credible.

# Feedback Capture, Analysis, and Public Accountability

The workstream emphasized that feedback should be centralized, organized, and reviewable. Allowing input through scattered emails alone makes it harder to identify themes, respond consistently, or demonstrate that community input mattered. A more credible model is a single primary intake path, supplemented by listening sessions, surveys, and targeted stakeholder engagement.

At minimum, the district should maintain a structured feedback process with five features:

a common intake channel for written submissions, paired with plain-language explanations of what kinds of questions or concerns belong there;

a shared topic taxonomy so submissions can be grouped by themes such as safety, privacy, integrity, equity, classroom practice, or implementation burden;

periodic internal review of themes, not just individual complaints, so recurring issues become visible to decision-makers;

public summaries that report major themes, clarifications, and any resulting policy or guidance changes;

an escalation path for issues serious enough to require rapid reassessment, such as harmful outputs, privacy breaches, discriminatory effects, or widespread confusion about district expectations.

The workstream supported the idea of a public-facing dashboard or periodic update cycle, but the deeper principle was accountability rather than a specific tool. FCCPS should be able to show what it heard, how it categorized the feedback, what it clarified immediately, and what it routed into later policy or implementation decisions.

This is also where communications intersects with other workstreams. A report of harmful content, a complaint about staff use, confusion over student disclosure, or concern about unapproved tools is not merely a communications issue. It may be an implementation, privacy, equity, or policy-review issue that communications channels help surface earlier.

# Success Indicators

The original workstream asked not only how FCCPS should communicate, but how it would know whether the effort was working. The strongest indicators were practical rather than performative:

participation from the stakeholder groups most directly affected by the policy, not only from the most vocal constituencies;

visible clarification of recurring questions about safety, integrity, privacy, and grade-band expectations;

evidence that the district can organize feedback into themes rather than react only to isolated comments;

public explanation of changes made in response to community input, where changes occur;

a communications structure that remains usable after initial adoption, so implementation questions and emerging risks continue to surface.

# Summary Takeaways for the Policy Package

Communications should be treated as a core part of AI governance because trust, compliance, and perceived fairness all depend on whether stakeholders understand what the district is doing and why.

FCCPS should communicate in audience-specific ways, while keeping a stable set of message pillars across materials and channels.

Notice should be paired with a usable, centralized feedback process so the district can identify patterns rather than just collect scattered reactions.

Public accountability depends on closing the loop: the district should periodically report major themes, clarifications, and resulting adjustments.

A phased communications sequence—internal alignment, public listening, trend review, and policy validation—provides a more disciplined path than ad hoc announcements or late-stage outreach.

The fuller planning appendices from earlier drafts may still be useful for administration, but the framework above captures the core board-facing logic of the workstream.

WS-EQA – Equity & Access Workstream

# Purpose and Role of This Workstream

This workstream addresses how FCCPS should evaluate artificial-intelligence decisions through an equity and accessibility lens from procurement through classroom practice, assessment, student support, family communication, and ongoing review. Its role is not to provide a fill-in worksheet for every classroom choice. Rather, it offers a review framework the district can use when policies, tools, or workflows may affect student groups differently in practice.

Earlier drafts preserved a checklist, open questions, and district-data requests. This revised section retains the strongest underlying point: a checklist alone is not enough if the district has not also named the principles, mitigation expectations, monitoring responsibilities, and decision owners that make equitable implementation possible. The workstream therefore treats equity as a design discipline, not a late-stage compliance add-on.

# Why Equity Must Be Built In Early

The workstream consistently argued that equity and access should be considered before tools are approved, before classroom rules are normalized, and before enforcement expectations are hardened. Once a tool, workflow, or integrity posture is already embedded in day-to-day practice, students who depend on accommodations, translation, adult guidance, school-based access, or clearly communicated alternatives often bear the cost of redesign.

This matters because AI-related inequities do not arise in only one way. Some are access problems: students may have unequal home devices, internet connectivity, paid-tool access, or AI fluency outside school. Some are accommodation problems: policies may unintentionally block translation, text-to-speech, speech-to-text, paraphrasing, or other legitimate supports. Some are enforcement problems: detector-based suspicion, vague rules, or inconsistent teacher practice may fall unevenly on multilingual learners, students with disabilities, or students whose work differs from expected norms. Others are implementation problems: if staff are not trained, if vendors are opaque, or if families do not understand what is changing, the burdens of uncertainty will not be distributed evenly.

# Guiding Principles

The revised workstream supports the following guiding principles for district review and implementation:

Equity begins at procurement and policy design, not after harm has already surfaced in classrooms or student-support settings.

Access must be meaningful. If FCCPS expects or permits AI use in ways that shape learning, students should have a realistic school-supported path and, where appropriate, a meaningful non-AI alternative.

Accommodations and language supports must be preserved. AI policy should distinguish misuse from legitimate disability-related, accessibility-related, or multilingual supports.

Human review remains necessary in consequential contexts. High-stakes decisions, integrity determinations, discipline, and disability-related planning should not be driven by automated indicators alone.

Implementation burden is itself an equity issue. Rules that are too complex, too opaque, or too training-dependent may be applied unevenly across classrooms and schools.

AI literacy is part of equity. Students and staff should learn enough about AI limitations, bias, and responsible use to participate safely and fairly, without assuming advanced technical expertise.

Monitoring and correction are ongoing obligations. FCCPS should be prepared to revise guidance, pause uses, or add safeguards when disaggregated experience shows that harms are emerging.

# Equity Review Criteria

The original checklist identified the right domains of concern. The revised version turns those domains into review criteria that can be applied to policy language, tool approvals, classroom guidance, implementation plans, and reassessment decisions.

# Required Mitigations and Ownership

The earlier checklist correctly treated Yellow and Red findings as action items, but it did not fully translate those concerns into durable district ownership. The revised framework makes that step explicit: a concern should not simply be logged; it should be assigned, routed to the part of the system that can address it, and revisited if the mitigation proves weak in practice.

Some mitigations belong in board-facing policy, such as preserving human judgment or protecting accommodations. Others belong in administrative guidance, procurement review, professional development, or incident response. The important point is that FCCPS should not normalize a use case when the needed mitigation is unidentified or ownerless.

# How the Framework Should Be Applied

The full framework is most useful at district and school leadership decision points, not as a burdensome form for every ordinary classroom choice. Teachers need clear rules, examples, and escalation paths. Central reviewers need the fuller diagnostic framework that helps them test whether a proposed policy, tool, or workflow is likely to create uneven burdens or uneven benefits.

In practice, the framework should be applied at four points in the governance cycle:

Policy drafting. Review whether proposed language preserves accommodations, differentiates support from misuse, and avoids hidden reliance on outside access or detector-based enforcement.

Tool approval and procurement. Combine the equity framework with privacy, accessibility, and vendor-governance review before classroom normalization.

Classroom guidance and examples. Translate district principles into grade-banded, task-sensitive examples so staff are not forced to improvise high-stakes equity decisions alone.

Monitoring and reassessment. Use feedback, complaints, incident patterns, and outcome data to test whether the original mitigation assumptions were accurate in practice.

This also means that not every concern requires the same response. Some problems call for clearer teacher guidance, better family communication, or stronger professional development. Others should trigger redesign, tighter restrictions, or non-approval. The framework is therefore meant to improve judgment and consistency, not to force every issue into a single procedural box.

# Metrics, Monitoring, and Feedback Loops

The workstream did not treat monitoring as optional. If FCCPS wants to know whether a policy or tool is equitable in practice, it must examine what happened after adoption, not only what the vendor or designer promised beforehand. At the same time, the record suggests using existing structures where possible rather than building an entirely separate reporting regime. Equity monitoring should therefore be integrated into approval review, incident reporting, family feedback, special-education processes, multilingual-support review, and regular policy reassessment.

A disciplined starter set of indicators is more useful than a long wish list that no one reviews consistently. The goal is not maximal data collection. The goal is to identify patterns early enough that the district can adjust guidance, add safeguards, pause a use, or redirect support before inequitable practice hardens into routine practice.

# Summary Takeaways for the Policy Package

Equity and access should be treated as upstream design constraints for AI policy, procurement, classroom guidance, and reassessment—not as an appendix added after the fact.

The original checklist surfaced the right risk domains, but FCCPS also needs a clearer framework naming review criteria, required mitigations, monitoring expectations, and owners.

Access, accommodations, language supports, enforcement fairness, privacy, vendor transparency, and staff capacity should all be reviewed before a use case is normalized.

Mitigations should be assigned to the part of the system that can actually carry them: policy, guidance, procurement, professional development, implementation, or incident response.

A small set of monitored indicators is more useful than a long unreviewed list. FCCPS should look for patterns early enough to revise guidance, add safeguards, or pause a use when needed.

The framework is intended to improve judgment and consistency across the district while preserving the central principle that AI should serve educational goals without widening inequity or weakening access.

WS-IPC — Implementation/PD & Change Adoption Workstream

# Decision — Committee Perspective (No Full Consensus)

The committee broadly agrees that AI policy implementation must be phased, that professional development is essential before broad adoption, and that governance infrastructure should be in place before higher-risk tools are deployed. There is strong alignment that unphased adoption creates inequitable implementation and that staff training is a prerequisite, not an afterthought.

There are differing perspectives on how prescriptive the phasing requirements should be.

Some members favor explicit gate requirements between phases, where Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 infrastructure is confirmed operational.

Others prefer a more flexible approach that allows building-level discretion on readiness.

These perspectives reflect a legitimate tradeoff between consistency and responsiveness. There is also meaningful disagreement on whether special education-specific professional development should be mandated in policy or left to administrative discretion. This draft recommends the former, but the Board should be aware this is a contested position.

# Commitments to Consider

## Phased Implementation Model

The committee recommends a phased implementation approach with explicit requirements between phases. This model is designed to align with FCCPS’s existing Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS), extending a familiar and proven tiered framework into the AI governance domain. Staff and administrators will recognize the logic immediately, which significantly improves feasibility and reduces the learning curve.

Gate requirement: Phase 2 cannot begin until Phase 1 governance and training infrastructure is confirmed operational. This is not a slowdown mechanism. It is a quality assurance requirement. Piloting higher-risk tools without governance infrastructure in place creates exactly the equity gaps the committee is trying to prevent.

Phasing principle:

Implementation should be phased by risk tier, not by school or grade level.

Piloting high-risk tools in school buildings with higher concentrations of students with IEPs and 504 plans, or buildings experiencing staff shortages, without full safeguards in place would create inequitable conditions for the students most in need of protection.

## Professional Development Framework

The committee recognizes that effective AI governance requires professional development as infrastructure, not as an add-on. CDT’s 2025 survey found that only 22% of teachers have received formal training on AI use and risk management — even as 57% of special education teachers report already using AI for IEP development. This gap between adoption and training must be closed before broader deployment.

The PD framework should include differentiated tracks based on role and responsibility:

PD should be designed to accommodate the full range of FCCPS staff — from experienced educators and support personnel   (paraprofessionals, counselors, nurses, custodians, office staff), to librarians and language staff (ELL specialists, district substitutes), to early-career teachers who may use AI tools extensively but without governance awareness. Training should meet staff where they are, not assume uniform starting knowledge.

## Policy Review Cadence

The committee recommends establishing a structured review cycle that balances adaptability with governance efficiency:

Annual administrative review: The superintendent or designee reviews the AI tool inventory, risk tier assignments, incident log, and equity indicators each year. Updates at this level should not require full Board action.

Biennial Board review: The full Board reviews the AI policy every two years, incorporating updated research, technological developments, legal and regulatory changes, and community feedback.

Trigger-based accelerated review: Immediate review required upon:

significant federal or state regulatory change affecting student data privacy or special education, such as updated VDOE guidance following Virginia HB1186

documented harm or inequitable impact on students

emergence of new AI capability categories not covered by existing risk tiers

Tradeoff for Board consideration: Annual versus biennial Board review is a live tradeoff. More frequent review keeps the policy current but requires sustained Board time and staff capacity. Less frequent review provides stability but risks the policy lagging a technology landscape that is moving very fast. The trigger-based mechanism ensures the policy cannot become dangerously outdated between scheduled review cycles regardless of which cadence is selected.

### Pilot and Innovation Pathway

The committee recommends that new or higher-risk uses of AI begin as bounded pilots rather than immediate system-wide implementation. Pilots should have clearly defined goals, include guardrails appropriate to the level of risk, be limited in scope and duration, and include evaluation criteria established before launch. Piloting should also be used as an opportunity to understand how AI can support learning, not only to identify risks.

Pilot evaluation should include both quantitative outcomes (student performance data, usage patterns, incident reports) and qualitative feedback from educators, students, and families. Tools that cannot demonstrate benefit or that produce inequitable outcomes should not advance to broad deployment.

#### Success Metrics

FCCPS should define a limited set of indicators to evaluate the effectiveness of AI policy implementation over time, consistent with outcome evaluation methods used in program evaluation research (Grinnell et al., 2019). Recommended metrics include:

Staff completion rates for AI PD by track (all staff, educators, administrators, SPED)

Reduction in potential compliance violations related to AI tool use

Number and tier classification of AI tools in the district inventory

Time from tool request to approval decision, by tier

Number and type of AI-related incidents reported, and resolution timelines

Equity indicators:

disaggregated incident and outcome data by student disability status, race, and language status (consistent with my capstone that uses the DEIPAR framework principles)

Family notification and consent completion rates for Tier 3 tools

Pilot outcomes relative to pre-established evaluation criteria

These indicators should be reviewed as part of the annual administrative review and reported to the Board during biennial policy review cycles.

#### Incident Response

FCCPS should establish a clear, documented process for identifying, reporting, and responding to incidents involving AI tools. Incidents may be caused by staff, students, vendors, or the tools themselves, and may include:

False or disputed academic integrity accusations generated by AI detection tools

Staff use of AI tools in ways that violate policy (e.g., entering student PII into unapproved tools, using AI to generate IEP content without disclosure)

Privacy or data exposure, including unauthorized sharing of student data with AI vendors

Harmful, biased, or inappropriate AI outputs affecting students

Student misuse of AI tools in ways that affect other students or compromise assessment integrity

Vendor breaches of DPA terms, including unauthorized data retention or model training

Response processes should include clear escalation pathways matched to the severity and risk tier of the incident, defined roles for who investigates and who communicates, documentation requirements for all incidents regardless of resolution, and timely notification to affected families where student data or well-being is involved. Incident data should feed into the annual administrative review cycle to identify patterns and inform policy updates. Incident response should prioritize student well-being, fairness, transparency, and due process for all parties.

## Decision Points and Tradeoffs for Board Consideration

Phase gate requirements: prescriptive vs. flexible. Should Phase 2 require confirmed operational readiness of Phase 1 infrastructure, or should building-level administrators have discretion to assess their own readiness? Prescriptive gates ensure consistency across the district; flexible gates allow faster movement for buildings that are ready

SPED-specific PD: mandated vs. discretionary. Should special education staff training on AI governance be required in policy or left to administrative discretion? IDEA’s qualified personnel requirements support the case for mandatory training, but mandating it in policy adds implementation burden.

Review cadence: annual vs. biennial Board review. Annual review maximizes responsiveness; a biennial review with strong trigger protocols may be more sustainable for a district of FCCPS’s size.
