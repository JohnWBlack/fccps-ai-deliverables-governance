WS-POL-I – Citation & Disclosure

Responsible citation practices are reinforced by many independent factors apart from norms of academic integrity. For example, responsible citation serves to honor intellectual ownership by giving credit where it is due; it helps to establish the credibility and rigor of written work by grounding it in informed research rather than uninformed opinion; it enables the independent verification of factual claims by others; and it situates the writer’s ideas within a broader conversation by positioning those ideas relative to existing debates.

Many of these factors simply do not apply to the output of generative AI technologies. Consequently, teachers and students are both likely to be in the dark as to how AI generated content ought to be cited. The first point to be clear about is that, despite their inevitable integration into information processing workflows, generative AI technologies should not be treated as reliable sources of information. AI literacy programs should be devised, in part, to discourage students from interacting with these technologies as if they were accessing reliable reference materials, and any policies adopted by the schools involving the citation of AI generated material should reinforce this cautious view. In fact, for this reason, it may well be that citation is not a particularly suitable lens through which to view policy regulating responsible AI usage. Disclosure is the more appropriate notion.

The main aim of a disclosure policy is to help maintain a culture of honesty, trust, and responsibility in the academic community by requiring students to fully disclose any external aids, resources, or borrowed material that they utilize in their schoolwork. It may be tempting for the schools to simply enact a general policy requiring full disclosure of any student use of generative AI technology for school related tasks or activities. Apart from being impractical, however, there are potential flaws with such a policy. For example, a 2025 study found that any AI disclosure—whether voluntary or mandatory—reduced trust in the person using AI, even when their AI usage was permitted. The researchers who conducted the study concluded that “the observed effect can be attributed primarily to the act of disclosure rather than to the mere fact of AI usage”. This effect creates a challenge: schools must require the disclosure of AI usage to ensure transparency and fairness, but that very requirement may end up penalizing students who abide by the rules.

For this reason, rather than trying to regulate responsible AI usage exclusively through disclosure policies, teachers and staff should also be encouraged to explore alternative methodologies that could circumvent the need for disclosure. For example, “reverse lecturing” or the flipped classroom paradigm—where students are delivered instructional content outside the classroom and produce their work in class—can reduce the need for AI disclosure because it shifts the tasks that would be most benefited by AI usage into a supervised environment where AI assistance is unavailable. Studies on flipped classrooms observe that, in addition to its general efficacy, this structure discourages academic dishonesty by allowing teachers to monitor students’ work habits closely, provide individual assistance, and clarify what counts as plagiarism and misuse in real time.

Ultimately, AI disclosure policy should be designed to empower teachers to make assignment choices on pedagogical, not policing, grounds. The primary question underlying their assignment choices should be: “What kind of task most effectively develops students’ understanding, skills, and judgment?” not “What kind of task is hardest to outsource to a generative AI system?” Framing policy around fear of academic dishonesty risks pushing instruction away from rudimentary work—such as short summarizing essays, take‑home reflections, problem sets, etc.—that, despite being easy to outsource to AI systems, are essential building blocks for higher learning.
