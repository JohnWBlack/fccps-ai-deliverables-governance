# Guiding Principles, Motivation, and Challenges

The Falls Church City Public Schools (FCCPS) School Board has committed to developing a comprehensive Artificial Intelligence (AI) policy during the 2025-26 school year. This policy will provide guidance on:

how students learn to use AI as a foundational tool for future study and work

how our schools integrate AI into the design and delivery of education to enhance

teaching and support to educators

personalized learning and the overall student experience.

how to capture the benefits that AI may create and how to mitigate the risks and community concerns posed by AI.

## Virtues we are trying to capture

Evidence-informed. The policy should clearly articulate the human skills, competencies, and other outcomes it prioritizes. Recommendations should be clearly connected to these outcomes and supported by evidence. The strength of recommendations should be in proportion to the evidence available, as appropriate.

Alignment with IB values. The policy should align with the values and learning goals for the IB program, which is designed to build the following five skills:

Thinking (critical, creative, and ethical)

Research (comparing, contrasting, validating and prioritizing information)

Communication (written and oral, effective listening, and formulating arguments)

Social (forming and maintaining positive relationships, and conflict resolution)

Self-management (organizational skills, such as managing time and tasks, and affective skills, such as managing state of mind and motivation)

Age-appropriate. The policy should account for differences in learning and risks of AI usage vary by age. The IB program in FCCPS takes different approaches to learning based on age.

Integrated with our community. The policy should ensure that students, parents, and educators receive all relevant information promptly and transparently. Further, their feedback should be gathered and prioritized for assessments of new AI-policy actions. The policy created by the school system should be revised frequently, because AI technologies and community experiences with them are evolving rapidly.

Risk mitigation. Community members and Ai experts can precisely demonstrate the risks of AI in K-12 education more easily than they can demonstrate the potential benefits. The policy should directly address potential risks of adopting AI in the FCCPS school system and ensure that sufficient mitigations are in place. Potential risks include, but are not limited to, academic integrity, data privacy, and inequitable access.

# Challenges this policy must address

The benefits of using AI in K-12 education are uncertain. If the benefits were clearly supported by school experience or research, the appropriate actions to take would be clear. In reality, some schools have reported amazing gains from the use of educational technologies and AI, while the broader literature contains many studies showing that AI technologies may undermine learning or fail to improve the lives of its users.

The use of AI introduces risks. AI exacerbates existing risks related to academic integrity by allowing the creation of long-form text. It also introduces new risks, such as easily creating media with content that is harmful to students or teachers, preventing kids from seeking human mental health support, or student inputs getting leaked by being used for training purposes. Whether adequate safeguards can be put in place remains is an open question.

The future quality of AI tools is uncertain. The AI community generally believes that their tools are improving and will continue to improve. However, “platform decay” is a documented trend for software systems to get worse over time as companies reduce service costs and quality to maximize profit. It is impossible to know whether specific tools the school adopts will improve or decay in the future.

Access to AI is ubiquitous and its use is not reliably detectable. Students and educators will have access to AI regardless of school policy. This means that those who choose to adopt the technology may be perceived as having an unfair advantage over those that do not adopt AI; this applies to both students and teachers alike. Likewise, educators may use the technology against the wishes of students and parents who do not want their work run through an AI system. It is generally not possible to tell when a student or educator has used AI, making any rule or policy potentially difficult to enforce.
