WS-POL-F+G — Student Use by Grade Band

The committee recommends that FCCPS organize student AI-use policy by grade band, with more guided and limited use in earlier grades and broader, better-governed use in later grades.  Broadly speaking, the committee recommends more limits on AI use with younger children and broader use of AI with older children.

Notably, the committee had different views about the long-term risks and benefits of AI usage by students, which is currently an active area of research.  This was especially salient for older children in the context of workforce preparation.  This led to a strong interest in creating guided opportunities to practice effective and ethical use of AI so that graduates are prepared to be strong AI users.  However, there were equally strong concerns about students becoming dependent on AI and AI eroding human-centered, “durable skills”, such as learning, resilience, creativity, and critical thinking.  A key job for FCCPS will be balancing these tensions. Differential weighting of these concerns led committee members to advocate for broader vs. restrictive policies.

Across grade bands, the committee supports clear categories of permitted, conditional, and prohibited student AI use, with concrete examples published for students and teachers. Detailed controls and examples should be developed elsewhere in the package.

The committee consistently emphasized FCCPS must continue to prioritize foundational cognitive, social, and relational learning, and uses of AI must be safe, ethical, developmentally appropriate, and consistent with learning and educational goals. The committee considered several broad areas of student use of AI: 1) AI Literacy, 2) Personalized Learning, and 3) Direct Student Use: Educator-mediated and Independent use of GenAI.

AI Literacy

There was strong agreement among the committee that AI Literacy is critical for all students (PreK-12) and FCCPS needs to integrate AI Literacy into the curriculum in developmentally-appropriate ways.  There are many AI Literacy frameworks that use the FCCPS school bands (e.g., UNESCO, AI4K12) and the committee encourages education practitioners to consider these frameworks.

The committee recommends FCCPS integrate developmentally appropriate AI Literacy across all grade bands and engage in vertical planning across grades and schools. UNESCO defines AI literacy as “the knowledge, skills, and attitudes that enable individuals to understand, engage with, and critically evaluate AI technologies in a responsible and ethical manner.” Core components include: 1) Understanding AI Fundamentals (data, algorithms, machine learning), including its applications and limitations, 2) Ethical Evaluation (bias, privacy, accountability, social justice), 3) Responsible Engagement (interacting safely and effectively, evaluating AI generated content), and 4) Human-Centered Mindset (understanding when and how to use AI to ensure that AI supports rather than replaces human decision-making).

Of note, AI Literacy was a student use case that the committee agreed was appropriate and important for students in the younger grade bands (PreK, K-2, and 3-5), whereas many other areas of student AI use had more caution for younger students. Indeed, the committee felt that the main student use of AI in younger grade bands should be related to AI Literacy and any direct usage required education-mediation.

Personalized Learning

There is a lot of excitement about the potential for AI for personalizing learning.  Personalized learning may occur in the context of specific AI-driven software (e.g., DreamBox, Lexia, Zearn) as well as broad GenAI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude). For software packages, vendor/procurement guidelines from relevant policies should be followed.  Personalized learning through approved software packages were generally viewed as acceptable to the committee.  Support for the use of broad GenAI tools for personalized support varied by grade band.

Notably, there were strong concerns in the younger grade bands about students placing too much trust in an AI partner and having trouble distinguishing humans and AI.  For this reason, the committee favored maintaining stronger boundaries around conversational, anthropomorphic, or role-simulating AI uses that may blur the distinction between human and machine interaction or encourage younger students to treat AI as a buddy, coach, co-teacher, or authority.  This concerns apply to both specific software packages and broader tools.

For students in older grade bands, there was recognition that GenAI holds promise as a tool for providing on-the-spot clarification and tutoring when students are working through homework.  The committee encourages teachers to support students in how to craft GenAI queries that are less likely to “spoil” the homework problems so that GenAI may be used in manner that promotes learning.

Direct Student Use: Educator-Mediated and Independent Use of AI

The Committee generally agreed that: 1) learning goals remain primary, 2) AI use should become broader as age and student judgment increase, and 2) that students should understand when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted. Broadly speaking, the committee recommends that any interactions with AI in younger children are educator-mediated and limited, whereas a mix of educator-mediated and independent use of AI is appropriate in older children.

Younger Children

For younger grade bands, there was general agreement among the committee that educator-mediated AI use in the context of AI Literacy was acceptable. Other uses may be acceptable when there is evidence of an educational benefit that could not otherwise be obtained, when there is close adult supervision, when learning and motivation remain, and there are minimal risks to the students in terms of safety, ethics and learning (e.g., mistaking AI tutors for humans.

In general, the committee felt that direct student interaction in younger grades with GenAI should be limited and educator-mediated. The early grades are not the place for routine independent chatbot use, free-form prompt experimentation, or unsupervised AI-supported task completion. Where students do interact directly with a system, that interaction should be brief, bounded, and supervised, with the teacher or another adult framing what the tool is doing and helping students interpret the output. It is particularly important, given that younger children may be prone to: (1) uncritical absorption of misinformation; (2) not learning how to “complete tasks independently,” and (3) exposure to “age-inappropriate material.”

Older Children

In addition to educator-mediated use of GenAI tools, the committee recognized that many students in older grade bands will encounter or use GenAI both in and out of school for learning support and schoolwork. For older grade bands, much of the committee discussion focused on independent use of GenAI and how permissible or restrictive policies should be. Regardless of whether committee members were restrictive or permissive, there was agreement that: 1) AI use in this band must support learning rather than replace it, and 2) students needed clear communication about when AI use is permitted, conditional, or restricted.  There was also a recognition that some learning scenarios may be especially well suited to assistance from GenAI and others may be impeded by assistance from GenAI.  The Committee recommends that the Board direct the schools to identify these learning scenarios in detail. Where AI use is optional, students should have a meaningful non-AI path or equivalent alternative.

Notably, there was a range of views on how permissible and restrictive student use policies should be in these grade bands.  By one view, there were deep concerns that GenAI can take over the essential thinking that writing is meant to teach.  Accordingly, it was recommended that teachers should set careful limits to ensure GenAI acts as a temporary support rather than a permanent replacement for fundamental thinking.  One specific recommendation was that educators vary their restrictions across assignments to prevent students from becoming dependent on technology for any single part of the writing process. There was a call to limit GenAI during early stages, like brainstorming and outlining, to endure students don’t skip the fundamental thinking to develop their own ideas.

In contrast, another view prioritizes structured opportunities for students to build AI fluency and judgment under teacher guidance in preparation for life after graduation. According to this view, it was recommended that FCCPS adopt a more permissive, innovation-forward, but clearly governed posture. AI should be integrated into student learning where it supports educational goals, builds literacy and judgment, and prepares students for the environments they are entering. Restrictions remain necessary in some contexts, especially where unaided performance is central. But the district should not define policy for older grade bands mainly by what students must not do. It should define it by what students should learn to do well, ethically, transparently, and with increasing independence.
