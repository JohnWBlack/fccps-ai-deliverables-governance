# Core conclusion

For Grades 6–12, the committee record shows broad agreement on progressive autonomy, not broad agreement on permissiveness. The center of gravity is that older students should receive increasingly structured opportunities to use AI as age, task complexity, and judgment increase; expectations should be clear; students should know when they are interacting with AI; and policy should distinguish among permitted, conditional, and prohibited uses. The deepest disagreements are over how much AI should be allowed in ordinary academic work, especially in writing-heavy courses and high-stakes contexts, and whether learning integrity should be protected mainly through assessment design and disclosure or through tighter default restrictions.

# How the committee got here

Meeting 3 appears to be the key framing moment. Thomas Colvin argued that the committee should stop pretending it already knew which uses were good or bad and instead organize the work around the challenges that make policy difficult: uncertain benefits, real risks, inevitable access, and difficulty determining when AI has been used. Elizabeth Chua then insisted that AI policy should be anchored in what students are supposed to learn and aligned with IB values and grade bands. John Black emphasized that the committee’s job was to reduce uncertainty enough to produce useful, flexible policy and that the committee might need to agree on problems even if it did not fully agree on solutions. Later in the same discussion, David Berol argued that AI literacy includes knowing when not to use AI, so restricting some uses and preparing students for an AI-shaped world are not contradictory goals. John Black then explicitly noted that poorly structured assessments can create competitive pressure that effectively forces students to use AI, even when teachers do not say so. At the same time, another participant argued that AI could sometimes make already-effective learning approaches more feasi

ble and scalable when coupled to sound pedagogy. Together, those exchanges largely define the Grades 6–12 fault line. (GitHub)

# Where agreement appears strongest

The strongest shared ground comes from the Shared Baseline and the outline itself. The Shared Baseline says FCCPS policy should be evidence-informed, aligned with IB values, and age-appropriate, and it explicitly distinguishes among the Primary Years, Middle Years, and Diploma/Career pathways. It also states that the benefits of AI in K–12 are highly uncertain and that AI introduces novel risks involving academic integrity, mental health, harmful media generation, and privacy. That makes developmental progression and risk differentiation foundational, not incidental, to the committee’s thinking. (GitHub)

That shared foundation is reflected in Decision 9 in the outline. For Grades 6–12, the outline says FCCPS should establish clearer consensus on use cases and posture by age, subject, and learning goal, permit progressively broader categories of use as age and judgment increase, and preserve clear expectations that students understand when they are interacting with AI rather than a human. It also ties this section directly to categories of use, interaction boundaries, disclosure expectations, and student experience.

The Meeting 4 packet reinforces the same architecture. It places the risk rubric and AI Use Guidance Library in the Green/Yellow zones, explicitly saying that grade bands and risk levels—not only “high-stakes” labels—should drive limits and disclosure. It also elevates R5 Human-Centered Learning Rule as a Red-Zone motion focused on independent reasoning, collaboration, and offline practice. That combination strongly suggests that the committee was converging on a risk-tiered, grade-banded, human-centered framework rather than a simple allow/ban model for older students.

# What the workstream products say

The Classroom Practice and Experience workstream is one of the clearest sources for Grades 6–12. D-CPX-1 says AI may be permitted and even encouraged where it supports learning and productivity, but that humans remain accountable for pedagogical judgment, evaluative accuracy, equity, and fairness, and that the examples are illustrative, not prescriptive. The 6–8 scenario—a “Study Coach for a History Exam”—is especially revealing. It identifies potential benefits such as immediate, tailored feedback and partially offsetting inequities created by private tutoring, but it also warns of overreliance, misinformation, and bias in AI-generated summaries, questions, and feedback. That is very close to the committee’s middle position for this band: use may be educationally valuable, but only within strong pedagogical guardrails. (GitHub)

The Equity and Access workstream also matters a great deal here. D-EQA-1 says FCCPS should evaluate AI tools and AI usage for equity and accessibility from procurement to classroom, monitor bias incidents, include equity topics within AI literacy, and explicitly screen for home access, premium tools, advanced digital literacy assumptions, disability/ELL issues, fairness in integrity enforcement, and staff capacity. For Grades 6–12, that supports a view that policy cannot be written only as a restriction problem, because out-of-school access, language supports, accommodations, and unequal background fluency shape the real effects of whatever rules FCCPS adopts. (GitHub)

The policy skeleton also shows how the committee intended the older-student section to operate. D-POL-1 lists Decision 9 as “Consensus on use-cases and postures” for Grades 6–12, immediately followed by categories of anticipated student use, interaction boundaries, and disclosure expectations. That confirms that the Grades 6–12 section was always meant to be a decision-rich middle band, not a generic statement of values. (GitHub)

A caution on coverage: in the public readiness snapshot, D-CCI-1 and D-IPC-1 were still listed as not started and missing principle/risk references, so the strongest accessible evidence for WS-POL-G comes from the meeting record, RSB, CPX, EQA, POL, the survey, and the written member contributions, not from communications or implementation deliverables. The same readiness snapshot shows D-AIN-1 existed and was linked to relevant principles and risks, but the accessible public snapshot does not expose substantive text for it. (GitHub)

# Range of positions in the accessible record

## 1. Restriction-first / scaffolding-only position

David Berol’s WS-POL-G contribution is the clearest articulation of the restriction-first view for older students. He explicitly says it is appropriate to expect students in Grades 6–12 to start using GenAI for assistance, but only under pragmatic and nuanced restrictions so that AI functions as scaffolding for learning goals and not as a durable substitute for human thinking. He is especially concerned about at-home writing in the humanities, argues that AI should be used only sparingly at the earliest stages of writing, and recommends more in-class writing, oral defense, disclosure requirements, and controlled settings as corrective measures. He does, however, carve out domains where AI may be more suitable, such as formative STEM/foreign-language work and computer science, where the long-term practice of the discipline is already becoming AI-integrated. That makes his position not anti-AI in general, but highly differentiated and especially restrictive in writing-heavy, cognitively generative tasks.

This position is consistent with Brookings and with the MIT Media Lab preprint that David cites. Brookings argues that, at this stage, the risks of generative AI in children’s education outweigh the benefits because the risks undermine foundational development and trusting relationships, even while it still calls for holistic AI literacy and AI tools that “teach, not tell.” The MIT paper reports reduced brain connectivity and lower essay ownership/recall in an LLM-assisted writing condition, though it is a preprint with adults rather than a K–12 sample. Together, those sources do not prove a ban case, but they strongly support the committee faction that worries most about AI in secondary writing and thinking-intensive work. (Brookings)

## 2. Guided integration / design-first position

Adriana Palacios’s student-use draft is the clearest written statement of the more balanced view. She says the committee generally agrees that student use should be developmentally appropriate and aligned with learning goals, but also notes that AI tools are already widely accessible outside school and that policies relying primarily on restriction may be difficult to enforce consistently and may encourage non-transparent use. For older students, she supports increased autonomy, guided use, clear categories, explicit boundaries, disclosure expectations, and student-facing clarity, while emphasizing that avoiding or delaying exposure entirely may limit students’ ability to develop AI literacy in meaningful ways. That is a design-first position: not “AI everywhere,” but not restriction-first by default either.

This position is strengthened by the committee’s own Meeting 4 packet. The packet’s green/yellow structure—risk rubric, grade-banded guidance library, advisory-only detection, and guardrailed drafting—fits more naturally with a model of guided, governed use than with a posture centered primarily on prohibition. The same packet’s Red-Zone R2 motion explicitly framed the central dispute as design/process evidence first versus restriction-first in high-stakes, which means design-first was not a fringe position inside the committee; it was one of the two formal options under active consideration.

External comparators point the same way. Virginia’s official AI education guidelines say AI integration should prioritize integrity, augment, not replace humans, and proceed through innovation and experimentation with access for all learners, while remaining constantly responsive to changing capabilities. Arlington Public Schools’ public guidance is similarly more permissive in tone, and its 2026 FAQ says students in grades 6–12 could begin using approved generative AI tools in guided environments once staff and student requirements are met. These are not binding analogues for FCCPS, but they do show that a guided-use posture for older students is mainstream enough to be plausible and governable. (Virginia Department of Education)

## 3. Process-centered / explicit-tradeoff position

A third strand in the committee record is less a substantive camp than a method. John Black repeatedly framed the work as colliding values, requiring discomfort and explicit tradeoffs rather than artificial harmony. Thomas Colvin repeatedly emphasized uncertainty, the need to connect recommendations to problems, and the risk of policy drifting into vague or hype-driven claims. This strand does not eliminate the substantive split between David and Adriana-style positions; it seeks to make that split legible to the Board and to drive it into operational guidance. For Grades 6–12, that process orientation is part of why the committee ended up talking so much about categories, risk levels, disclosure, process evidence, and use-case guidance, rather than trying to settle the issue with a single sentence about “responsible use.” (GitHub)

## 4. More permissive / innovation-forward position

The survey also captures a smaller but real position that is more adoption-forward than either Adriana’s balanced draft or your own design-first stance. One response objected that “balance” can be too contrastive and argued that if AI works to improve learning, FCCPS should adopt it even if that means abandoning older, more human-centered paradigms of learning. Another response emphasized that use case matters, that pilot projects should keep humans in the loop, and that teacher judgment should weigh heavily even when external evidence lags behind the technology. These are not the dominant views in the accessible record, but they are part of the range. (GitHub)

# What the survey says about the split

The survey is especially useful for the Grades 6–12 band because many of its tested clauses map directly onto older-student policy: common use-case language, design before bans, student disclosure, non-AI path, access plus supports, prevent cognitive offloading, risk-based restrictions, AI literacy, human-centered learning, and visible thinking. That tells us the committee was already thinking about older-student policy not just as “whether AI is allowed,” but as a cluster of design choices around academic integrity, equity, clarity, and developmental appropriateness. (GitHub)

One survey response strongly represents the restriction-first side: it rejected reliance on process evidence because it is “easily fakable,” argued that the restriction-versus-design dichotomy is false because design constraints are themselves restrictions, favored a guaranteed non-AI path, and said students are better off using zero GenAI because it is easy to use and easy to overuse. That response is a strong match for David’s overall logic, especially in writing-intensive secondary work. (GitHub)

Another response is the cleanest statement of the balanced design-first side. It explicitly chose Option C: default to design/process evidence, apply restrictions only where validity cannot otherwise be preserved, keep detector outputs advisory only, use grade-banded disclosure with the strongest expectations for high-stakes work, and pair equitable access with supports. That respondent also said clear grade-banded guidance would be essential for consistency and sustainability. This is probably the best single survey articulation of the middle/design-first coalition for Grades 6–12. (GitHub)

A further response pushed toward stronger innovation and teacher autonomy, arguing that FCCPS should not lean too heavily on external evidence because the technology changes too quickly, that teacher judgment about what helps students should count heavily, and that AI should be adopted if it improves learning—even if it displaces older “human-centered” paradigms. That is the accessible record’s clearest evidence of a more adoption-forward position. (GitHub)

# What this means for your drafting

For WS-POL-G / Grades 6–12, the record supports a clear description of consensus and disagreement.

The consensus is that older students should encounter more AI than younger students, but under clear, grade-banded, task-specific expectations. The committee agrees that learning goals remain primary, that AI should support rather than replace student thinking, that use categories and disclosure rules matter, and that students should know when AI is permitted, conditional, or restricted. It also agrees that integrity and fairness cannot be protected by detector-driven policing alone and that expectations must be clearer in this band because older students will encounter AI both inside and outside school.

The disagreement is about default posture and about domain-specific limits. One camp wants AI in Grades 6–12 to be treated mainly as temporary scaffolding, with especially strong restrictions in secondary writing, stronger proctoring or in-class components, and a non-AI path protected against competitive pressure. Another camp wants guided, explicit, risk-tiered integration, especially because home access is inevitable and uneven, and because some uses—tutoring, formative practice, structured feedback, selected STEM/language support, and computer science—may have real educational value when designed well. A smaller but real position is more willing to trust teacher judgment and innovation even when the evidence base is incomplete. (GitHub)

That means you can fairly draft a section that adopts a design-first posture while still taking the restriction-first critique seriously. The accessible record gives you good grounds to say: secondary students should receive progressively broader, guided exposure; AI literacy includes when not to use AI; writing-intensive and high-stakes tasks deserve stronger protection; disclosure should be grade-banded and strongest where stakes are highest; and categories of use should be explicit enough to avoid both competitive coercion and classroom confusion. What you will need to choose is how strongly you want to protect a non-AI path, how sharply you want to restrict AI in early-stage writing and argument formation, and how much teacher discretion you want to preserve across subjects.
