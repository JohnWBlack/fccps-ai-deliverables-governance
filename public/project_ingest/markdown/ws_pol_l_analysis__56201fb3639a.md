# WS POL L Analysis

WS-POL-L – Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback

Here is a research guide for WS-POL-L — Public Accountability, Notice, Consent, and Feedback based on the public GitHub repo, the uploaded WS-DPS draft, and the uploaded V3 outline. The strongest overall conclusion is that the committee shows broad agreement on transparency and accountability in principle, but meaningful disagreement on how far to go operationally with notice, consent, opt-out, public-facing inventories, and feedback mechanisms. The public record supports a center of gravity around plain-language notice, accountable ownership, accessible communication, and some form of public feedback loop, but it does not support full consensus on stronger procedural rights such as broad parental opt-in or universal opt-out.

# How the committee got here

This section appears to emerge from two overlapping streams in the committee’s work.

The first is the community-input and legitimacy stream. In Meeting 3, Thomas Colvin said Virginia’s state-level guidance stressed the importance of getting rapid, frequent, easy feedback from students, parents, educators, school board members, and concerned community members, and he presented that as a notable feature of the external policy environment the committee was studying. In the same meeting, John Black discussed the need to define principles and tradeoffs in a way that could support public-facing discussion, and later the committee discussed town halls, public input collection, and guidance for committee participation in community events. Bethany Henderson also emphasized the School Board’s oversight role and the importance of building in periodic review so the policy does not run away from its original intent. (Meeting 3 transcript)

The second is the governance-and-implementation stream. In the V3 outline, WS-POL-L is explicitly framed around Decision 11, asking how to scope commitments so that they are implementable. The section then lists public notice, consideration of public comment, parental opt-out or consent where applicable, plain-language communication, multilingual accessibility, family understanding of opportunities and risks, accountable owners, public-facing explanations of AI uses and safeguards, feedback channels, and review processes when harms or complaints emerge. That structure shows the committee was not treating public accountability as a generic “good thing.” It was trying to decide which commitments belonged in policy and which might become unworkable if drafted too broadly.

# Where agreement appears strongest

The strongest agreement appears in four areas.

First, the committee broadly agrees that clear communication matters. The V3 outline says successful implementation will depend on clear, accessible communication with students and families, plain-language guidance understandable to non-technical audiences, and accessibility across languages and community backgrounds. It also warns that without clear communication, expectations may be unevenly understood or applied. That is about as close to consensus language as this section gets.

Second, the committee appears broadly aligned that some form of accountable ownership is necessary. The WS-DPS draft repeatedly assigns owners to privacy and governance risks, and its tradeoff table explicitly asks who should own compliance monitoring: IT, Curriculum & Instruction, or a shared model with a defined RACI and cadence. Even where members differ on org structure, the accessible record supports the idea that AI governance cannot be ownerless.

Third, there is broad support for some kind of feedback channel. In the survey, the “feedback channels” principle was often approved or only lightly revised. Even where respondents wanted changes, the changes usually focused on ownership, structure, or reporter safety rather than rejecting the idea of feedback channels altogether. One respondent wanted the district to publicly identify a staff person responsible for AI security complaints; another stressed that feedback systems must be structured so information is synthesized effectively and does not create diffuse accountability or unsustainable workload. (Survey export)

Fourth, there is substantial support for plain-language public-facing explanation at an appropriate level, even when members disagree on how exhaustive it should be. The V3 outline’s language about public-facing explanation, family engagement, and helping families understand both opportunities and risks reflects that center of gravity.

# Main fault lines and the range of opinions

## 1. Broad public transparency vs. tightly scoped, implementable notice

This is the largest divide in the accessible record.

One position favors broad transparency commitments, including advance notice, public-facing explanation, and possibly fairly robust visibility into how AI is used in student-affecting contexts. David Berol’s uploaded WS-POL-L draft is the clearest expression of this posture. He argues that for any summative assessment or decision affecting student educational rights, students and parents should have the right to know whether the work was produced without AI, with non-substantive AI assistance, or with substantive AI contributions. He further argues that where substantive AI use contributed to the document, parents and students should have the right to inspect the record of that substantive AI use. He also supports opt-in parental consent for substantively AI-assisted planning or drafting of critical decision documents affecting children. David Berol WS-POL-L draft

A narrower, more implementation-sensitive position worries that expansive notice commitments become impracticable quickly. The strongest evidence comes from survey responses objecting that a public registry of every AI tool on campus would be unmanageable, asking whether the district really needs to catalog every classroom where Grammarly or AI-adjusted reading levels are in use, and insisting that “the core issue is GEN AI.” The same respondent warned that broad opt-in/opt-out data collection and “where feasible” language would create controversy and implementation burden without a much tighter focus. (Survey export)

So the disagreement is not over whether parents and families deserve transparency. It is over whether policy should promise broad rights to know and inspect or more bounded, practical notice structures.

## 2. Notice plus consent/opt-out vs. notice plus governance safeguards

This is the sharpest formal policy split, and it is visible both in the outline and in Meeting 4 materials.

The V3 outline includes “parental opt-out or consent where applicable,” which is a deliberately qualified formulation rather than an absolute right. The Meeting 4 packet then elevates R4 — Notice / Consent / Opt-Out into the Red Zone, explicitly saying the committee still had to “lock consent posture for AI uses involving student data,” with key considerations including advance notice, opt-out feasibility, and privacy/vendor terms. Meeting 4 packet

The survey shows at least three distinct positions:

a strong procedural-rights position that wants notice and opt-out broadly available and in some cases rejects “where feasible” as too weak,

a balanced middle that supports advance notice for all and consent/opt-out where feasible, with approved tools and data minimization as the primary safeguards,

and an implementation-skeptical position that thinks broad consent/opt-out commitments may create excessive classroom and administrative burden, including quasi-parallel instructional pathways. (Survey export)

## 3. Public accountability as governance architecture vs. public accountability as family-facing rights

Another real divide is what “public accountability” is primarily for.

The governance-heavy view, visible in WS-DPS, treats accountability mainly as a system of owners, inventories, vendor obligations, review cycles, incident response, and notification of material changes in vendor data practices. That is a back-end governance model.

The rights-and-trust view, visible in David’s draft and some survey responses, treats accountability more as something families and students should be able to see, question, and in some cases refuse. That is a front-end legitimacy model.

The V3 outline is trying to bridge those two models by including both accountable owners and public-facing explanation, both public comment and process for harms, both notice and community accessibility.

## 4. Broad accountability framework vs. narrower GenAI-focused framing

As with WS-POL-K, some respondents clearly want this section narrowed to the specific AI uses that are actually driving controversy. The survey contains repeated complaints that questions were too broad, that “AI” was being defined too expansively, and that the district should “walk this back to just talking about GenAI.” That suggests some members may resist a WS-POL-L section that sounds like a universal public-reporting and consent framework for all AI-enabled tools and workflows. (Survey export)

# What the workstream products imply

The public repo does not expose a substantive D-CCI-1 communications pack, and the Meeting 4 readiness snapshot shows D-CCI-1 as not started in the public view. That matters. It means the public record for WS-POL-L is being carried mainly by:

the policy shell,

the survey,

Meeting 3’s town hall / public input discussion,

and the governance language in WS-DPS,
rather than by a mature communications workstream. (Meeting 4 readiness)

That is important for drafting because it means the committee likely agrees more on the need for accountability and communication than on the final form of notices, FAQs, consent language, or feedback operations.

# Best characterization of the range of opinions

The accessible record suggests four main positions.

A maximal transparency / parental-rights position wants strong notice, visible labeling of AI involvement in consequential documents, access to records of substantive AI use, and often opt-in consent for the most sensitive uses. David Berol’s WS-POL-L draft is the clearest version of this view. David Berol WS-POL-L draft

A balanced transparency-and-governance position wants advance notice, feedback channels, clear communication, approved tools, data minimization, and accountability owners, but only consent/opt-out where feasible and without overpromising exhaustive public reporting. This appears to be the closest match to the V3 outline and to several survey respondents.

An implementation-skeptical / narrow-scope position worries that broad registries, consent architectures, and tool-by-tool public notice become impossible to administer and distract from the real public concern, which is GenAI and high-visibility student-data risk. That position is especially clear in anonymous survey feedback. (Survey export)

A governance-first position emphasizes accountable owners, incident response, review cadence, and vendor notification rather than family-facing procedural rights. This is the strongest through-line in WS-DPS.

# What this means for your drafting

For WS-POL-L, the strongest committee-grounded common ground is:

FCCPS should communicate AI policy expectations clearly, accessibly, and in plain language.

Families and the public should have some meaningful visibility into AI uses and safeguards.

FCCPS should designate accountable owners for governance and incident response.

FCCPS should provide feedback channels and some process for review when harms, complaints, or new risks emerge.

Public-accountability commitments should be implementable, not merely aspirational.

Where you will need to choose your own position is on three narrower questions:

whether public notice should be broad and rights-forward or more targeted and operationally constrained,

how strong consent/opt-out rights should be,

and whether public accountability should be framed primarily as family-facing transparency or as internal governance with public explanation.
